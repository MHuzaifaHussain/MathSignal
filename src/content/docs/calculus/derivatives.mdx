## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Some slides have a sharp bend where a flat top meets the slope, and pencils laid near a bend behave differently from pencils on a smooth curve; the second board below looks at one. And a steepness is not only something you draw on paper. It is any rate, such as meters per second, or how fast a model's error changes as one of its numbers moves.">

Draw a slide on squared paper. It starts flat on the ground and climbs more and more steeply: 1 square across it has risen 1 square, and 2 squares across it has risen 4. You sit on it 1 square across. How steep is the slide exactly where you sit?

Lay a pencil from your seat to the point of the slide 1 square further on. It climbs 3 squares, so it says "3 up for every 1 across". Now lay a pencil from your seat back to the point 1 square behind you, at the bottom. It drops 1 square in 1 square, so it says "1 up for every 1 across". The two pencils disagree, and neither of them sits only where you sit: each one also measures a stretch of slide you are not on. What would shorter pencils say? The board below lets you find out.

A second picture covers what the slide leaves out: steepness is not only about shapes. On a bike ride, total distance divided by total time is an average, a long pencil. The speedometer shows how fast you are going right now: what a very short stretch of the ride, divided by its very short time, closes in on.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Training a model means changing its weights so that its loss goes down, and the derivative is what says which way to change each one. If the loss rises as a weight grows, the derivative is positive and the weight should shrink; if the loss falls, the derivative is negative and the weight should grow. Gradient descent does exactly this, step after step, with one derivative per weight. Derivatives also explain why a learning rate can be too large, and what happens at ReLU's corner, where there is no derivative and every library has to pick a value.
</WhyThisMatters>

## From average slope to slope at a point

The slope at a point is the number that the slopes of shorter and shorter rulers through that point close in on, and that number is the derivative.

### Rise over run between two points

A straight line has one slope everywhere: rise ÷ run. A curve has no single slope, so a ruler through two of its points gives an average. From x = 1 to x = 1.5 on y = x², the rise is 2.25 − 1 = 1.25 over a run of 0.5, a slope of 1.25 ÷ 0.5 = 2.5. The straight line through two points of a graph is called a secant, and Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong call its slope the difference quotient (§5.1, Definition 5.1, p. 141) <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.

<Simpler label="the same idea as a table of rulers">

Here are the six rulers you could use before Check, all starting at x = 1 on y = x². Read down each half: the slopes squeeze toward 2 from above and from below.

| Ruler length h | Other end | Rise | Run | Slope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2 | 3 | 1 | 3 |
| 0.5 | 1.5 | 1.25 | 0.5 | 2.5 |
| 0.25 | 1.25 | 0.5625 | 0.25 | 2.25 |
| −0.25 | 0.75 | −0.4375 | −0.25 | 1.75 |
| −0.5 | 0.5 | −0.75 | −0.5 | 1.5 |
| −1 | 0 | −1 | −1 | 1 |

A ruler behind the point has a negative run and, here, a negative rise, so its slope is still positive: the curve goes up as x grows on that side too.

</Simpler>

### Let the run shrink to zero

The run can't be 0, because a rise of 0 over a run of 0 has no value, which is why the board leaves h = 0 out. But the run can be as small as you like, on either side. The slopes of these short rulers close in on one number, 2 for y = x² at x = 1, and that number is the derivative at x = 1. The line through the point with that slope is the tangent. Deisenroth and coauthors describe the same picture: in the limit, the secant becomes the tangent (§5.1, Definition 5.2, p. 141) <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.

<GoDeeper label="why the ruler misses by exactly h" id="ruler-misses-by-h">

For $y = x^2$, the rise from $x$ to $x + h$ is $(x + h)^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2$. Divide by the run $h$, which is not 0, and the ruler's slope is $2x + h$. At $x = 1$ that is $2 + h$ for every $h \ne 0$, so the miss is exactly $h$, and it shrinks to 0 with $h$. The slope at the point is $2x$.

On $y = x^3$ the rise is $3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3$, so at $x = 1$ the ruler's slope is $3 + 3h + h^2$: 3.31 at $h = 0.1$ and 3.0301 at $h = 0.01$. It misses 3 by more than $h$, but the miss still shrinks to 0, so the slope at the point is 3.

</GoDeeper>

### A slope is a rate, with units

A derivative is measured in output units per input unit. A cyclist pulling away from a standstill is 5t² meters along after t seconds. From t = 1 to t = 2 the bike covers 20 − 5 = 15 meters, an average of 15 meters per second. From t = 1 to t = 1.1 it covers 1.05 meters in 0.1 seconds, 10.5 meters per second, and from t = 1 to t = 1.01 the average is 10.05 meters per second. These averages close in on the speedometer's reading at t = 1: 10 meters per second. For a model, the derivative of the loss with respect to a weight is the change in loss per unit of weight.

<SideNote>
The number depends on the units of the input: 10 meters per second is 0.01 meters per millisecond, the same speed.
</SideNote>

### The slope at every point is a new function

Find the slope at every x and you get a new function, the derivative function. For y = x² it is 2x: at x = −2, −1, 0, 1 and 2 the slopes are −4, −2, 0, 2 and 4. A negative slope means the curve goes down as x grows, a positive one that it goes up, and 0 that it is flat for an instant. After Check, Show as Slope curve draws the derivative function in a strip below the curve; with the Point x slider at −1.5 the board says "Slope curve: at x = −1.5 the slope of y = x² is −3." Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville put it the same way: the derivative gives the slope of f at x, and says how to scale a small change in the input to get the change in the output (§4.3) <Cite id="goodfellow2016numerical" />.

<GoDeeper label="flat does not mean lowest" id="flat-is-not-lowest">

On $y = x^3$ at $x = 0$, every ruler slope is $h^2$: positive on both sides, closing in on 0. On the board, pick the S-curve, move the point to $x = 0$ and use rulers of length 0.5 and −0.5: both give 0.25, and Tangent says "Tangent at x = 0: slope 0." The curve is flat there for an instant, yet it keeps climbing through the point: $x^3$ is negative on the left and positive on the right. Goodfellow and coauthors call points with zero slope critical or stationary points, and show the three kinds in one dimension, a minimum, a maximum and a saddle point, in Figure 4.2 (§4.3) <Cite id="goodfellow2016numerical" />.

</GoDeeper>

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="A ruler on y = x² at x = 3 has length 0.5. What is its slope, and what is the slope at the point?">
6.5 and 6. A ruler of length $h$ at $x$ has slope $2x + h$, so this one has slope $6 + 0.5$, and the slope at the point is $2 \times 3 = 6$. Check: the rise is $3.5^2 - 3^2 = 3.25$, and $3.25 \div 0.5 = 6.5$.
</CheckYourself>

### The tangent line as a short-range forecast

Near the point, the tangent line is a good forecast of the curve. On y = x² at x = 1, it says the curve 0.1 further on is about 1 + 0.1 × 2 = 1.2; the true value is 1.21. The miss, 0.01 = 0.1², shrinks much faster than the step, but a step of 1 misses by 1. Goodfellow and coauthors write this forecast as f(x + ε) ≈ f(x) + ε f′(x) (§4.3) <Cite id="goodfellow2016numerical" />.

## Corners where the slope splits

Before you look: ReLU is the curve that is 0 for negative inputs and equals x for positive ones. At x = 0, does a ruler to the right give the same slope as a ruler to the left? Going further: if they differ, which slopes could you fairly call "the slope at the corner"?

Among the Curves presets, V is |x|: rulers to the right give 1 and to the left −1, so again there is no tangent. On Smooth, x² at 0, the rulers close in on 0 from both sides, and the board says "Tangent at x = 0: slope 0."

### Smooth enough to differentiate

A function has a derivative at x when the ruler slopes on the right and on the left close in on the same number. A corner fails that test, and so does a jump. For the step that is 0 for negative inputs and 1 from 0 on, rulers to the right of 0 give 0, while a ruler to the left of length h has slope −1 ÷ h: 4 at h = −0.25 and 1,000 at h = −0.001, growing without limit. Having no gap is not enough, though: |x| has no gap at 0 and still no derivative there. Deisenroth and coauthors assume differentiable functions throughout their chapter on calculus, and note that many methods extend to sub-differentials for functions that are continuous but not differentiable at some points (chapter 5 introduction, p. 141) <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.

<SideNote>
The slope that the right-hand rulers close in on is called the right derivative, and the left-hand one the left derivative. ReLU at 0 has a right derivative of 1 and a left derivative of 0.
</SideNote>

<GoDeeper label="every slope in between: subgradients" id="subgradients">

On the corner board after Check, turn on Lines under the corner. It draws a line through the corner with the slope set by the Line slope s slider. On Hinge, "A line of slope 0.5 through the corner stays on or below the curve everywhere." At slope 1.5 the board says "Slope 1.5: the line rises above the curve to the right of 0." and at −0.5 "Slope −0.5: the line rises above the curve to the left of 0." A line of slope $s$ through $(0, 0)$ stays on or below ReLU exactly when $0 \le s \le 1$.

Every such $s$ is called a subgradient of ReLU at 0, and the set of them, the interval $[0, 1]$, is its subdifferential. For $|x|$ it is $[-1, 1]$: on V the board says the line stays on or below the curve for every slope it offers from −1 to 1. Kakade and Lee describe the more general (Clarke) subdifferential as the convex hull of the limits of gradients at nearby points where the function is differentiable, a set that generalizes both the gradient of a smooth function and the subgradient of a convex one (§1, p. 2) <Cite id="kakade2018" />. For ReLU at 0 those nearby slopes are 0 and 1, and their convex hull is again $[0, 1]$.

PyTorch's notes say that for a convex function at a point with no gradient, it uses the subgradient of minimum norm <Cite id="pytorch-autograd-notes" />. The element of $[0, 1]$ closest to 0 is 0, and the same holds for $[-1, 1]$, so that rule gives 0 for both ReLU and $|x|$ at 0. This page deduces that from the stated rule; the code below checks the intervals.

</GoDeeper>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["function", "run", "difference-quotient", "limit", "derivative", "leibniz", "absolute-value", "relu", "weight", "loss", "learning-rate", "data-value", "sample-size", "sum"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $f$ be a real function defined on an open interval around $x$. Then $f$ is differentiable at $x$ if the limit

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

exists and is finite, the limit taken over $h \ne 0$ from both sides. The number $f'(x)$, also written $\frac{df}{dx}$, is the derivative of $f$ at $x$, and the line $y = f(x) + f'(x)(t - x)$ is the tangent at $x$. The function $f$ is differentiable on an interval when this holds at every point of it, and $x \mapsto f'(x)$ is then the derivative function.

Conventions: $h$ may be negative; $h = 0$ is never used. Boundary cases: if the one-sided limits ($h > 0$ only, $h < 0$ only) both exist but differ, as for $\operatorname{ReLU}$ and $|x|$ at 0, there is no derivative; if $f$ jumps at $x$ there is none; a function differentiable at $x$ is continuous at $x$, but not conversely; and $f'(x) = 0$ marks a critical point, which may be a minimum, a maximum or neither ($x^3$ at 0). Scope: one input and one output. Functions of several inputs need partial derivatives, which get their own page.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="The derivative of xⁿ is n xⁿ⁻¹" rigor="full">

1. A constant $c$ has rise $c - c = 0$ over every run, so its derivative is 0. The function $x$ has rise $h$ over run $h$, so its derivative is 1.
2. For $n = 2$: $(x + h)^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2$. Divide by $h \ne 0$: $2x + h$, which closes in on $2x$ as $h \to 0$.
3. For $n = 3$: $(x + h)^3 - x^3 = 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3$. Divide by $h$: $3x^2 + 3xh + h^2$, which closes in on $3x^2$.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="Any whole power n" rigor="sketch">

1. Expand $(x + h)^n$ with the binomial theorem: $x^n + n x^{n-1} h + (\text{terms with } h^2 \text{ or a higher power})$.
2. Subtract $x^n$ and divide by $h$: $n x^{n-1} + (\text{terms with } h \text{ or a higher power})$.
3. Every term but the first goes to 0 with $h$, so the derivative is $n x^{n-1}$. Deisenroth and coauthors work this out as their Example 5.2 (p. 142) <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="Sums and constant multiples" rigor="full">

1. The difference quotient of $f + g$ is $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} + \frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h}$, the sum of the two quotients. If both limits exist, the limit of the sum is the sum of the limits: $(f + g)' = f' + g'$.
2. The difference quotient of $c f$ is $c$ times that of $f$, so $(c f)' = c f'$.
3. So $3x^2 - 4x + 1$ has derivative $3 \times 2x - 4 \times 1 + 0 = 6x - 4$. Deisenroth and coauthors list the sum rule as equation (5.31) (§5.1.2, p. 145) <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="A derivative at x means no jump at x" rigor="full">

1. For $h \ne 0$, write $f(x + h) - f(x) = h \times \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$.
2. As $h \to 0$ the first factor goes to 0 and the second to $f'(x)$, a finite number.
3. So $f(x + h) - f(x) \to 0 \times f'(x) = 0$: $f(x + h)$ closes in on $f(x)$, and $f$ has no jump at $x$.
4. The converse fails: $|x|$ has no jump at 0 and no derivative there.

</StepThrough>

| --- | --- | --- |
| Constant | $c' = 0$ | The ground under the slide |
| Power | $(x^n)' = n x^{n-1}$ | $x^2 \to 2x$, $x^3 \to 3x^2$ |
| Constant multiple | $(c f)' = c f'$ | $(5t^2)' = 10t$ |
| Sum | $(f + g)' = f' + g'$ | $(3x^2 - 4x + 1)' = 6x - 4$ |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="A bike pulling away">

A bike is $s(t) = 5t^2$ meters along after $t$ seconds. Its average speed from 1 to 2 seconds is $(20 - 5)/1 = 15$ m/s, from 1 to 1.1 seconds 10.5 m/s, and from 1 to 1.01 seconds 10.05 m/s. The power rule and the constant-multiple rule give $s'(t) = 5 \times 2t = 10t$, so the bike moves at 10 m/s at $t = 1$ and 20 m/s at $t = 2$. The units are meters per second, because the output is in meters and the input in seconds.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="A polynomial and its tangent line">

Let $g(x) = 3x^2 - 4x + 1$. Then $g(2) = 12 - 8 + 1 = 5$ and $g'(x) = 6x - 4$, so $g'(2) = 8$. The tangent at $x = 2$ is $y = 5 + 8(x - 2) = 8x - 11$. Rulers from $x = 2$ of length 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 have slopes 8.3, 8.03 and 8.003, which is $8 + 3h$, closing in on 8. The slope is 0 where $6x - 4 = 0$, at $x = 2/3$, and that is where $g$ is lowest: $g(2/3) = -1/3$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Which single height fits the class best?" faded>

Find the guess where the slope is 0, and check with the signs of $L'(133)$ and $L'(140)$ that it is the lowest point.

<div slot="solution">

That page [proves the same fact without calculus](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#formal-definition), and the [mean, median and mode page](/statistics/mean-vs-median-vs-mode/) shows what changes when the misses are not squared.

</div>

## Derivatives in machine learning

### Which way to move a weight

Gradient descent moves a weight a small step against the sign of the derivative of the loss: new $w = w - \eta\,L'(w)$, where $\eta$ is the learning rate. Goodfellow and coauthors explain why: a small enough step against the sign of the derivative lowers the function, and repeating such steps is gradient descent, which they credit to Cauchy in 1847 (§4.3, Figure 4.1) <Cite id="goodfellow2016numerical" />.

Take the board's Loss preset, $L(w) = (w - 3)^2$, with $L'(w) = 2(w - 3)$. On the first board after Check, press Loss, set Show as to Tangent and set Ruler length h to 0.5. The board says "Loss at w = 0: 9." and "Slope −6: a step to the right lowers the loss. This ruler's slope, −5.5, is 0.5 above it." With learning rate 0.1, the first step takes $w$ from 0 to $0 - 0.1 \times (-6) = 0.6$. The slope there is $-4.8$, so the next step lands on 1.08, then 1.464. The loss falls from 9 to 5.76, 3.6864 and 2.359296. With many weights, the steps together are vector arithmetic, as on the [vectors page](/linear-algebra/vectors/#a-gradient-step-is-a-scaled-sum), and equation (4.5) in Goodfellow and coauthors is that version <Cite id="goodfellow2016numerical" />.

<GoDeeper label="why each step closes 1/5 of the gap" id="step-shrinks">

One step sends $w - 3$ to $(w - 3) - 0.1 \times 2(w - 3) = 0.8\,(w - 3)$. So each step keeps four fifths of the gap to 3 and closes one fifth of it. From $w = 0$, after $k$ steps $w = 3 - 3 \times 0.8^k$: 0.6, 1.08, 1.464 and 1.7712. In general one step multiplies the gap by $1 - 2\eta$ on this loss.

</GoDeeper>

### How big a step the slope allows

The derivative describes the curve only near the current weight, so a large step can overshoot the bottom. With the same loss and start, a learning rate of 0.5 lands on 3 in one step and stays there. A learning rate of 1 jumps from 0 to 6, then back to 0, forever. A learning rate of 1.1 goes to 6.6, then −1.32, then 8.184: the distance from 3 grows by a factor of 1.2 every step. A later page shows how the curvature of the loss sets a safe learning rate.

### Checking a gradient with finite differences

When you write a gradient by hand, compare it with a ruler slope. Goodfellow and coauthors recommend a one-sided ruler, or better a centered one that reaches equally far on both sides of the point, with a step large enough not to be lost to rounding (§11.5, p. 432) <Cite id="goodfellow2016practical" />. On $x^3$ at 1 with $h = 0.01$, the one-sided ruler says 3.0301, while the centered ruler from 0.99 to 1.01 says 3.0001. Baydin and coauthors explain why: the centered ruler's error is much smaller, and once $h$ gets too small, rounding error takes over (§2.1, Figure 3) <Cite id="baydin2018" />. In 64-bit floats, $h = 10^{-12}$ gives a worse estimate of the slope of $x^3$ at 1 than $h = 10^{-6}$.

<GoDeeper label="why the centered ruler is more accurate" id="centered-difference">

For $x^3$ at 1, the ruler of length $h$ has slope $3 + 3h + h^2$ and the ruler of length $-h$ has slope $3 - 3h + h^2$. The centered ruler from $1 - h$ to $1 + h$ has the average of the two slopes, $3 + h^2$: the $3h$ terms cancel. So halving $h$ cuts its miss by a factor of 4 instead of 2.

</GoDeeper>

### What libraries do at ReLU's corner

ReLU has no derivative at 0, but automatic differentiation must still return a number there. PyTorch's notes list rules it applies in order: use the gradient where it exists, and for a convex function where it doesn't, use a subgradient of minimum norm <Cite id="pytorch-autograd-notes" />. For ReLU at 0 that rule gives 0. Bertoin and coauthors report that TensorFlow, PyTorch and JAX all use 0 there (§1, p. 1) <Cite id="bertoin2021" />. The choice is usually harmless, but not always, as the research frontier below shows.

# Kakade and Lee's identity three ways, with a tiny forward-mode differentiator.
class Dual:
    """A value and its slope, carried through every operation (forward mode)."""

    def __init__(self, value, slope):
        self.value, self.slope = value, slope

    def __add__(self, other):
        return Dual(self.value + other.value, self.slope + other.slope)

    def __sub__(self, other):
        return Dual(self.value - other.value, self.slope - other.slope)

    def __neg__(self):
        return Dual(-self.value, -self.slope)

    def scaled(self, c):
        return Dual(c * self.value, c * self.slope)


def make_relu(slope_at_zero):
    def relu_dual(u):
        if u.value > 0:
            return Dual(u.value, u.slope)
        if u.value < 0:
            return Dual(0 * u.value, 0 * u.slope)
        return Dual(0 * u.value, slope_at_zero * u.slope)  # the library's choice at the corner
    return relu_dual


def slopes_at_zero(s):
    r = make_relu(s)
    x = Dual(0 * s, 1 + 0 * s)
    f1 = x
    f2 = r(x) - r(-x)
    f3 = f1.scaled(10) - f2.scaled(9)
    f4 = r(r(x)) - r(-x)
    return f1.slope, f2.slope, f3.slope, f4.slope

## Research frontier

<ResearchFrontier reviewed="2026-11-25">

**Corners in automatic differentiation.** Kakade and Lee write the identity function three ways: $f_1(x) = x$, $f_2(x) = \operatorname{ReLU}(x) - \operatorname{ReLU}(-x)$ and $f_3(x) = 10 f_1(x) - 9 f_2(x)$. All three equal $x$, so all three have derivative 1 at 0. Yet they report that TensorFlow and PyTorch returned 1, 0 and 10, because both libraries used the subgradient 0 for ReLU at 0 (§1, p. 2; library behavior in 2018) <Cite id="kakade2018" />. Choosing $\operatorname{ReLU}'(0) = 1/2$ repairs $f_2$ and $f_3$, but $f_4(x) = \operatorname{ReLU}(\operatorname{ReLU}(x)) - \operatorname{ReLU}(-x)$, which is also the identity, would need $(\sqrt{5} - 1)/2 \approx 0.618$, so no single value works for every program (footnote 1) <Cite id="kakade2018" />. The code above reproduces 0, 10 and that value with a ten-line forward-mode differentiator. Their main result is positive: for a restricted class of programs, correct generalized derivatives can be computed at a cost within a factor of 6 of evaluating the function, whatever the dimension (abstract) <Cite id="kakade2018" />.

**Does the choice matter in training?** Bolte and Pauwels give a mathematical model of automatic differentiation on programs with corners. In it, automatic differentiation can create artificial critical points, but the usual training methods avoid them with probability one (abstract) <Cite id="bolte2020" />. Bertoin and coauthors found that floating point breaks that guarantee in practice. Two PyTorch training runs with the same initialization and the same mini-batches, one with $\operatorname{ReLU}'(0) = 0$ and one with 1, drift apart (Figure 1). The set of weights where the two choices give different results, which theory says is negligible, occupied 20% to 80% of weight space at 32 bits in their experiments, became invisible at 64 bits and filled the whole space at 16 bits. For plain stochastic gradient descent, 0 was the most efficient choice, and batch normalization and Adam damped the effect (v1, abstract and §1) <Cite id="bertoin2021" />. This is a floating-point effect: in exact arithmetic the theory stands.

**What is open.** Kakade and Lee call "how to obtain numerically stable and accurate solutions" the most important open question (§4, p. 15) <Cite id="kakade2018" />. For other activations with corners, Bertoin and coauthors write that "it is not clear a priori which choice would be the most robust" (§5, p. 10) <Cite id="bertoin2021" />.

</ResearchFrontier>

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="On y = x³ at x = 0 every ruler, left or right, has a positive slope. Is x = 0 the lowest point nearby?">
No. The ruler slopes are $h^2$, which close in on 0, so $x = 0$ is flat for an instant. But $x^3$ is negative to the left of 0 and positive to the right, so points on the left are lower. A flat point can be a minimum, a maximum or neither.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Find f′(x) for f(x) = 4x³ − x, and the slope at x = −1.">
$f'(x) = 12x^2 - 1$, by the power, constant-multiple and sum rules. At $x = -1$ the slope is $12 - 1 = 11$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="L(w) = (w − 3)², w = 5, learning rate 0.25. Where does one step of gradient descent take w?">
To 4. The slope is $L'(5) = 2(5 - 3) = 4$, positive, so the step goes left: $5 - 0.25 \times 4 = 4$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Same loss, start at w = 0, learning rate 1. What happens?">
$w$ jumps from 0 to 6, then back to 0, then to 6, forever. The slope at 0 is $-6$, so the step is $+6$, which overshoots the bottom at 3 by exactly as much as $w$ was short of it. At 6 the slope is $+6$, and the step brings it straight back.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that |x| has no derivative at 0.">
For $h > 0$ the ruler's slope is $(|h| - 0)/h = h/h = 1$, and for $h < 0$ it is $|h|/h = -h/h = -1$. So the rulers on the right close in on 1 and those on the left on $-1$. The two one-sided limits differ, so the limit of the difference quotient does not exist.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="The slope between two points is the derivative.">
    That is an average, the slope of a secant. The derivative is what those slopes close in on as the run shrinks. On y = x² at 1, a ruler of length 0.5 says 2.5, while the derivative is 2.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A derivative of 0 means a minimum.">
    It means the curve is flat for an instant. $x^3$ at 0 is flat and keeps rising through it; see [flat does not mean lowest](#flat-is-not-lowest).
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="In code, use the tiniest step h you can.">
    Rounding takes over. In 64-bit floats, $h = 10^{-12}$ gives a worse estimate of the slope of $x^3$ at 1 than $h = 10^{-6}$. Use a moderate $h$ and the centered ruler.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="If a curve has no gaps, it has a derivative.">
    |x| and ReLU have no gaps and no derivative at 0: the rulers on the two sides close in on different numbers. See [every slope in between](#subgradients).
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A steep slope means a big step is safe.">
    The slope only describes the curve nearby. On $(w - 3)^2$ with learning rate 1.1, every step overshoots further than the one before.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
