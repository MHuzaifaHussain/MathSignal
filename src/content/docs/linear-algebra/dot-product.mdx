## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="The cart picture works only when the track arrow has length 1. With a longer track arrow the dot product grows with it, though the cart moves no further. The picture also gives the push and the track different jobs, while the dot product treats its two vectors the same way: a · b always equals b · a.">

A cart sits on a straight track that runs east across a sheet of squared paper. You push it with an arrow that goes 3 squares east and 4 squares north, a push of length 5. Only the east part, 3 squares, moves the cart. The north part presses into the rails and does nothing. Turn your push to point straight along the track and all 5 count. Push straight across the track and none of it counts. Push back toward the west and the count goes negative: the cart slows down.

A shopping bill shows the same sum without any angles. You buy 2 apples, 3 loaves and 1 carton of milk, at 0.5, 2 and 1.5 dollars. The bill is 2 × 0.5 + 3 × 2 + 1 × 1.5 = 8.5 dollars: multiply each count by its price, then add. Swap the roles of the two lists and the bill is the same, and doubling every price doubles the bill.

In symbols, the cart's sum is (3, 4) · (1, 0) = 3 × 1 + 4 × 0 = 3.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Every unit in a neural network starts by computing a dot product of its weights with its inputs, w · x, and then adds a bias. A recommender scores a user against an item with the dot product of two learned vectors, and a transformer scores each query against each key with one. Whether such a score comes out positive or negative, and how much it depends on the vectors' lengths rather than their directions, is what the rest of this page is about.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Multiply matching entries, then add

The dot product of two vectors with the same number of entries is the sum of the products of their matching entries. For two entries, a · b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂, and with more entries the sum just gets longer. The answer is a single number, not a vector.

The running example on this page is a = (3, 4) and b = (4, 3):

a · b = 3 × 4 + 4 × 3 = 12 + 12 = 24.

With three entries, (1, 2, 3) · (4, 5, 6) = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32, the example Margalit and Rabinoff use in Section 6.1 of *Interactive Linear Algebra* <Cite id="margalit-ila" />. Their section also notes that the dot product of two vectors is a scalar, a single number. Both vectors need the same number of entries; (1, 2) · (1, 2, 3) has no meaning.

Swapping the two vectors changes nothing, because each product a₁b₁ is the same as b₁a₁. Multiplying one vector by a number multiplies the dot product by that number, and the dot product spreads over a sum: (a + c) · b = a · b + c · b <Cite id="margalit-ila" />. These are the shopping bill's rules: swap the lists and the bill stays put, double the prices and it doubles.

<Simpler label="the shopping bill as a table" id="shopping-bill">

In NumPy, `a * b` multiplies matching entries and stops, so for the running pair it gives the list (12, 12). The dot product adds them: `a @ b` or `np.dot(a, b)` gives 24.
</SideNote>

## The sign tells you the angle

The sign of a · b says whether two arrows point roughly the same way, meet at a right angle or point apart. It is positive when the angle between them is under 90 degrees, 0 at exactly 90 degrees and negative when the angle is wider. ### Perpendicular arrows give 0

Two nonzero vectors are perpendicular exactly when their dot product is 0. For a = (3, 4) and b = (−4, 3), a · b = 3 × (−4) + 4 × 3 = −12 + 12 = 0. Margalit and Rabinoff take this as the definition: vectors are orthogonal, or perpendicular, when their dot product is 0, written with the symbol ⊥ <Cite id="margalit-ila" />. By that definition the zero vector is perpendicular to every vector, since 0 times anything is 0, but it has no direction and so no angle. Drag b to (0, 0) and the board says "a · b = 0 because b is the zero vector. The angle is undefined: b has no direction."

## Size: lengths times the cosine

The size of a · b is the product of the two lengths and the cosine of the angle between the arrows:

a · b = ‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos θ,

where ‖a‖ is the length of a and θ is the angle, from 0 to 180 degrees. The cosine is 1 at 0 degrees, 0 at 90 and −1 at 180, which is where the signs in the last section come from. The derivation is in the formal definition below.

### A vector's length from a · a

A vector's dot product with itself is its length squared. For a = (3, 4), a · a = 9 + 16 = 25, so ‖a‖ = √25 = 5, the Pythagorean theorem in the language of dot products <Cite id="margalit-ila" />. Both running vectors have length 5, so the formula gives cos θ = 24 / (5 × 5) = 24/25 = 0.96, and θ is about 16.26 degrees, the board's 16.3 to one decimal.

### Longer arrows, bigger numbers

Doubling b doubles a · b: (3, 4) · (8, 6) = 24 + 24 = 48, twice 24, with the same angle. This is the Long b preset in general form. Since lengths multiply the number, a · b on its own can't say how well two arrows line up. Dividing by both lengths removes them and leaves the cosine:

cos θ = a · b / (‖a‖ ‖b‖).

### The shadow of one arrow on the other

Dividing by one length gives the signed length of a shadow. a · b / ‖a‖ is how far b reaches along a's line: 24 / 5 = 4.8 for the running pair, the board's ochre segment. It is negative when b leans backward, as with the opening −0.24. When the arrow you divide by has length 1, the division does nothing and the dot product is the shadow itself, which is why the cart got exactly 3. A later page on projections turns this length into the shadow vector.

<GoDeeper label="why the ratio never leaves the range from −1 to 1" id="cauchy-schwarz">

For the cosine formula to make sense, a · b / (‖a‖ ‖b‖) must lie between −1 and 1. That is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, |a · b| ≤ ‖a‖ ‖b‖, and it follows from one fact: a length squared is never negative.

<StepThrough title="Cauchy–Schwarz from a quadratic that never goes below 0" rigor="full">

1. Let $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\mathbf{b} \ne \mathbf{0}$. (If $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$, both sides are 0.)
2. For every real $t$, $0 \le \lVert \mathbf{a} - t\,\mathbf{b} \rVert^2$, because a length squared is a sum of squares.
3. Expand with the rules above: $\lVert \mathbf{a} - t\,\mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 - 2t\,(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}) + t^2 \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$.
4. This is a quadratic in $t$ with positive leading coefficient $\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$ that never goes below 0, so it has at most one real root, and its discriminant is at most 0: $4(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})^2 - 4\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 \le 0$.
5. Divide by 4 and take square roots: $|\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}| \le \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert$. Equality holds exactly when $\mathbf{a} - t\,\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$ for some $t$, that is, when one vector is a multiple of the other.

</StepThrough>

For the running pair, $24^2 = 576 \le 625 = 25 \times 25$. In two or three dimensions this is a fact about triangles. In higher dimensions there is no picture to check against, and Cauchy–Schwarz is what makes the cosine formula a valid definition of the angle.

</GoDeeper>

## Alignment, or just long arrows?

A larger dot product does not always mean two arrows point more nearly the same way, because length counts too. Take a = (3, 4). Its dot product with itself is 25, but with (6, 8), which points the same way and is twice as long, it is 50. Both pairs have an angle of 0, yet one number is twice the other. To compare directions only, divide by both lengths, which gives the cosine, called cosine similarity in ML. A later comparison page sets the dot product, cosine similarity and Euclidean distance side by side.

The dot product is also tied to distance. Expanding a length squared gives

‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2 a · b.

For the running pair, a − b = (−1, 1), so ‖a − b‖² = 1 + 1 = 2, and the right side is 25 + 25 − 48 = 2. When every vector in a collection has the same length, the one with the largest dot product with a query is also the nearest one. When lengths differ, the two can disagree, which matters for search in ML below.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["vector", "vector-entry", "real-space", "dot-product", "dot-product-transpose", "norm", "absolute-value", "angle", "perpendicular", "unit-vector", "zero-vector", "sum", "transpose", "argmax", "softmax"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

For $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, written as columns, the dot product is the real number

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i.
$$

It is symmetric, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{a}$; linear in each vector, $(c\,\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{d}) \cdot \mathbf{b} = c\,(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}) + \mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{b}$ for every real $c$; and $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 \ge 0$, with equality only for $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}$. For nonzero $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, the angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ is defined by $\cos\theta = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} / (\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert)$, which lies in $[-1, 1]$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}| \le \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert$. We write $\mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{b}$ when $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$.

Boundary cases. The zero vector has dot product 0 with every vector and no angle with any. The dot product is defined only for two vectors with the same number of entries. Scope: real vectors only; vectors with complex entries need a complex conjugate in the sum and are not covered here.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<div id="law-of-cosines-derivation">

<StepThrough title="From the law of cosines to a · b = ‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos θ" rigor="full">

1. Let $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ be nonzero vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$, and let $\theta$ be the angle between them. Draw $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ from the origin. With the segment from the tip of $\mathbf{b}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{a}$, which is the vector $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$, they form a triangle whose angle at the origin is $\theta$.
2. A length squared is a dot product: $\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b})$.
3. The dot product spreads over sums: this equals $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b}$.
4. Symmetry turns the two middle terms into one, and $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2$: $\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$.
5. The law of cosines on the same triangle, whose angle between the sides $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is $\theta$: $\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 - 2\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta$.
6. The left sides of steps 4 and 5 are equal, so the right sides are too. Cancel $\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$ and divide by $-2$: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta$.

</StepThrough>

</div>

Check with the running pair: step 4 gives $2 = 25 + 25 - 48$, and step 6 gives $5 \times 5 \times 0.96 = 24$. In $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $n > 3$ there is no triangle to draw, so the order is reversed: the equation in step 6 is taken as the definition of the angle between two vectors, which is how Margalit and Rabinoff define angles in higher dimensions <Cite id="margalit-ila" />. Cauchy–Schwarz guarantees that the cosine it gives lies between −1 and 1.

| Property | Statement | For $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$, $\mathbf{b} = (4, 3)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symmetry | $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{a}$ | $3 \times 4 + 4 \times 3 = 4 \times 3 + 3 \times 4 = 24$ |
| Scaling | $(c\,\mathbf{a}) \cdot \mathbf{b} = c\,(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})$ | $(6, 8) \cdot (4, 3) = 24 + 24 = 48 = 2 \times 24$ |
| Length | $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2$ | $9 + 16 = 25 = 5^2$ |
| Angle | $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta$ | $\cos\theta = 24/25$, $\theta \approx 16.26°$ |
| Perpendicular | $\mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{b} \iff \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$ | $(3, 4) \cdot (-4, 3) = 0$ |
| Cauchy–Schwarz | $\lvert \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} \rvert \le \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert$ | $24 \le 25$ |
| Distance | $\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$ | $2 = 25 + 25 - 48$ |

</FormalDefinition>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="One neuron, two input vectors" faded>

A neuron has weights $\mathbf{w} = (0.8, -0.5, 0.3)$ and bias $-1$. For the input $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1, 4)$, its weighted sum is $\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} = 1.6 - 0.5 + 1.2 = 2.3$, and adding the bias gives $1.3$, a positive score. Your turn: find the score for $\mathbf{x}' = (1, 3, 0)$. Is it positive or negative?

<div slot="solution">

$\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}' = 0.8 \times 1 + (-0.5) \times 3 + 0.3 \times 0 = 0.8 - 1.5 + 0 = -0.7$, and adding the bias gives $-1.7$, a negative score. The second input leans toward the negative weight, the second entry, and the third entry, which had helped the first input, is now 0.

</div>

## Where the dot product shows up in ML

### Every neuron's first step

A unit in a neural network computes the dot product of its weight vector with its input and adds a number, its bias: $z = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b$, where $b$ here is a plain number, not a vector. The sign of $\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}$ says whether the input leans toward the weights or away from them. A linear classifier with one weight vector $\mathbf{w}_i$ per class predicts the class whose dot product with the input is largest,

$$
y = \arg\max_{i} \; \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{w}_i,
$$

which Shrivastava and Li write as their equation (6) for multi-class SVMs and logistic regression, noting that the weight vectors do not all have the same length (§1.4) <Cite id="shrivastava2014" />. So a class can win because its weight vector is long, not only because it points along the input.

### Recommenders

A matrix-factorization recommender gives each user $i$ a vector $\mathbf{u}_i$ and each item $j$ a vector $\mathbf{v}_j$, and models the user's rating of the item as $\mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j$ plus a constant and two bias terms (Shrivastava and Li, §1.1, equation 3). To recommend, it looks for the item with the largest score, $\arg\max_j \mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j$ (their equation 4), and they note that the item vectors' lengths often vary over a wide range <Cite id="shrivastava2014" />. Long item vectors get high scores from many users.

### Attention scores

A transformer's attention compares a query vector $\mathbf{q}$ with each key vector $\mathbf{k}$ through $\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k}$. Stacking the queries as the rows of a matrix $Q$ and the keys as the rows of $K$ puts every such dot product into $QK^\top$, and Vaswani and coauthors define

$$
\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V,
$$

their equation (1) in §3.2.1, where $d_k$ is the number of entries in each query and key <Cite id="vaswani2017" />. The softmax turns each row of scores into weights that add up to 1, so a key with a larger dot product with the query gets more weight.

<GoDeeper label="why attention divides by the square root of d_k" id="attention-scale">

The authors suspect that for large $d_k$ the dot products grow large in size and push the softmax into regions with very small gradients, so they divide each one by $\sqrt{d_k}$. Their footnote 4 gives the reason: if the entries of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1, then $\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i$ has mean 0 and variance $d_k$ <Cite id="vaswani2017" />. In their base model $d_k = 64$ (§3.2.2), so under that assumption the raw scores have a standard deviation of 8, and dividing by $\sqrt{64} = 8$ brings the variance back to 1. The code below checks this exactly for entries that are $+1$ or $-1$ with equal chance. A later page on scaled dot-product attention derives the variance in general.

</GoDeeper>

### Similarity search

Search over embeddings often asks for the stored vector with the largest dot product with a query, a task called maximum inner product search (on $\mathbb{R}^n$ the usual inner product is the dot product). It differs from nearest-neighbor search, because

$$
\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \lVert \mathbf{q} - \mathbf{x} \rVert^2 = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \big( \lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{x} \big),
$$

where $\lVert \mathbf{q} \rVert^2$ is dropped because it is the same for every candidate. Shrivastava and Li's equation (2) shows the two searches agree when every stored vector has the same length <Cite id="shrivastava2014" />. Otherwise they can disagree. With the query $(1, 0)$ and stored vectors $(1, 0)$ and $(3, 1)$, the nearest one is $(1, 0)$, at distance 0, but the largest dot product, 3, belongs to $(3, 1)$.

def dot(a, b):
    """Multiply matching entries and add: one number."""
    if len(a) != len(b):
        raise ValueError(f"a has {len(a)} entries and b has {len(b)}; the dot product needs the same number")
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))


def squared_length(a):
    return dot(a, a)


def cosine(a, b):
    """cos(theta) = a . b / (|a| |b|), as an exact fraction when both lengths are whole numbers."""
    if squared_length(a) == 0 or squared_length(b) == 0:

    for v in (a, b):

## Research frontier

<ResearchFrontier reviewed="2026-11-25">

**Finding the largest dot product fast.** A recommender or a retrieval system may hold millions of item vectors, and checking the query's dot product with every one of them is too slow. For nearest-neighbor search, locality-sensitive hashing (LSH), which hashes similar points into the same bucket with high probability, gives fast approximate answers. Shrivastava and Li show that this framework cannot work for the largest dot product: there can be no LSH family for maximum inner product search (§1.6). They then allow asymmetric hashing, with different transformations for the stored vectors and for the query, which turns the problem into approximate nearest-neighbor search, and obtain what they call the first provably sublinear-time algorithm for approximate maximum inner product search. They test it on item recommendation with the Netflix and Movielens datasets <Cite id="shrivastava2014" />. The obstacle is the one from the section on alignment: when stored vectors have different lengths, the largest dot product and the nearest point differ.

**Compressing vectors with the dot product in mind.** Systems at this scale usually store compressed vectors. Guo and coauthors argue that ordinary compression, which minimizes how far each compressed point lands from the original, treats every error the same, while for a query the points with the largest dot products matter most. Their anisotropic loss penalizes the part of a point's error that lies along the point itself more than the part perpendicular to it, the same split into "along" and "into the rails" as the cart board, and they report state-of-the-art results on public approximate-nearest-neighbor benchmarks <Cite id="guo2019" />. On the hardware side, Johnson, Douze and Jégou build a k-nearest-neighbor graph over 1 billion vectors in under 12 hours on 4 GPUs, with a nearest-neighbor implementation 8.5 times faster than earlier GPU methods <Cite id="johnson2017" />. That result is for nearest-neighbor search, which equals the largest dot product only when the vectors have equal lengths.

**Where to read further.** Bruch's monograph *Foundations of Vector Retrieval* collects the data structures and algorithms for finding similar vectors, written to lower the barrier of entry to the area <Cite id="bruch2024" />.

</ResearchFrontier>

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Two nonzero vectors have a · b = −10. Is the angle between them less than, equal to, or more than 90 degrees?">
More than 90 degrees. Since $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta$ and both lengths are positive, the sign of the dot product is the sign of $\cos\theta$, and the cosine is negative only for angles between 90 and 180 degrees. The size, 10, says nothing about the angle until you know the lengths.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Are (2, −1, 3) and (4, 5, −1) perpendicular?">
Yes. Their dot product is $2 \times 4 + (-1) \times 5 + 3 \times (-1) = 8 - 5 - 3 = 0$, and both vectors are nonzero, so the angle between them is exactly 90 degrees. You don't need their lengths to decide.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="‖a‖ = 2, ‖b‖ = 3 and the angle between them is 60 degrees. What is a · b?">
$3$. The formula gives $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 2 \times 3 \times \cos 60° = 6 \times 1/2 = 3$. It is positive because 60 degrees is under a right angle, and smaller than $6$ because the arrows are not lined up.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that ‖a + b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² + 2 a · b, and deduce Pythagoras for perpendicular vectors. Check it with (3, 4) and (−4, 3).">
$\lVert \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 + 2\,\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$, using that the dot product spreads over sums and is symmetric. If $\mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{b}$, the last term is 0 and $\lVert \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$, the Pythagorean theorem. For $(3, 4) + (-4, 3) = (-1, 7)$: $1 + 49 = 50 = 25 + 25$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="The dot product of two vectors is a vector.">
    It is a single number. $(3, 4) \cdot (4, 3) = 24$, not a pair. The operation that keeps a list, one product per entry, is a different one.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Multiply matching entries and stop: (3, 4) · (4, 3) = (12, 12).">
    That is the entry-by-entry product. The dot product adds the products: $12 + 12 = 24$. In NumPy, `a * b` gives the list and `a @ b` gives the dot product.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A bigger dot product always means two vectors are more alike.">
    Length counts as much as direction. $(3, 4) \cdot (6, 8) = 50$ is bigger than $(3, 4) \cdot (3, 4) = 25$, though both pairs point exactly the same way, and the query $(1, 0)$ has a larger dot product with $(3, 1)$ than with itself. To compare directions only, divide by both lengths.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="If a · b = 0, one of the vectors must be the zero vector.">
    Two nonzero vectors at a right angle also give 0: $(3, 4) \cdot (-4, 3) = -12 + 12 = 0$. A dot product of 0 means perpendicular, or that one vector is zero.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The angle formula works for any two vectors.">
    With a zero vector it gives $0/0$, which has no value. The zero vector has no direction, so it has no angle with anything, and the board says so: "The angle is undefined: b has no direction."
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
