## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Whole steps reach every grid spot only by luck of the east and north cards. With 1 east, 1 north and 1 west, 2 north, the spot 1 east needs two thirds of the first card and minus one third of the second: a span uses every real number of steps. And backward walking is not optional; without it, two cards reach only a wedge of the field.">

On the playground grid from the vectors page, you start at the gate with two trip cards: "1 step east" and "1 step north". You may walk each card as many times as you like, part of a time, or backward. To reach the bench 4 east and 5 north, walk the east card 4 times and the north card 5 times. Every spot can be reached this way, and the two counts are the spot's coordinates.

Swap the cards for "1 east, 1 north" and "1 west, 2 north". Can you still reach every spot? Now take "1 east, 1 north" and "3 east, 3 north". The second card is the first walked three times, so however you mix them, you stay on one diagonal path through the gate. And walking every card zero times leaves you at the gate, so the gate is always reachable.

The field hides one thing. A drone in a sports hall with only the controls "forward" and "left" can reach any spot at its own height, but never the ceiling. Two directions in a three-dimensional room give a flat floor, not the whole room.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
A layer of a neural network multiplies its input by a weight matrix, and that product is a linear combination of the matrix's columns, with the input's entries as the weights. So every output the layer can produce lies in the span of those columns, and a layer with 2 inputs and 3 outputs can only ever produce points of one plane in R³. Fine-tuning methods such as LoRA use the same fact on purpose: they let a layer's weights change only within the span of a few vectors, which cuts the number of numbers to train.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Scale, then add: a linear combination

A linear combination of two vectors **a** and **b** is c **a** + d **b**: scale each one by a number, its weight, and add the results. On the board, 1 × (2, 1) + 2 × (1, 2) = (2, 1) + (2, 4) = (4, 5). Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville give the general version, for any number of vectors: multiply each vector by a scalar coefficient and add the results (Section 2.4, equation 2.28, p. 34) <Cite id="goodfellow2016linalg" />. Sanderson calls scaling two vectors and adding them a linear combination too <Cite id="sanderson2016span" />.

The word "linear" has a picture behind it: fix d = 1 and let c run, and the tips c (2, 1) + (1, 2) march along a straight line through (1, 2), parallel to **a**. A single vector is a linear combination too (take d = 0), and so is the zero vector **0** (take both weights 0).

### The weights are the coordinates, for east and north

Coordinates are weights: the point (4, 5) is 4 steps east plus 5 steps north, 4 **e**₁ + 5 **e**₂, where **e**₁ = (1, 0) and **e**₂ = (0, 1). Sanderson reads coordinates the same way, as scalars that stretch the two unit vectors along the axes <Cite id="sanderson2016span" />. He writes them î and ĵ. Other pairs give other weights for the same point, as (2, 1) and (1, 2) give 1 and 2 for (4, 5), an idea the page on basis and dimension takes up.

### Which weight goes with which vector matters

1 **a** + 2 **b** = (4, 5), but 2 **a** + 1 **b** = (5, 4). The order of adding never matters, as the [vectors page's section on adding](/linear-algebra/vectors/#adding-tip-to-tail-entry-by-entry) showed; pairing a weight with a vector does.

<Simpler label="finding the weights with two small equations" id="weights-by-hand">

For the flag, c (2, 1) + d (1, 2) = (4, 5) means one equation per entry: 2c + d = 4 for east and c + 2d = 5 for north. Double the second, 2c + 4d = 10, and subtract the first: 3d = 6, so d = 2. Then c + 4 = 5 gives c = 1. Worked example 1 below uses the same method with new numbers.

</Simpler>

<GoDeeper label="a formula for every point" id="finding-the-weights">

With **a** = (2, 1) and **b** = (1, 2), any point (x, y) is reached with $c = (2x - y)/3$ and $d = (2y - x)/3$. Check: (4, 5) gives $c = 3/3 = 1$ and $d = 6/3 = 2$. The point (1, 0) gives $c = 2/3$, $d = -1/3$, and (0, 1) gives $c = -1/3$, $d = 2/3$: fractions that no half-step slider reaches. A slider weight is a multiple of 1/2, and $(2x - y)/3$ is one exactly when 3 divides $2x - y$, which is where the board's 41 points come from. The 3 in the denominator is $2 \times 2 - 1 \times 1$; the third proof under Formal definition derives the formula for any pair.

</GoDeeper>

## Span: every point you can reach

The span of some vectors is the set of every linear combination of them: all the points you can reach by scaling them and adding. Margalit and Rabinoff define it as the collection of all linear combinations of v₁, …, v_k (Section 2.2) <Cite id="margalit-ila-spans" />, and Goodfellow et al. as the set of all points obtainable by linear combination of the original vectors (Section 2.4, p. 34) <Cite id="goodfellow2016linalg" />.

The span always contains the origin, because you can take every weight 0, so a span is never a line that misses the origin. The span of one nonzero vector is the line through the origin and its tip: as the [vectors page's section on scaling](/linear-algebra/vectors/#multiplying-a-vector-by-a-number) showed, c **a** never leaves that line. The zero vector alone spans just the origin. When there are many vectors to picture, Sanderson suggests drawing each one as a point at its tip rather than as an arrow <Cite id="sanderson2016span" />, so a span becomes a set of points: a dot, a line or a filled plane.

Two vectors in the plane reach either a line or the whole plane (or, if both are zero, only the origin), and the board below asks you to say which before it shows you.

A line. Check says "Yes. b = −2 × a, so a and b lie on one line through the origin, and every c a + d b stays on it. The span is that line." and draws that line in ochre. Now press Nearly lined up: **a** = (4, 2) and **b** = (3, 2). The readout says "a and b point along different lines, so their span is the whole plane." The span doesn't shrink a little as the arrows close in. It stays the whole plane until they line up exactly, and then it jumps to a line. Both zero reads "a and b are both the zero vector, so every c a + d b is (0, 0). The span is just the origin." On the first board, Same line sets **b** = (4, 2) and ends "The flag is off that line, so no c and d reach it."

So two vectors in the plane span a line, not the plane, exactly when one is a multiple of the other. Sanderson gives the same three cases: for most pairs the span is the whole plane, it is a line when the two line up, and only the origin when both are zero <Cite id="sanderson2016span" />.

There is a test with two numbers. For **a** = (a₁, a₂) and **b** = (b₁, b₂), the vectors lie on one line exactly when a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ = 0. For this board's pair, 2 × (−2) − 1 × (−4) = 0, a line. For the first board's pair, 2 × 2 − 1 × 1 = 3, the whole plane, and for the nearly lined-up pair 4 × 2 − 2 × 3 = 2, still the whole plane. This number gets its own name later, on the page about the determinant.

A lined-up vector adds nothing new: with **b** = −2 **a**, every c **a** + d **b** equals (c − 2d) **a**. Vectors that each bring a new direction are called linearly independent, the subject of a later page.

<GoDeeper label="nearly lined up: still the whole plane, at a price" id="nearly-lined-up">

With **a** = (4, 2) and **b** = (3, 2), the point (1, 0) is 1 × **a** − 1 × **b**, and (0, 1) is −1.5 × **a** + 2 × **b**. With the first board's **a** = (2, 1) and **b** = (1, 2), the point (0, 1) is −1/3 × **a** + 2/3 × **b**. Both pairs span the same plane, but the nearly lined-up pair needs weights up to 2 in size where the other needs at most 2/3, and its weights cancel each other. When two features of a dataset nearly line up, a model's weights for them can grow large and swing in opposite directions; the page on linear independence takes this up.

</GoDeeper>

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Is (0, 0) in the span of (5, −3) and (1, 4)?">
Yes: $0 \times (5, -3) + 0 \times (1, 4) = (0, 0)$. Every span contains the zero vector, whatever the vectors are.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="What is the span of (3, −2) and (−6, 4)?">
A line through the origin. $(-6, 4) = -2 \times (3, -2)$, so the two vectors lie on one line, and the test agrees: $3 \times 4 - (-2) \times (-6) = 12 - 12 = 0$.
</CheckYourself>

### Two vectors in space: a plane, not all of space

In three dimensions, two vectors that don't line up span a flat plane through the origin, so most points of space are out of reach. Margalit and Rabinoff describe it as the plane containing the origin and the heads of the vectors, and note that three vectors in one plane span only that plane (Section 2.2) <Cite id="margalit-ila-spans" />. Sanderson pictures a flat sheet through the origin, which a third vector widens only if it points off the sheet <Cite id="sanderson2016span" />.

Take **u** = (1, 0, 1) and **v** = (0, 1, 1). Then 2 **u** + 3 **v** = (2, 3, 5), in the span. The point (1, 1, 0) is not: the first two entries force c = 1 and d = 1, and then the third entry is 2, not 0. This is the drone in the hall: two controls, one floor. Goodfellow et al. make the same point with a matrix of 3 rows and 2 columns: changing its 2-entry input traces out at best a 2-D plane within R³ (Section 2.4, p. 35) <Cite id="goodfellow2016linalg" />.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["vector", "scalar", "zero-vector", "linear-combination", "span", "set-builder", "standard-basis", "matrix", "matrix-vector-product"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

In plain words: a linear combination scales some vectors and adds them; their span is every linear combination, with any real weights.

Let $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$, with $k \ge 1$. A linear combination of them is $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$ with $c_1, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}$, and their span is

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \{\, c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k : c_1, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} \,\}.
$$

Conventions: the weights are any real numbers, negatives and fractions included; vectors are columns, written inline as (2, 1).

Boundary cases. $\mathbf{0}$ is in every span (all weights 0), and $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{0}\} = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. For $\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0}$, $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ is the line through the origin and $\mathbf{a}$. For $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2$, $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\} = \mathbb{R}^2$ exactly when $a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \ne 0$; otherwise it is a line if $\mathbf{a}$ or $\mathbf{b}$ is nonzero, and $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ if both are zero. In $\mathbb{R}^3$, two vectors span at most a plane through the origin. Adding a vector that is already in the span does not change the span.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

| Property | Statement | With $\mathbf{a} = (2, 1)$, $\mathbf{b} = (1, 2)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contains the origin | $\mathbf{0} = 0\,\mathbf{a} + 0\,\mathbf{b}$ | $(0, 0)$ |
| Closed under adding | $(c_1\mathbf{a} + d_1\mathbf{b}) + (c_2\mathbf{a} + d_2\mathbf{b}) = (c_1 + c_2)\mathbf{a} + (d_1 + d_2)\mathbf{b}$ | $(3, 3) + (-1, 1) = 0\,\mathbf{a} + 2\,\mathbf{b} = (2, 4)$ |
| Closed under scaling | $k(c\,\mathbf{a} + d\,\mathbf{b}) = (kc)\,\mathbf{a} + (kd)\,\mathbf{b}$ | $2 \times (3, 3) = 2\,\mathbf{a} + 2\,\mathbf{b} = (6, 6)$ |
| A redundant vector adds nothing | $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}\} = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\}$ | see the second proof |

<StepThrough title="A span is closed under adding and scaling" rigor="full">

1. Take two points of $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\}$: $\mathbf{p} = c_1\mathbf{a} + d_1\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{q} = c_2\mathbf{a} + d_2\mathbf{b}$.
2. $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{q} = (c_1 + c_2)\mathbf{a} + (d_1 + d_2)\mathbf{b}$, by the laws on the vectors page that adding is order-free and grouping-free and that scalars add ([anything you can add and scale](/linear-algebra/vectors/#vector-spaces)).
3. That is again a linear combination of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, so $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{q}$ is in the span.
4. For a number $k$, $k\mathbf{p} = (kc_1)\mathbf{a} + (kd_1)\mathbf{b}$, by the laws for scaling a sum and scaling twice, so $k\mathbf{p}$ is in the span.
5. With $k = 0$, $\mathbf{0}$ is in the span. Check: $\mathbf{p} = (3, 3)$ ($c = d = 1$) and $\mathbf{q} = (-1, 1)$ ($c = -1$, $d = 1$) give $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{q} = (2, 4) = 0\,\mathbf{a} + 2\,\mathbf{b}$.

</StepThrough>

These three facts make a span what is called a subspace; subspaces get their own page later.

<StepThrough title="Adding a vector that is already reachable changes nothing" rigor="full">

1. Let $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$. Every $c\,\mathbf{a} + d\,\mathbf{b}$ equals $c\,\mathbf{a} + d\,\mathbf{b} + 0\,\mathbf{w}$, so $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\} \subseteq \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{w}\}$.
2. Any $x\,\mathbf{a} + y\,\mathbf{b} + z\,\mathbf{w} = x\,\mathbf{a} + y\,\mathbf{b} + z(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = (x + z)\,\mathbf{a} + (y + z)\,\mathbf{b}$, so $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{w}\} \subseteq \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\}$.
3. Both inclusions hold, so the spans are equal. Check: $(4, 5) = 1\,\mathbf{a} + 2\,\mathbf{b} = -1\,\mathbf{a} + 0\,\mathbf{b} + 2\,\mathbf{w} = -1 \times (2, 1) + 2 \times (3, 3)$.

</StepThrough>

The same argument works for any vector $\mathbf{w}$ in the span: replace $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$ by $\mathbf{w}$'s own weights.

<StepThrough title="Two vectors in the plane that don't line up reach every point" rigor="full">

1. Let $\mathbf{a} = (a_1, a_2)$, $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2)$ and $D = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \ne 0$. Take any target $(x, y)$.
2. We need $c\,a_1 + d\,b_1 = x$ and $c\,a_2 + d\,b_2 = y$.
3. Multiply the first equation by $b_2$, the second by $b_1$, and subtract: $c\,(a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1) = x b_2 - y b_1$, so $c = (x b_2 - y b_1)/D$.
4. Likewise, multiplying by $a_2$ and $a_1$ gives $d = (a_1 y - a_2 x)/D$.
5. Substituting back gives $(x, y)$ exactly, so every point is reached: $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\} = \mathbb{R}^2$.
6. The converse, as a sketch: if $D = 0$, then $a_1 b_2 = a_2 b_1$, so $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are multiples of one vector (or zero), and every combination stays on that line. Check: $\mathbf{a} = (2, 1)$, $\mathbf{b} = (1, 2)$ give $D = 3$, $c = (2x - y)/3$ and $d = (2y - x)/3$, the formula from the fold above.

</StepThrough>

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="Is (7, 4) a combination of (1, 2) and (3, 1)?">

We need $c\,(1, 2) + d\,(3, 1) = (7, 4)$, so $c + 3d = 7$ and $2c + d = 4$. The second gives $d = 4 - 2c$; then $c + 12 - 6c = 7$, so $c = 1$ and $d = 2$. Check: $1 \times (1, 2) + 2 \times (3, 1) = (1, 2) + (6, 2) = (7, 4)$. Since $1 \times 1 - 2 \times 3 = -5 \ne 0$, this pair reaches every point.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="In the span or not, in three dimensions">

Let $\mathbf{u} = (1, 0, 1)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (0, 1, 1)$. Is $(2, 3, 5)$ in their span? The first entry of $c\,\mathbf{u} + d\,\mathbf{v}$ is $c$, so $c = 2$; the second is $d$, so $d = 3$; the third is $c + d = 5$, which matches. Yes: $2\,\mathbf{u} + 3\,\mathbf{v}$. Is $(1, 1, 0)$? The first two entries give $c = 1$ and $d = 1$, and then the third entry is 2, not 0. No. Two of the equations fix the weights, and the third is the test.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Which outputs can this layer produce?" faded>

A layer with 2 inputs and 3 outputs has the weight columns $(1, 2, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, so its output for the input $(x_1, x_2)$ is $x_1 (1, 2, 0) + x_2 (0, 1, 1)$. Can it output $(2, 5, 1)$? Can it output $(2, 5, 2)$?

<div slot="solution">

The output is $(x_1,\ 2x_1 + x_2,\ x_2)$. For $(2, 5, 1)$: the first entry gives $x_1 = 2$, the second $4 + x_2 = 5$ gives $x_2 = 1$, and the third entry is $x_2 = 1$, which matches. So the input $(2, 1)$ produces it. For $(2, 5, 2)$, the same two entries force $x_2 = 1$, but the third entry asks for 2. No input produces it: $(2, 5, 2)$ is outside the span of the columns.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Where spans show up in machine learning

Every output of a linear layer, every attention output and every low-rank weight update is a linear combination of a fixed set of vectors, so it lies in their span.

### A layer's outputs live in the span of its columns

A matrix times a vector is a linear combination of the matrix's columns. Goodfellow et al. read the columns of a matrix as directions to travel from the origin, with each entry of the input saying how far to move along its column (Section 2.4, equation 2.27, p. 34) <Cite id="goodfellow2016linalg" />:

$$
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} = x_1\,\mathbf{W}_{:,1} + \cdots + x_n\,\mathbf{W}_{:,n}.
$$

With the columns (1, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 1) of worked example 2, the input (2, 3) gives (2, 3, 5), and no input gives (1, 1, 0). The equation $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$ has a solution exactly when $\mathbf{y}$ is in the span of the columns, the column space, and reaching every $\mathbf{y}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ needs at least $m$ columns (p. 35) <Cite id="goodfellow2016linalg" />.

So a linear layer from 2 inputs to 3 outputs can only produce points of one plane, whatever the input. A bias shifts that plane off the origin, and an activation function after the layer bends it.

### An attention output is a weighted sum of values

In a transformer, the output of attention is computed as a weighted sum of the values (Section 3.2) <Cite id="vaswani2017" />, with weights that add up to 1 ([where the attention weights come from](/linear-algebra/dot-product/#attention-scores)). With weights 1/2, 1/4 and 1/4 and values (2, 0), (0, 4) and (4, 4), the output is (1, 0) + (0, 1) + (1, 1) = (2, 2), a linear combination of the values, so it lies in their span.

### Low-rank fine-tuning keeps updates in a small span

LoRA freezes a pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_0$, with $d$ rows and $k$ columns (the paper's letters, not the boards' weight d), and learns an update $\Delta\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$, where $\mathbf{B}$ has $d$ rows and $r$ columns, $\mathbf{A}$ has $r$ rows and $k$ columns, and $r$ is much smaller than $d$ and $k$. The layer computes $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{W}_0\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ (Section 4.1, equation 3). $\mathbf{A}$ starts random and $\mathbf{B}$ at zero, so the update is zero when training begins <Cite id="hu2021" />.

Read with spans: $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ is a list of just $r$ numbers, so $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})$ is a linear combination of the $r$ columns of $\mathbf{B}$. However the input changes, the update stays in the span of those $r$ vectors. The paper reports that for GPT-3 175B, LoRA reduces the trainable parameters by 10,000 times (abstract), and that a rank as small as one was enough for adapting the query and value matrices on the datasets they tried, though they don't expect a small rank to work for every task (Section 7.2, Table 6) <Cite id="hu2021" />.

<Simpler label="the update with four numbers a side" id="lora-by-hand">

A small illustration of our own: take $d = k = 4$ and $r = 1$, with $\mathbf{B} = (1, 2, 0, 1)$ as a column, $\mathbf{A} = (1, 0, -1, 2)$ as a row and $\mathbf{x} = (1, 1, 1, 1)$. Then $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = 1 + 0 - 1 + 2 = 2$, and $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = (2, 4, 0, 2)$, which is $2\,\mathbf{B}$. Any other input gives some other multiple of $\mathbf{B}$: the update always lies on the line that $\mathbf{B}$ spans. Training $\Delta\mathbf{W}$ directly would mean 16 numbers; $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ hold 8. At full size, for a matrix of 4,096 by 4,096 with $r = 8$ (again our own arithmetic), the update has 65,536 numbers to train instead of 16,777,216, 256 times fewer.

</Simpler>

### The softmax bottleneck

A language model turns a context into a vector of $d$ numbers $h_1, \ldots, h_d$, and its scores for every word in the vocabulary are $h_1\,\mathbf{w}_1 + \cdots + h_d\,\mathbf{w}_d$, a linear combination of the $d$ columns of the word-embedding matrix. So every context's scores lie in the span of $d$ vectors, however many words there are, and Yang, Dai, Salakhutdinov and Cohen show that this can leave some context with the wrong next-word distribution <Cite id="yang2017" />.

<GoDeeper label="the bound, and the limit with three words" id="softmax-bottleneck-toy">

The matrix of scores over all contexts has rank at most $d$, and if $d$ is less than the rank of the true log-probability matrix minus 1, some context gets the wrong distribution (Section 2.1, Corollary 1). They note that $d$ is usually around $10^2$ while that rank could be as high as the vocabulary size, around $10^5$ (Section 2.2), and they escape the limit by averaging several softmaxes (Section 2.4) <Cite id="yang2017" />. Rank, the number of independent directions in a span, gets its own page later.

Take 3 words and $d = 1$, with the embedding column $(1, 2, -1)$, so every context's scores are $h\,(1, 2, -1)$ for one number $h$. A softmax gives the same probabilities if the same number $t$ is added to every score, so the scores that matter are $h\,(1, 2, -1) + t\,(1, 1, 1)$: the span of two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The scores $(1, 1, 0)$, which make words 1 and 2 equally likely and word 3 less likely, would need $h + t = 1$ and $2h + t = 1$, so $h = 0$ and $t = 1$, and then the third score is 1, not 0. Shifting $(1, 1, 0)$ by any $s$ changes nothing: $h + t = 1 + s$ and $2h + t = 1 + s$ still force $h = 0$, and the third score $t = 1 + s$ never equals $s$. No context can produce that distribution. This toy is our own, not the paper's.

</GoDeeper>

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Two vectors in the plane, not both zero, lie on one line. Can adding a third vector on the same line make their span bigger?">
No. Every multiple of a vector on that line stays on the line, so the third vector is already in the span, and a vector that is already reachable adds nothing. The span grows only when a new vector leaves the line.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Write (3, −1) as a combination of (1, 1) and (1, −1).">
$c = 1$ and $d = 2$. The entries give $c + d = 3$ and $c - d = -1$; adding them gives $2c = 2$. Check: $(1, 1) + (2, -2) = (3, -1)$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A layer has 3 inputs and 2 outputs, and its 3 weight columns are (1, 0), (0, 1) and (1, 1). Which outputs can it produce?">
Every point of the plane. The first two columns already reach every $(x, y)$ as $x\,(1, 0) + y\,(0, 1)$, and the third column $(1, 1)$ is their sum, so it adds nothing new. Three columns in $\mathbb{R}^2$ can never reach more than $\mathbb{R}^2$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that the span of a and b equals the span of a and a + b.">
Each side's combinations are combinations of the other's. $x\,\mathbf{a} + y\,\mathbf{b} = (x - y)\,\mathbf{a} + y\,(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b})$, and $c\,\mathbf{a} + d\,(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = (c + d)\,\mathbf{a} + d\,\mathbf{b}$. Check with $\mathbf{a} = (2, 1)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (1, 2)$: $(4, 5) = 1\,\mathbf{a} + 2\,\mathbf{b} = -1 \times (2, 1) + 2 \times (3, 3)$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Two vectors always span the plane.">
    Only if they don't lie on one line. $(2, 1)$ and $(-4, -2)$ span a line, because $(-4, -2) = -2 \times (2, 1)$; the test gives $2 \times (-2) - 1 \times (-4) = 0$.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The span is the points you reach with whole steps.">
    The weights are any real numbers. With $(2, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$, the point $(1, 0)$ needs $2/3$ and $-1/3$, and the board's half-step sliders reach only 41 of its 121 whole-number points.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Weights have to be positive.">
    A negative weight walks backward: $(-1, 1) = -1 \times (2, 1) + 1 \times (1, 2)$. Without negative weights, two vectors along different lines reach only a wedge between them, not the whole plane.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Two vectors in space span all of space.">
    They span at most a plane through the origin. $(1, 1, 0)$ is not in the span of $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$: the first two entries force both weights to be 1, and then the third entry is 2.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A span can be a line that misses the origin, such as the line through the tips of the arrows.">
    Every span contains $\mathbf{0}$, with all weights 0. The line through the tips of $(2, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$ misses the origin, so it is not a span; the span of those two is the whole plane.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
