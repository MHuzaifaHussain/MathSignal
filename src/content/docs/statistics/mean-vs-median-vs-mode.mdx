## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="The jar's share is never wrong: if the cafe really pooled its savings, that is what each person would get, so the mean answers a different question from the median, not a worse one. And the mode needs values that repeat: savings counted to the cent would hardly ever tie.">

Nine regulars sit in a cafe, each with some savings, in thousands of dollars: 1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5 and 7. Pour everyone's savings into one jar, 36 in all, and share it out equally: each gets 4. That is the mean. Line the regulars up from poorest to richest and ask the one in the middle: 4. That is the median. Ask which amount the most people hold: 5, held by three regulars. That is the mode.

Then a millionaire walks in with 1,000 thousand dollars, a million. Guess what that does to each answer before you try it on the board below.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
A flexible enough model that predicts a number learns a mean if it is trained to keep its squared misses small, and a median if it is trained to keep its plain distance from the answer small <Cite id="goodfellow2016" />. Filling a missing value with a column's mean, median or most frequent value is a choice data pipelines make on every feature <Cite id="sklearn-simpleimputer" />.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["data-value", "sample-size", "sum", "mu", "order-statistic", "median", "mode", "absolute-value", "argmin", "expectation", "conditional-expectation"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be $n \ge 1$ values.

**Mean** (needs numbers you can add):

$$
\vmu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad \text{so} \qquad n\vmu = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i .
$$

Among all single guesses $c$, it is the only one that makes $\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - c)^2$ smallest.

**Median** (needs an order). Sort the values to $x_{(1)} \le \cdots \le x_{(n)}$. Then

$$
\operatorname{median}(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \begin{cases} x_{((n+1)/2)} & n \text{ odd,} \\ \tfrac{1}{2}\big(x_{(n/2)} + x_{(n/2+1)}\big) & n \text{ even,} \end{cases}
$$

as NIST defines it <Cite id="nist-location" />. For odd $n$ only the median makes $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lvert x_i - c \rvert$ smallest; for even $n$ every $c$ from $x_{(n/2)}$ to $x_{(n/2+1)}$ does, and the median is that interval's midpoint.

**Mode** (needs only values you can compare, labels included). The values held by the most $x_i$ each make the number of misses, the count of $i$ with $x_i \ne c$, as small as it can be. They are the modes, $\operatorname{mode}(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, except that on this site a list of at least two different values that all appear equally often has no mode. When only some values tie for most, each is a mode; an all-equal list has that value as its mode. Python's `statistics.multimode` lists every tied value, and `statistics.mode` returns the first one it meets <Cite id="python-statistics" />.

Boundary cases: the mode is always one of the values; the mean and median need not be. With $n = 1$, or every value equal, all three equal that value. Ordered categories such as T-shirt sizes can have a median, the middle size, when the count is odd or the two middle orders agree, but never a mean.

</PreciseStatement>

## Three questions, three answers

The three centers answer three different questions: what everyone gets from an equal share, what the middle value is, and what value is most common.

### Share, rank or count

The mean shares: it needs numbers you can add, and only it gives the total back. The median ranks: it needs only an order. The mode counts: it needs only to tell values apart, so it works for labels. The [descriptive statistics page](/statistics/descriptive-statistics/) computes each one step by step.

| | Mean | Median | Mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question it answers | Equal share of the total | The middle value | The most common value |
| Needs | Numbers you can add | An order | Values you can compare for equal |
| Works on labels (drinks)? | No | Only if ordered (sizes), and with an even count only if the two middle values agree | Yes |
| Gives back the total? | Yes, n × mean | No | No |
| Always a value someone has? | No (103.6) | Not always when n is even (4.5) | Yes |
| Can there be several? | No | No (but see the even-n interval) | Yes, or none |
| One extreme value | Moves it without limit | Moves it at most to a neighboring value | Usually ignored |
| Best constant guess under | Squared error | Absolute error | Number of misses |
| Cafe, 9 regulars → with millionaire | 4 → 103.6 | 4 → 4.5 | 5 → 5 |
| Python | `statistics.mean` | `statistics.median` | `statistics.multimode` |

### What one extreme value does

One new value $v$ added to $n$ values moves the mean by $(v - \vmu)/(n + 1)$, so the mean follows $v$ without limit. The median moves at most to a neighboring value, or halfway to it, because one new person shifts the sorted line by one place. Data are skewed right when a long tail of large values stretches out on one side, as the millionaire does to the cafe. NIST says the mean "will be pulled in the direction of the skewness" and that the median is not strongly affected by extreme values in the tails <Cite id="nist-location" />. The variance page's [research frontier](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#research-frontier) shows the same split for spread.

<Simpler label="when the median does jump" id="median-jumps-across-a-gap">

At the bus stop, 1, 1, 1, 1, 10, 10, 10 and 10, the two middle values are 1 and 10, so the median is (1 + 10)/2 = 5.5, the same as the mean. One more arrival at minute 10 makes the median 10; one more at minute 1 makes it 1. One value moved it by 4.5, because the middle sat in a gap.

</Simpler>

### When to use which

- **Mean:** when totals matter, such as budgets, and when the data have no far values.
- **Median:** a typical value for skewed data, such as incomes, prices and response times. When it disagrees with the mean, report both: the gap says the data are lopsided.
- **Mode:** categories, such as the most ordered drink, and counts with a clear peak. For measurements that hardly ever repeat, NIST suggests the middle of a histogram's highest bar instead <Cite id="nist-location" />.
- **More than one:** for skewed data, NIST says all three are useful <Cite id="nist-location" />.

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="The median makes the total absolute distance as small as it can be" rigor="full">

1. Sort the values, then pair the smallest with the largest, the second smallest with the second largest, and so on.
2. For one pair $a \le b$, $\lvert a - c \rvert + \lvert b - c \rvert \ge b - a$, with equality exactly when $a \le c \le b$: a detour through $c$ is never shorter.
3. Add over the pairs. The total is at least the sum of the pair widths, and it reaches that sum exactly when $c$ lies inside every pair. The pairs are nested, each inside the one before, so $c$ lies inside every pair exactly when it lies inside the innermost one: between the two middle values. With $n$ odd, the middle value is left unpaired, and its own term $\lvert x_{((n+1)/2)} - c \rvert$ is smallest at $c = x_{((n+1)/2)}$.
4. The cafe with the millionaire: the pairs are (1, 1,000), (2, 7), (3, 5), (4, 5) and (4, 5), whose widths add to $999 + 5 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 1{,}008$. Every $c$ from 4 to 5 gives a total of 1,008: 4, 4.25, 4.5 and 5 all do. $c = 3$ gives 1,012, and the mean, 103.6, gives 1,792.8.

</StepThrough>

The mean's matching property, for squared distances, is proved on the [variance page](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#formal-definition).

<StepThrough title="The mode makes the fewest misses" rigor="full">

1. A guess $c$ misses every value that isn't $c$, so the number of misses is $n$ minus the number of values equal to $c$.
2. That is smallest when $c$ is held by the most values: a mode.
3. The cafe with the millionaire: guessing 5 misses 7 of the 10, guessing 4 misses 8, and guessing the median, 4.5, misses all 10.

</StepThrough>

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="Planning a shift: the mean">

Five delivery times are 20, 22, 25, 28 and 95 minutes, one of them stuck in traffic. The mean is $190/5 = 38$ and the median 25. A courier plans 40 deliveries. The total time is estimated by $40 \times 38 = 1{,}520$ minutes; planning with the median would give 1,000 and run 520 minutes short. The far value is part of the cost, and only the mean carries it.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="A typical salary: the median">

Six salaries, in thousands of dollars a year, are 30, 35, 40, 45, 50 and 400. The mean is $600/6 = 100$ and the median $(40 + 45)/2 = 42.5$. Five of the six earn less than the mean, so "the average salary is 100" describes almost nobody. No value repeats, so there is no mode.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Orders by size and by drink" faded>

Twelve T-shirt orders are S 2, M 5, L 3 and XL 2. Twelve drink orders are coffee 5, tea 4 and juice 3. Find (a) the mode of each; (b) whether each has a median; (c) why neither has a mean.

<div slot="solution">

(a) The mode of the sizes is M, held by 5 of the 12 orders, and the mode of the drinks is coffee. (b) Sizes have an order: sorted, the 6th and 7th orders are both M, so the median is M. Drinks have no order, so they have no median. (c) They are not numbers: averaging codes such as 1, 2, 3 means nothing, because [the codes are a choice](/probability/random-variables-and-distributions/#labels-become-numbers-by-a-choice).

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## The loss you choose picks the center

A loss is the penalty for a wrong guess: squared error charges (y − guess)², absolute error charges |y − guess|, and a miss count charges 1 for every guess that isn't exactly right. When a model is trained to minimize squared error it learns to predict a mean, with absolute error a median, and with the number of misses a mode.

### Squared error predicts the mean, absolute error the median

Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville show that minimizing mean squared error over all functions predicts, for each input $x$, the mean of $y$, and minimizing mean absolute error the median of $y$ (§6.2.1.2, equations (6.14)–(6.16), p. 177) <Cite id="goodfellow2016" />:

$$
\arg\min_{f}\, \mathbb{E}\big[(y - f(x))^2\big] \text{ gives } f^*(x) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid x].
$$

Here $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$ is the mean of $y$ among cases with input $x$. Shrink this to a model with no inputs, the best constant guess for the 10 cafe values. The mean, 103.6, gives a mean squared error of 89,284.04, against 99,104.85 at the median. The median, 4.5, gives a mean absolute error of 100.8, against 179.28 at the mean. So training with squared error on data with far values pulls the model toward them; absolute error does not.

### Accuracy predicts the mode

A classifier scored by accuracy does best by predicting, for each input, the most likely class: the mode of the class distribution given that input. A trained model estimates it with the class it gives the highest probability. The baseline with no features predicts the training set's most common label: scikit-learn's `DummyClassifier` with `strategy="most_frequent"` <Cite id="sklearn-dummyclassifier" />.

### Filling gaps and setting baselines

`SimpleImputer` replaces missing values with a column's `"mean"`, `"median"` or `"most_frequent"` value. The first two need numbers, while `"most_frequent"` works for strings too and, on a tie, returns the smallest value <Cite id="sklearn-simpleimputer" />, but `statistics.mode` keeps whichever comes first, so two tools can fill one tied column differently. `DummyRegressor` always predicts the training set's `"mean"` or `"median"` <Cite id="sklearn-dummyregressor" />. Match the baseline to the metric: for a model scored by mean absolute error, the median baseline is the fair one to beat.

import statistics
from fractions import Fraction
from statistics import multimode


def mean(xs):
    return Fraction(sum(xs), len(xs))


def med(xs):
    """Sort first, then take the middle value or the average of the two middle values."""
    s = sorted(xs)
    n = len(s)
    if n % 2 == 1:
        return Fraction(s[n // 2])
    return Fraction(s[n // 2 - 1] + s[n // 2], 2)


def modes(xs):
    """This site's rule: no mode when two or more different values all tie."""
    found = multimode(xs)
    return [] if 1 < len(found) == len(set(xs)) else found


def sse(xs, c):
    return sum(((x - c) ** 2 for x in xs), Fraction(0))


def sae(xs, c):
    return sum((abs(x - c) for x in xs), Fraction(0))


def misses(xs, c):
    return sum(x != c for x in xs)


# The nine regulars, savings in thousands of dollars.
R = [1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 7]
assert sum(R) == 36 and mean(R) == 4 and med(R) == 4 and statistics.median(R) == 4
assert modes(R) == [5] and R.count(5) == 3

# The millionaire walks in.

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="The list 2, 3, 3, 8, 9 has a typo: 9 becomes 90. Give the three centers before and after.">
Before: mean 25/5 = 5, median 3, mode 3. After: mean 106/5 = 21.2, median 3, mode 3. The typo moved only the mean, by (90 − 9)/5 = 16.2.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="On the board, a neighbor with 6 moved the median further than the mean. Does that contradict 'the median resists extreme values'?">
No. 6 is not extreme. The median moved half a step because the list grew by one, and half a step is the most any newcomer could move it here. The mean moved 0.2 for the neighbor but 99.6 for the millionaire.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that for the list 1, 2, 3, 4 every c from 2 to 3 gives the same total absolute distance, and find it.">
For $2 \le c \le 3$, the total is $(c - 1) + (c - 2) + (3 - c) + (4 - c) = 4$, since the $c$ terms cancel. At $c = 1$ or $c = 4$ the total is 6. This is the even-count interval from the precise statement under Notation: every point between the two middle values minimizes the total.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="The median is the value in the middle of the list.">
    It is the middle of the *sorted* list. Of 518 secondary students, 12.4% and 10% took the median of unsorted data in two questions (Table 1, p. 76) <Cite id="mayen2009" />.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The mean is always the best single number.">
    With a far value it can sit above almost everyone: 5 of the 6 salaries in worked example 2. In the same study, 236 of the 518 students (45.6%) called the mean adequate for data with an outlier, against 159 (30.7%) who chose the median (Table 2, p. 76) <Cite id="mayen2009" />.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Under right skew the order is always mode, median, mean.">
    The cafe with the millionaire is skewed far right, yet its mode, 5, is above its median, 4.5. Von Hippel found the rule that right skew puts the mean above the median in 14 of the 18 introductory textbooks he examined (§1) <Cite id="vonhippel2005" />. Five of them extend it to the mode, one saying the order is "always from mode, to median, to mean" (§1) <Cite id="vonhippel2005" />. Even the mean–median part often fails for discrete data (§1; examples in §2) <Cite id="vonhippel2005" />.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The mode is how often the most common value appears.">
    The mode is the value, 5. When the board says "Mode: 5, held by 3 customers", 3 is its count. Mixing up frequencies and values is among the conflicts students show in the median study (pp. 75–76) <Cite id="mayen2009" />.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Every list has exactly one mode.">
    At 4 the board says "Mode: 4 and 5, each held by 3 customers." Python's `statistics.mode` returns only the first one it meets <Cite id="python-statistics" />.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
