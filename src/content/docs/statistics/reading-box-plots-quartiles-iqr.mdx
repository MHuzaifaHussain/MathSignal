## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Four equal stretches are not four equal groups: the children at the cuts each sit on a boundary and belong to two stretches. With 10 children a cut can fall between two children, so a quartile can be a height nobody has.">

Line up our class of 9 children from shortest (122 cm) to tallest (148 cm). From the first child to the last there are 8 steps between neighbors. Now cut those 8 steps into four equal stretches. The children standing at the three cuts mark the quartiles: one a quarter of the way along, one halfway, one three quarters of the way. The middle two stretches hold the middle half of the class, and that part of the line is the box of a box plot.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Model servers are judged by latency percentiles such as the median and the 99th percentile, because a few very slow requests make the average misleading. Feature scaling can use the median and the interquartile range (IQR) in place of the mean and the standard deviation. Box plots compare results across seeds, folds or groups at a glance.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Cut the line into quarters

To find the quartiles, line the values up from smallest to largest and cut the steps between them into four equal stretches. Before you press Quartiles, decide: does the 2nd, the 3rd or the 4th shortest child mark the first quarter? Going further: suppose the tallest child were 162 cm instead of 148 cm. Which of Q1, the median, Q3 and the IQR (Q3 − Q1, the width of the box) would change, and would the tallest child still sit at the end of a whisker?

Your task: say which places in line (1st to 9th) mark the three quartiles, then read the box plot's five numbers: the shortest, Q1, the median, Q3 and the tallest.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["data-value", "sample-size", "order-statistic", "quantile-level", "quantile-position", "quantile", "median", "first-quartile", "third-quartile", "iqr", "fences", "scalar"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be real numbers, $n \ge 1$, sorted as $x_{(1)} \le \cdots \le x_{(n)}$. For $0 \le p \le 1$ let $h = (n-1)p$, $j = \lfloor h \rfloor$ and $g = h - j$. Then

$$
Q(p) = x_{(j+1)} + g\,\big(x_{(j+2)} - x_{(j+1)}\big),
$$

read as $Q(p) = x_{(j+1)}$ when $g = 0$. The quartiles are $Q_1 = Q(1/4)$, $\operatorname{median} = Q(1/2)$ and $Q_3 = Q(3/4)$, the interquartile range is $\operatorname{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$, and the 100p-th percentile is $Q(p)$.

A box plot draws a box from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ with a line at the median, fences at $Q_1 - 1.5\,\operatorname{IQR}$ and $Q_3 + 1.5\,\operatorname{IQR}$, whiskers to the smallest and largest values on or inside the fences, and every value strictly past a fence as a dot.

Convention: NumPy's default linear method, Hyndman and Fan's type 7. Boundary cases: for $n = 1$, $Q_1 = Q_3 = x_1$ and the IQR is 0; if $Q_1 = Q_3$, both fences sit on $Q_1$ and every value different from $Q_1$ is a dot; a value exactly on a fence is not a dot.

</PreciseStatement>

## Five steps from a list to its box plot

Sort, find the quartiles, take the IQR, set the fences, then draw the whiskers. On our class:

1. **Sort.** 122, 125, 128, 131, 133, 137, 140, 142, 148.
2. **Quartiles.** 9 values have 8 steps, so the cuts sit $h = (n-1)p$ steps past the first value: 8 × 1/4 = 2, 8 × 1/2 = 4 and 8 × 3/4 = 6, the 3rd, 5th and 7th values. Q1 = 128, the median is 133 and Q3 = 140.
3. **IQR.** 140 − 128 = 12 cm.
4. **Fences.** 1.5 × 12 = 18, so 128 − 18 = 110 cm and 140 + 18 = 158 cm.
5. **Whiskers.** Every value lies inside the fences, so the whiskers reach 122 and 148 cm and there are no dots.

NIST's handbook describes the same parts: a box over the middle 50% of the data, fences 1.5 IQRs beyond it, and points past them drawn separately <Cite id="nist-boxplot" />.

### When the cut falls between two values

When $(n - 1)p$ is not a whole number, take the value just below the cut and move toward the next value by the leftover fraction of the gap. The 90th percentile is the value 90% of the way along the line. For our class $h = 8 \times 0.9 = 7.2$: 7 whole steps past the first value is the 8th value, 142; then move 0.2 of the 6 cm gap up from 142 toward 148: 142 + 1.2 = 143.2 cm. The 10th percentile has $h = 0.8$, so it is 122 + 0.8 × 3 = 124.4 cm.

<Simpler label="the same idea on a ruler" id="between-two-values">

Lay 142 and 148 on a ruler: they are 6 cm apart, and 0.2 of 6 cm is 1.2 cm, so a cut 0.2 of the way along sits at 143.2. A cut exactly halfway is the plain average, which is how an even-sized list gets its median.

</Simpler>

### Reading a box plot you didn't draw

Each of the four parts (lower whisker, lower half-box, upper half-box, upper whisker) holds about a quarter of the values, so a longer part means more spread, not more values. In our class the median sits 5 cm above Q1 and 7 cm below Q3, so the upper half of the box is more spread out. With only 9 children the quarters are rough: the children at Q1, the median and Q3 each sit on a boundary, counting each boundary child in both parts it touches, each part holds 3 children and the box 5.

A dot is a value past a fence: drawn, not deleted, and not proven wrong. Wickham and Stryjewski trace the plot to Tukey and note that it suits comparing distributions across groups <Cite id="wickham2011" />.

### Which quartile? Tools disagree

There is no single agreed formula for quartiles, so two tools can report different values for the same list (see [library defaults in the descriptive statistics cheat sheet](/statistics/descriptive-statistics-cheat-sheet/#library-defaults-that-change-the-answer)); this page uses NumPy's default. NIST's handbook says no interpolation is universally accepted, and its own default differs from NumPy's <Cite id="nist-percentiles" />. Hyndman and Fan found packages using one definition for a quantile and another for the box plot <Cite id="hyndman1996" />. NumPy numbers nine of its methods as Hyndman and Fan do, type 1 to type 9; its default, `linear`, is type 7 <Cite id="numpy-quantile" />. Tukey's hinges take Q1 and Q3 as the medians of the lower and upper halves of the sorted list, each half including the middle value when n is odd.

| Method | Q1 | Q3 | IQR | Upper fence | 162 cm drawn as a dot? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NumPy default, `linear` (type 7); this site | 128 | 140 | 12 | 158 | yes |
| Tukey's hinges | 128 | 140 | 12 | 158 | yes |
| NIST default, `weibull` (type 6) | 126.5 | 141 | 14.5 | 162.75 | no |

The same child is a dot under one method and not under another, so write the method down with the number. Type 7 is also R's default <Cite id="nist-percentiles" />. pandas' `describe()` does not name its method, but its documented example, a column 1, 2, 3 with 25% at 1.5 and 75% at 2.5, matches NumPy's default <Cite id="pandas-describe" />.

<SideNote>
Hyndman and Fan recommend a different estimator, the median-unbiased one <Cite id="hyndman1996" />, which NumPy lists as method 8, `median_unbiased` <Cite id="numpy-quantile" />.
</SideNote>

<GoDeeper label="when Tukey's hinges and NumPy agree" id="hinges-vs-linear">

R's documentation says the hinges equal the quartiles for odd n and can differ for even n <Cite id="r-boxplot-stats" />. The second proof below covers odd n.

</GoDeeper>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="Why 4k + 1 values put every quartile on a value" rigor="full">

1. Let $n = 4k + 1$, so $n - 1 = 4k$.
2. Then $h = k$, $2k$ and $3k$ for $p = \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34$, all whole, so $g = 0$.
3. So $Q_1 = x_{(k+1)}$, $\operatorname{median} = x_{(2k+1)}$ and $Q_3 = x_{(3k+1)}$. Our class has $k = 2$: the 3rd, 5th and 7th child. Thirteen values have $k = 3$: the 4th, 7th and 10th.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="For odd n, the hinges are NumPy's quartiles" rigor="full">

1. Let $n = 2m + 1$. The lower half with the median is $x_{(1)}, \ldots, x_{(m+1)}$, which has $m + 1$ values.
2. Its median, the lower hinge, is $x_{((m+2)/2)}$ when $m$ is even, and halfway between $x_{((m+1)/2)}$ and $x_{((m+3)/2)}$ when $m$ is odd.
3. The linear method has $h = (n - 1)/4 = m/2$. If $m$ is even, $g = 0$ and $Q_1 = x_{(m/2+1)} = x_{((m+2)/2)}$. If $m$ is odd, $j = (m-1)/2$ and $g = 1/2$, so $Q_1$ is halfway between $x_{((m+1)/2)}$ and $x_{((m+3)/2)}$.
4. They agree, and the upper half is the mirror image. Our class has $m = 4$: position 3 both ways.

</StepThrough>

| Property | Statement | Our class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordered | $Q_1 \le \operatorname{median} \le Q_3$ and $\operatorname{IQR} \ge 0$ | 128 ≤ 133 ≤ 140 |
| Shift | Adding the same number to every value adds it to every quartile; the IQR stays | +10 cm: Q1 138, IQR 12 |
| Scale | Multiplying by $c$ multiplies the IQR by $\lvert c \rvert$; for $c < 0$, $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ swap roles | × (−1): quartiles −140 and −128, IQR 12 |
| Resistant | If $n \ge 5$, moving the largest value further out leaves $Q_1$, $Q_3$ and the fences unchanged | 148 → 162: still 128 and 140 |
| Lands on values | If $n = 4k + 1$, every quartile is a value in the list | 3rd, 5th, 7th |

</FormalDefinition>

## Where quartiles show up in machine learning

### Latency percentiles for model servers

Model servers are monitored by percentiles such as p50 and p99 rather than by the average. Masson, Rim and Lee describe web latencies as heavily skewed: in their example the average sits closer to the 75th percentile than to the median <Cite id="masson2019" />. Exact quantiles over a stream need every value, so monitors keep a sketch, a small summary that answers quantile questions approximately. A sketch accurate in rank can be far off in value: for their 2 million requests, a rank accuracy of 0.005 lets a p99 answer land anywhere from the 98.5th to the 99.5th percentile, from 2 to 20 seconds <Cite id="masson2019" />. So their DDSketch bounds the relative error of the value instead <Cite id="masson2019" />.

### Robust feature scaling with the IQR

scikit-learn's `RobustScaler` subtracts the median and divides by a quantile range, by default from the 25th to the 75th percentile, the IQR. Its documentation gives the reason: outliers can often influence the sample mean and variance in a negative way <Cite id="sklearn-robustscaler" />. The linear quartiles reproduce its documented example exactly, and NIST's type 6 would not, as the code below checks. [Standardization vs min-max scaling](/statistics/standardization-vs-min-max-scaling/) compares the scalers.

## Check every quartile in code

This program uses exact fractions and stops with an error if any number on this page is off.

<PyRunner
  title="quartiles.py"
  code={`
import math
from fractions import Fraction


def quantile(values, p):
    """NumPy's default linear method (Hyndman and Fan type 7), exactly."""
    if not values:
        raise ValueError("a quantile needs at least one value")
    p = Fraction(p)
    if p < 0 or p > 1:
        raise ValueError("p must be between 0 and 1")
    xs = sorted(Fraction(v) for v in values)
    h = (len(xs) - 1) * p
    j = math.floor(h)
    g = h - j
    if g == 0:
        return xs[j]
    return xs[j] + g * (xs[j + 1] - xs[j])


def quantile_type6(values, p):
    """NIST's default (type 6): position p(n + 1), clamped to the ends."""
    xs = sorted(Fraction(v) for v in values)
    position = Fraction(p) * (len(xs) + 1)
    if position <= 1:
        return xs[0]
    if position >= len(xs):
        return xs[-1]
    k = math.floor(position)
    d = position - k
    return xs[k - 1] + d * (xs[k] - xs[k - 1])


def median_of(xs):
    xs = sorted(xs)
    middle = len(xs) // 2
    if len(xs) % 2 == 1:
        return Fraction(xs[middle])
    return Fraction(xs[middle - 1] + xs[middle], 2)


def hinges(values):
    """Medians of the lower and upper halves, each including the middle value when n is odd."""
    xs = sorted(values)
    half = (len(xs) + 1) // 2
    return median_of(xs[:half]), median_of(xs[len(xs) - half:])


def box_plot(values, q=quantile):
    q1, middle, q3 = q(values, Fraction(1, 4)), q(values, Fraction(1, 2)), q(values, Fraction(3, 4))
    iqr = q3 - q1
    low_fence, high_fence = q1 - Fraction(3, 2) * iqr, q3 + Fraction(3, 2) * iqr
    inside = [v for v in values if low_fence <= v <= high_fence]
    dots = sorted(v for v in values if v < low_fence or v > high_fence)
    return dict(q1=q1, median=middle, q3=q3, iqr=iqr, low_fence=low_fence, high_fence=high_fence,
                low_whisker=min(inside), high_whisker=max(inside), dots=dots)


# Our class, in the order the children arrived.
heights = [133, 122, 140, 128, 148, 125, 137, 131, 142]
in_line = sorted(heights)
assert in_line == [122, 125, 128, 131, 133, 137, 140, 142, 148]
box = box_plot(heights)
assert (box["q1"], box["median"], box["q3"], box["iqr"]) == (128, 133, 140, 12)
assert (box["low_fence"], box["high_fence"]) == (110, 158)
assert (box["low_whisker"], box["high_whisker"], box["dots"]) == (122, 148, [])
assert (in_line[2], in_line[4], in_line[6]) == (128, 133, 140)
assert [heights.index(v) + 1 for v in (128, 133, 140)] == [4, 1, 3]
assert box["median"] - box["q1"] == 5 and box["q3"] - box["median"] == 7
assert (heights[2], heights[6], heights[6] - heights[2]) == (140, 137, -3)
print("our class: Q1", box["q1"], " median", box["median"], " Q3", box["q3"], " IQR", box["iqr"],
      " fences", box["low_fence"], "and", box["high_fence"], " whiskers", box["low_whisker"], "and", box["high_whisker"])

# Percentiles between two values.
assert quantile(heights, Fraction(9, 10)) == Fraction(716, 5) == Fraction(1432, 10)
assert quantile(heights, Fraction(1, 10)) == Fraction(622, 5) == Fraction(1244, 10)

# One very tall child: only the top end changes.
tall = [162 if v == 148 else v for v in heights]
tall_box = box_plot(tall)
for key in ("q1", "median", "q3", "iqr", "low_fence", "high_fence"):
    assert tall_box[key] == box[key]
assert tall_box["dots"] == [162] and tall_box["high_whisker"] == 142

# Resistant only from n = 5: for n = 2 to 4, Q3 interpolates toward the largest value.
assert quantile([1, 2, 3, 4], Fraction(3, 4)) == Fraction(13, 4)
assert quantile([1, 2, 3, 100], Fraction(3, 4)) == Fraction(109, 4)
for n in range(5, 30):
    xs = [k * k for k in range(n)]
    pushed = xs[:-1] + [xs[-1] + 1000]
    for p in (Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(3, 4)):
        assert quantile(pushed, p) == quantile(xs, p), n

# Three methods on our class.
assert hinges(heights) == (128, 140)
nist = box_plot(heights, quantile_type6)

# Ten children: our class plus a 136 cm child.

# Odd n: the hinges equal the linear quartiles. Unevenly spaced lists, so it is not luck.
for n in range(1, 42, 2):

    assert hinges(xs) != (quantile(xs, Fraction(1, 4)), quantile(xs, Fraction(3, 4))), n

# n = 4k + 1: every quartile is a value in the list.
for k in range(1, 11):
    xs = list(reversed(range(4 * k + 1)))
    for p in (Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(3, 4)):
        assert quantile(xs, p) in xs

assert (hw["low_fence"], hw["high_fence"], hw["dots"]) == (-15, 65, [90])
assert (hw["low_whisker"], hw["high_whisker"]) == (5, 45)

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Every child grows 5 cm over the summer. What happens to Q1, the median, Q3 and the IQR?">
The quartiles each rise by 5 cm, to 133, 138 and 145 cm, and the IQR stays 12 cm. Every value moved by the same amount, so the box slides along without changing width.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Find Q1, Q3 and the IQR of 3, 7, 8, 12, 13, and say whether any value is drawn as a dot.">
Five values have 4 steps, so Q1 is the 2nd value, 7, the median is 8 and Q3 is the 4th, 12. The IQR is 5, and the fences sit 7.5 beyond the box, at −0.5 and 19.5. Every value is inside, so there are no dots.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A report gives Q1 = 126.5 cm and Q3 = 141 cm for our class. Is it wrong?">
No. Those are NIST's default, type 6, which NumPy calls `method='weibull'`. Ask for the method before comparing them with this page's 128 and 140 cm.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that multiplying every value by a negative number c multiplies the IQR by |c|.">
Multiplying by $c < 0$ reverses the sorted order, so the value $(n-1)/4$ steps from the bottom of the new list is $c$ times the value $(n-1)/4$ steps from the top of the old one. So the new $Q_1$ is $cQ_3$ and the new $Q_3$ is $cQ_1$, and the new IQR is $cQ_1 - cQ_3 = \lvert c \rvert (Q_3 - Q_1)$. Our class times −1 has quartiles −140 and −128, still 12 apart.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Find the quartiles by counting along the list as it was given.">
    Sort first. Arrival order picks 140 and 137 cm in our class, an IQR of −3 cm.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A longer part of a box plot holds more values.">
    Every part holds about a quarter of the values; length shows spread. Our class's half-boxes are 5 and 7 cm wide and hold 3 children each.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The whiskers always reach the smallest and largest values.">
    They stop at the last values on or inside the fences <Cite id="r-boxplot-stats" />. With the tall child the whisker stops at 142 cm and 162 cm is a dot.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Quartiles have one correct value.">
    Methods differ. NumPy's default gives our class an IQR of 12 cm, NIST's 14.5 cm, and only the first draws 162 cm as a dot.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A dot on a box plot is a mistake to delete.">
    It is a value past a fence. Calling it an error needs a reason from outside the plot, such as a typo.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
