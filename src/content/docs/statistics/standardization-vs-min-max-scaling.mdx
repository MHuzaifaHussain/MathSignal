## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="The ruler hangs on just two children, so one bad day stretches it for everyone. The typical step uses every child, but one far-off child still lengthens it. A new child above the old top mark lands past the end of the ruler.">

Ana's class sat two tests, one marked out of 50 and one out of 80. Her teacher wants to know which test went better for Ana, compared with the rest of the class.

The first picture is a stretchy ruler. Pin one end at the lowest mark in the class and the other at the highest. Ana's place is a share of the way along: 0 at the bottom pin, 1 at the top. Do this for both tests and compare the two shares.

The second picture is a typical step. Stand at the class average and count how far above it Ana is in steps, where one step is how far a typical classmate stands from the average on that test.

The stretchy ruler is called min-max scaling; the typical-step count is a z-score, and turning every value into one is called standardization. The two pictures can give different answers, and the board below shows when.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Models that measure distances between examples, or that learn by gradient descent, see raw numbers, not units. A house's area in square meters runs in the hundreds and its bedroom count stays under 10, so the area decides almost every distance and stretches the model's error (its loss) into a long, narrow valley. Rescaling fixes that, but the choice matters: a comparison of 5 scaling techniques on 82 datasets found that an inadequate one can do more harm than not scaling at all (§7) <Cite id="deamorim2022" />.
</WhyThisMatters>

**It depends on the yardstick.** On Raw marks the board says "Ana scored 36 out of 50 on test A (72%) and 58 out of 80 on test B (72.5%)." Percentages compare her with the top possible mark, not with her classmates.

On Min–max (0 to 1): "Test A: (36 − 21) / (39 − 21) = 15/18 = 5/6 ≈ 0.83. Test B: (58 − 38) / (64 − 38) = 20/26 = 10/13 ≈ 0.77. Min–max says test A." Min-max only asks where Ana sits between the lowest and highest mark: 3 below the top on A, 6 below it on B.

On z-scores: "Test A: (36 − 30) / 6 = 1. Test B: (58 − 50) / 8 = 1. Ana is exactly one standard deviation above the mean on both." So the two scales already disagree: min-max measures against the two extreme classmates, z-scores against the whole class.

**One very low mark.** With a scale showing, the preset is announced as "One mark of 10 moved both scores for test B: min–max rose from ≈ 0.77 to 48/54 = 8/9 ≈ 0.89, the z-score fell from 1 to ≈ 0.76. Ana's 58 did not change." Min-max now says test B, and z says test A. Min-max rose because the bottom pin moved down 28 marks. Two changes pulled the z-score opposite ways. The mean dropped to 422/9 ≈ 46.89, so Ana now stands 100/9 ≈ 11.11 marks above it instead of 8, about 1.39 times as far. But the standard deviation, <Var name="sigma" />, grew from 8 to ≈ 14.70, about 1.84 times as large. The bigger step wins, so her z-score falls. Neither scale is protected from a single extreme value.

## What each scale pins down

Standardization pins the mean at 0 and the standard deviation at 1; min-max scaling pins the smallest value at 0 and the largest at 1.

|  | Standardization (z-scores) | Min-max scaling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subtracts | the mean | the smallest value |
| Divides by | the standard deviation | the range, largest − smallest |
| Uses | every value | two values |
| After scaling | mean 0, standard deviation 1, no fixed ends | smallest 0, largest 1 |
| Test A after scaling | mean 0, standard deviation 1 | mean 1/2, standard deviation 1/3 |
| Test B after scaling | mean 0, standard deviation 1 | mean 6/13, standard deviation 4/13 |
| One low mark on B (38 → 10) | Ana 1 → ≈ 0.76 | Ana 10/13 → 8/9 |
| A new student's 70 on B, using the nine marks' mean, σ and ends | 5/2 | 16/13, past 1 |
| scikit-learn | `StandardScaler` | `MinMaxScaler` |
| Good default for | features without fixed bounds, roughly bell-shaped, and most models trained by gradient descent | features with known, fixed bounds and few outliers (pixel values 0–255, ages 0–100) |

### Min-max equalizes the ends, not the spread

After min-max scaling both tests run from 0 to 1, but every distance is divided by the range, so the new standard deviation is the old one over the range: 6/18 = 1/3 on test A and 8/26 = 4/13 on test B. de Amorim and coauthors note that without outliers min-max has an effect similar to standardization, but with outliers it fails to equalize the means and variances (§2.2) <Cite id="deamorim2022" />. Standardization makes every <Var name="sigma" /> exactly 1. Both keep the order within a test, so they disagree only across tests or features.

### One extreme value moves both

scikit-learn's comparison of scalers on California housing data finds that "Both StandardScaler and MinMaxScaler are very sensitive to the presence of outliers", and shows min-max squeezing the ordinary values of average house occupancy into the narrow range [0, 0.005] <Cite id="sklearn-scaler-outliers" />. The `StandardScaler` page itself warns that it is sensitive to outliers and that features may then scale differently from each other <Cite id="sklearn-standardscaler" />.

### When neither fits: the median and the IQR

A third scaler subtracts the median and divides by the interquartile range (IQR), so one extreme value can't move it. scikit-learn's `RobustScaler` does this, with the IQR running from the 25th to the 75th percentile by default <Cite id="sklearn-robustscaler" />. With NumPy's default quartiles (the [box plot page](/statistics/reading-box-plots-quartiles-iqr/) shows how they're found), test B has lower quartile 44, median 49 and upper quartile 56, so the IQR is 12 and Ana scores (58 − 49)/12 = 3/4, with the 38 or with the 10. On test A the quartiles are 24, 30 and 36, so she scores (36 − 30)/12 = 1/2. This third yardstick says test B. The extreme mark itself stays in the scaled data <Cite id="sklearn-scaler-outliers" />.

### When to use which

- **Standardization** for most features, especially for models that compare distances or train by gradient descent. Google's Machine Learning Crash Course calls Z-score scaling typically a better choice than linear scaling, its name for min-max, and a good one when the data look roughly normal <Cite id="google-mlcc-normalization" />.
- **Min-max** when the feature has fixed, known bounds and few outliers, such as age, bounded at about 0 and 100 in the crash course's example <Cite id="google-mlcc-normalization" />, or when a model needs inputs in [0, 1].
- **Median and IQR** when a few extreme values would otherwise set the scale.
- **No scaling** for decision trees and ensembles built on them, which de Amorim and coauthors found virtually insensitive to the choice (§7) <Cite id="deamorim2022" />.

Take 2, 4 and 9: mean 5, standard deviation √(26/3) ≈ 2.94. Min-max gives 0, 2/7 and 1; z-scores give about −1.02, −0.34 and 1.36. If the 9 becomes 30, min-max gives 0, 1/14 and 1: the 4 slides toward 0. z-scores move too: the mean becomes 12 and the 4 lands at about −0.63.

</Simpler>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["data-value", "sample-size", "mu", "sigma", "z", "list-min", "list-max", "min-max-value"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be real numbers.

**Standardization.** With $\vmu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$ and $\vsigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \vmu)^2$, and provided $\vsigma > 0$, the z-scores are

$$
z_i = \frac{x_i - \vmu}{\vsigma}.
$$

They have mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and no fixed bounds, although with $n$ values no $|z_i|$ can exceed $\sqrt{n - 1}$ ([how far a z-score can go](/statistics/outlier-detection-z-score-vs-iqr/#most-z-can-reach)).

**Min-max scaling.** Provided $x_{\max} > x_{\min}$,

$$
x_i' = \frac{x_i - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}},
$$

so the smallest $x_i'$ is 0 and the largest is 1. For $[a, b]$ instead, use $a + (b - a)\,x_i'$, as `MinMaxScaler`'s `feature_range` does <Cite id="sklearn-minmaxscaler" />; $[-1, 1]$ is common (§2.2, equation (7)) <Cite id="deamorim2022" />.

Boundary cases. If every value is the same, both formulas divide by 0. A new value uses the stored $\vmu$, $\vsigma$, $x_{\min}$ and $x_{\max}$, so its $x'$ can fall outside $[0, 1]$. Here $\vsigma$ divides by $n$, like `StandardScaler`, equivalent to `numpy.std(x, ddof=0)` <Cite id="sklearn-standardscaler" />; the variance page explains [the n − 1 version](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#two-versions-the-whole-group-or-a-sample).

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="Both scales ignore the units and keep the order" rigor="full">

1. Both maps are $x \mapsto (x - a)/b$ with $b > 0$: $a = \vmu$, $b = \vsigma$ for standardization; $a = x_{\min}$, $b = x_{\max} - x_{\min}$ for min-max.
2. Change units by $y_i = c\,x_i + d$ with $c > 0$; percentages on test A use $c = 2$. Then $\vmu_y = c\,\vmu + d$ and $\vsigma_y = c\,\vsigma$ ([shifting and stretching](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#shifting-and-stretching-the-whole-class)), and $y_{\min} = c\,x_{\min} + d$, $y_{\max} = c\,x_{\max} + d$.
3. In $(y_i - a_y)/b_y$ the $d$ cancels in the difference and the $c$ in the ratio, so both scores are unchanged.
4. Because $b > 0$, the order is kept. Every gap between two values is divided by the same $b$, so a histogram keeps its shape and only slides and stretches; a one-to-one map that is not a shift and stretch, such as a log, keeps the order but changes the shape.
5. If $c < 0$, every $z$ flips sign and $x'$ becomes $1 - x'$.

</StepThrough>

So the two scales differ only in which reference points they use, never in how they treat units.

</FormalDefinition>

## Scaling features for machine learning

Scaling matters for models that compare distances or learn by gradient descent, and it must be fitted on the training data alone.

### Distances let the biggest unit win

Take three houses as (area, bedrooms): P = (100, 3), Q = (102, 2) and R = (110, 3). Which is nearest to P? Raw squared distances give P to Q $2^2 + 1^2 = 5$ and P to R $10^2 = 100$, so Q, though it has a bedroom fewer. Divide by the four houses' standard deviations, 25 m² and 1 bedroom, and they become $629/625$ and $100/625$: now R is nearest. Min-max with ranges 70 and 2 gives 1,229/4,900 against 100/4,900, also R. scikit-learn warns that a feature with a far larger variance might dominate the objective function (§8.3.1) <Cite id="sklearn-preprocessing" />.

### Gradient descent on a stretched valley

Scaling changes the shape of the loss, and with it the speed of gradient descent (its own page is coming soon). A linear model gives each feature a weight, a number it learns. With the four houses' features centered, the error curves 625 times more steeply as you change the area's weight than as you change the bedrooms' weight. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville explain why that hurts: a step small enough not to overshoot in the steep direction is usually too small to make progress in the shallow one (§4.3.1, Figure 4.6) <Cite id="goodfellow2016numerical" />. That ratio of steepest to shallowest curvature is called the condition number (§4.2, equation (4.2)) <Cite id="goodfellow2016numerical" />.

Steps until the error along the shallow direction is down to 1% of where it started, with each step sized to settle the steep direction in one move:

| Scaling | Curvature ratio | Steps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| raw | 625 | 2,876 |
| min-max | 49/25 = 1.96 | 7 |
| standardized | 1 | 1 |

Min-max helps but not fully: the area's range is 2.8 of its standard deviations, the bedrooms' 2 of theirs. Curvature grows with the square of a feature's spread, so the ratio is $(2.8/2)^2 = 1.96$. The step counts are this page's own derivation, checked in the code below.

<GoDeeper label="where 625, 49/25 and 1 come from" id="conditioning-derivation">

<StepThrough title="Curvature ratios on the four houses" rigor="sketch">

1. For the loss $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - w_1 a_i - w_2 b_i)^2$ on centered features $a$ (area) and $b$ (bedrooms), the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, is $\frac{2}{n}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$, where row $i$ of $\mathbf{X}$ is $(a_i, b_i)$.
2. Here $\sum_i a_i b_i = 0$, so it is diagonal, with entries $2\vsigma_a^2$ and $2\vsigma_b^2$. The condition number is the larger over the smaller: $625$ raw, $(1/4)/(25/196) = 49/25$ after min-max, $1$ standardized.
3. With step size 1 over the largest curvature (stable up to twice that), each step multiplies the shallow error by $1 - 1/\kappa$, with $\kappa$ the condition number: $624/625$, $24/49$ and 0.
4. Standardizing can't fix correlated features: with correlation $r = 3/5$ the curvatures are proportional to $1 \pm r$, so $\kappa = (8/5)/(2/5) = 4$. Nor uncentered inputs: min-max leaves each mean somewhere between 0 and 1 (1/2 for both house features), not at 0, which ties each weight to the intercept.

</StepThrough>

</GoDeeper>

### Fit on the training data, then reuse it

`StandardScaler` computes the mean and standard deviation on the training samples and stores them for `transform` <Cite id="sklearn-standardscaler" />. scikit-learn's pitfalls guide says "never call fit on the test data" (§12.2) and transforms test data the same way as training data, ideally in a `Pipeline` (§12.1) <Cite id="sklearn-common-pitfalls" />. de Amorim and coauthors scaled each cross-validation fold with parameters from its training part only (§4.2) <Cite id="deamorim2022" />. With the board's numbers, a new student's 70 on test B gets $z = (70 - 50)/8 = 5/2$ and a min-max score of $(70 - 38)/26 = 16/13 \approx 1.23$, which `MinMaxScaler` leaves past 1 unless `clip=True` <Cite id="sklearn-minmaxscaler" />.

## Compare the scalers in code

This runs in your browser with exact fractions and stops with an error if any number on this page is off.

<PyRunner
  title="scaling_compared.py"
  code={`
import math
from fractions import Fraction

import numpy as np

# Every list is fixed, so nothing here is random and no seed is needed.


def mean(xs):
    return Fraction(sum(xs), len(xs))


def variance(xs):
    """Divide by n, as np.std and StandardScaler do."""
    mu = mean(xs)
    return sum(((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs), Fraction(0)) / len(xs)


def min_max(x, xs):
    """Share of the way from the smallest value in xs to the largest."""
    low, high = min(xs), max(xs)
    if high == low:
        raise ValueError("every value is the same: the range is 0")
    return Fraction(x - low, high - low)


def z_score(x, xs):
    """Exact z when the variance is a perfect square, as on both tests."""
    mu, var = mean(xs), variance(xs)
    sigma = Fraction(math.isqrt(var.numerator), math.isqrt(var.denominator))
    assert sigma * sigma == var, "sigma is not a whole fraction here"
    return (x - mu) / sigma


A = [21, 24, 24, 27, 30, 33, 36, 36, 39]  # out of 50
B = [38, 41, 44, 47, 49, 53, 56, 58, 64]  # out of 80
B_low = [10] + B[1:]  # one classmate's 38 becomes a 10
assert A[6] == 36 and B[7] == 58 and B_low[7] == 58

# Raw marks: percentages favor B by half a point.
assert Fraction(36 * 100, 50) == 72 and Fraction(58 * 100, 80) == Fraction(145, 2)

# Means and standard deviations, dividing by n.
assert mean(A) == 30 and variance(A) == 36 and mean(B) == 50 and variance(B) == 64
assert np.std(A) == 6.0 and np.std(B) == 8.0

# The board's Min-max readout, then its z-scores readout.
assert min_max(36, A) == Fraction(15, 18) == Fraction(5, 6)
assert min_max(58, B) == Fraction(20, 26) == Fraction(10, 13)
assert Fraction(5, 6) > Fraction(10, 13)  # min-max says test A
assert z_score(36, A) == 1 and z_score(58, B) == 1  # z says equally well
print("min-max: A", min_max(36, A), " B", min_max(58, B), "   z: A", z_score(36, A), " B", z_score(58, B))

# After scaling: same ends, different spreads for min-max; mean 0 and sigma 1 for z.
mm_A = [min_max(x, A) for x in A]
mm_B = [min_max(x, B) for x in B]
assert min(mm_A) == min(mm_B) == 0 and max(mm_A) == max(mm_B) == 1
assert mean(mm_A) == Fraction(1, 2) and variance(mm_A) == Fraction(1, 9)  # sigma 1/3
assert mean(mm_B) == Fraction(6, 13) and variance(mm_B) == Fraction(16, 169)  # sigma 4/13
for xs in (A, B):
    zs = [z_score(x, xs) for x in xs]
    assert mean(zs) == 0 and variance(zs) == 1
    assert sorted(zs) == zs and sorted(xs) == xs  # the order is kept
# With n values no |z| can pass the square root of n - 1, here 8: check it on every list.
for xs in (A, B, B_low):
    assert max((x - mean(xs)) ** 2 / variance(xs) for x in xs) <= len(xs) - 1

# One very low mark on B: 38 becomes 10.
mu_low, var_low = mean(B_low), variance(B_low)
assert mu_low == Fraction(422, 9) and var_low == Fraction(17504, 81)
assert 58 - mu_low == Fraction(100, 9)  # Ana now stands 100/9 above the mean, not 8
z_low_squared = (58 - mu_low) ** 2 / var_low
assert z_low_squared == Fraction(625, 1094)
z_low = math.sqrt(z_low_squared)
assert abs(z_low - 0.7558) < 5e-4 and abs(math.sqrt(var_low) - 14.7003) < 5e-4
assert abs(float(mu_low) - 46.89) < 5e-3 and abs(float(Fraction(100, 9)) - 11.11) < 5e-3
assert abs(float(Fraction(100, 9) / 8) - 1.39) < 5e-3 and abs(math.sqrt(var_low) / 8 - 1.84) < 5e-3
assert min_max(58, B_low) == Fraction(48, 54) == Fraction(8, 9) > Fraction(5, 6)  # min-max now says B
assert z_low < 1  # z now says A
print(f"low mark: min-max B {min_max(58, B_low)}, z B ~ {z_low:.2f}")

# Median and IQR (NumPy's default linear quartiles): the 10 moves nothing.
assert list(np.percentile(B, [25, 50, 75])) == [44.0, 49.0, 56.0]
assert list(np.percentile(B_low, [25, 50, 75])) == [44.0, 49.0, 56.0]
assert list(np.percentile(A, [25, 50, 75])) == [24.0, 30.0, 36.0]
assert Fraction(58 - 49, 56 - 44) == Fraction(3, 4) and Fraction(36 - 30, 36 - 24) == Fraction(1, 2)

# A new mark of 70 on B, with the scaler fitted on the nine marks.
assert min_max(70, B) == Fraction(32, 26) == Fraction(16, 13) > 1
assert abs(float(Fraction(16, 13)) - 1.23) < 5e-3
assert z_score(70, B) == Fraction(20, 8) == Fraction(5, 2)

# Units: re-marking A out of 100 doubles every mark; a minus sign flips the scale.
A_100 = [2 * x for x in A]
assert min_max(72, A_100) == Fraction(5, 6) and z_score(72, A_100) == 1
assert min_max(-36, [-x for x in A]) == 1 - Fraction(5, 6) == Fraction(1, 6)
assert z_score(-36, [-x for x in A]) == -1

# The Simpler fold: 2, 4, 9, then 30 in place of the 9.
small = [2, 4, 9]
assert mean(small) == 5 and variance(small) == Fraction(26, 3)
assert [min_max(x, small) for x in small] == [0, Fraction(2, 7), 1]
sigma_small = math.sqrt(26 / 3)
assert abs(sigma_small - 2.94) < 5e-3
zs_small = [(x - 5) / sigma_small for x in small]
assert all(abs(z - t) < 5e-3 for z, t in zip(zs_small, (-1.02, -0.34, 1.36)))
assert [min_max(x, [2, 4, 30]) for x in (2, 4, 30)] == [0, Fraction(1, 14), 1]
assert mean([2, 4, 30]) == 12 and variance([2, 4, 30]) == Fraction(488, 3)
assert abs((4 - 12) / math.sqrt(488 / 3) - (-0.63)) < 5e-3

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Test A is re-marked out of 100, so every mark doubles. What happens to Ana's min-max score and z-score?">
Neither changes: 5/6 and 1. Doubling multiplies the mean, the standard deviation, the smallest and the largest mark by 2, and the 2 cancels in each ratio: (36 − 21)/(39 − 21) becomes (72 − 42)/(78 − 42) = 30/36 = 5/6, and (36 − 30)/6 becomes (72 − 60)/12 = 1.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="After fitting on the nine test B marks, a new student scores 70. Find the min-max score and the z-score.">
Min-max: (70 − 38)/(64 − 38) = 32/26 = 16/13 ≈ 1.23, past 1. z = (70 − 50)/8 = 5/2.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Pick a scaler for each: pixel brightness from 0 to 255; household incomes with a few billionaires; children's heights for a nearest-neighbor model.">
Pixels: min-max, since the bounds are fixed and known. Incomes: the median and the IQR, or a log first, which Google's crash course lists as log scaling. Heights: standardization, with no fixed bounds and no extreme values.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="pandas' .std() makes Ana's z-score on test A about 0.94, not 1. Which is right?">
Both are consistent. pandas divides by n − 1, so the standard deviation is √(81/2) ≈ 6.36, z² = 36/(81/2) = 8/9 and z ≈ 0.94. Use one convention for every feature; `StandardScaler` divides by n. The variance page explains [the two versions](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#two-versions-the-whole-group-or-a-sample).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that a min-max score is unchanged by y = cx + d with c > 0, and find it when c is negative.">
For c > 0, the smallest and largest y are c·x_min + d and c·x_max + d, so (y − y_min)/(y_max − y_min) = c(x − x_min)/(c(x_max − x_min)) = x′. For c < 0 the ends swap: the smallest y comes from x_max, so the score is c(x − x_max)/(c(x_min − x_max)) = (x_max − x)/(x_max − x_min) = 1 − x′.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Min-max and standardization rank values the same way, so the choice doesn't matter.">
    Within one feature, yes; across features, no: min-max picks Ana's test A, z-scores call it a tie.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Standardizing makes data normally distributed.">
    It shifts and stretches, so the shape and the outliers stay. The normal distribution page says the same of [batch normalization](/statistics/normal-distribution/#batch-normalization).
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Min-max scores always lie between 0 and 1.">
    Only on the data it was fitted on. A new 70 on test B scores 16/13, and `MinMaxScaler` does not clip by default.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Min-max is safe from outliers because its output is bounded.">
    The bound comes from the outlier. One 10 moved Ana from 10/13 to 8/9.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Fit the scaler on the whole dataset, then split.">
    That leaks the test set's mean, standard deviation or ends into training. Split first, then fit on the training part.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Normalization means min-max scaling.">
    Google uses the word for both methods, and scikit-learn for rescaling each row to length 1. Name the formula.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
