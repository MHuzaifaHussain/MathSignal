<WhyThisMatters>
The normal distribution is the single most important distribution in machine learning. Neural network weights are initialized by sampling from Gaussian distributions (He, Xavier). Diffusion models like Stable Diffusion start from pure Gaussian noise and learn to reverse the process. Linear regression, t-tests and ANOVA assume normally distributed errors when they report exact p-values and intervals. And the central limit theorem says averages of many independent measurements with finite variance are approximately normal, which is why the bell appears even when the underlying data isn't.
</WhyThisMatters>

## What is the normal distribution?

Take any measurement that results from many small, independent factors adding together — human heights, manufacturing tolerances, exam scores, measurement errors — and plot a histogram. As your sample grows, that histogram almost always converges to the same shape: a symmetric bell curve peaked at the average, tapering smoothly in both directions. That shape is the normal distribution.

Two numbers define it completely. The **mean** (<Var name="mu" />) tells you where the bell is centered. The **standard deviation** (<Var name="sigma" />) tells you how wide it is. A small <Var name="sigma" /> produces a tall, narrow peak — data tightly clustered around the mean. A large <Var name="sigma" /> produces a flat, wide curve — data spread out.

### The 68-95-99.7 rule

This is worth memorizing. For any normal distribution:

- about **68%** (68.27%) lies within 1σ of the mean
- about **95%** (95.45%) lies within 2σ
- about **99.7%** (99.73%) lies within 3σ

The exact band that holds 95% is ±1.96σ.

This is why "three sigma" is often treated as a practical boundary. If a data point is more than 3σ from the mean, it's either extremely rare or something interesting is happening.

### Why does it appear everywhere?

The **central limit theorem** (CLT) is the reason. Add up $n$ independent, identically distributed variables with mean $\vmu$ and finite variance $\vsigma^2$: the standardised sum $(S_n - n\vmu)/(\vsigma\sqrt{n})$ approaches the standard normal distribution, so the average of $n$ draws is approximately normal with spread $\vsigma/\sqrt{n}$. The finite variance matters: averages of Cauchy draws never become normal. Human height isn't determined by a single gene; it's the sum of many genetic and environmental factors. A measurement error isn't caused by one thing going wrong; it's the accumulation of many tiny imprecisions. The CLT is why the normal distribution emerges from complexity.

This doesn't mean all data is normally distributed. Income, city populations, earthquake magnitudes, and post-ReLU activations are all decidedly non-Gaussian. The normal distribution dominates where **additive** effects are at play. When **multiplicative** effects dominate, you tend to see log-normal or power-law distributions instead.

<FormalDefinition>
The **probability density function** (PDF) of the normal distribution is:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\vsigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{(x - \vmu)^2}{2\vsigma^2}}
$$

where:
- $\vmu$ is the **mean** (center of the distribution)
- $\vsigma$ is the **standard deviation** (spread), with $\vsigma > 0$
- $\sigma^2$ is the **variance**

The **standard normal distribution** is the special case where $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$. Any normal random variable $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ can be standardized:

$$
Z = \frac{X - \vmu}{\vsigma} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

This transformation is called the **z-score** and converts any normal distribution to the standard form, making it possible to use a single reference table (or `scipy.stats.norm`) for all normal distributions.

The **moment generating function** (MGF) is:

$$
M_X(t) = e^{\mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}}
$$

which can be used to derive all moments of the distribution. The $n$-th moment about the mean is zero for odd $n$ (by symmetry) and $\sigma^n (n-1)!!$ for even $n$.
</FormalDefinition>

## In practice: machine learning applications

### Weight initialization

Neural networks are sensitive to how weights are initialized. **He initialization** draws weights from $\mathcal{N}(0,\, 2/n_{\text{in}})$, a standard deviation of $\sqrt{2/n_{\text{in}}}$, and was derived for ReLU layers. **Xavier/Glorot initialization** uses $\mathcal{N}(0,\, 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}))$ and was derived for tanh, sigmoid and linear layers; Glorot and Bengio used the uniform version $U(-a, a)$ with $a = \sqrt{6/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}$, which has the same variance. In both, the second argument is the variance.

### Batch normalization

Batch normalization standardises each feature over a mini-batch, $\hat{x} = (x - \mu_B)/\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}$, then rescales it with learned parameters, $y = \gamma\hat{x} + \beta$. That is a z-score: it fixes the mean and variance, not the shape, so activations are not made Gaussian. It still stabilises training and tolerates higher learning rates.

### Gaussian naive Bayes

This classifier assumes each feature follows a normal distribution within each class. It stores one mean and one variance per feature per class, plus the class priors, and suits continuous features such as flower measurements or sensor readings. For word counts in text, multinomial or Bernoulli naive Bayes fit better.

### VAE latent spaces

Variational autoencoders impose a $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ prior on the latent space. The encoder outputs $\mu$ and $\sigma$ parameters, and the reparameterization trick ($z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$) enables backpropagation through the sampling step.

### Gaussian processes

A Gaussian process is a distribution over *functions*, where any finite subset of function values is jointly normally distributed. GPs give predictions with uncertainty attached, well calibrated when the kernel and noise model fit, making them valuable for Bayesian optimization and active learning.

<PyRunner
  title="check_68_95_997.py"
  code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=0)
mu, sigma = 0.0, 1.0
samples = rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=100_000)

expected = {1: 0.6827, 2: 0.9545, 3: 0.9973}
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    inside = np.mean(np.abs(samples - mu) < k * sigma)
    print(f"within {k} sigma of the mean: {inside:.2%} (exact {expected[k]:.2%})")
    assert abs(inside - expected[k]) < 0.005

print(f"sample mean {samples.mean():.3f}, sample std {samples.std():.3f}")
assert abs(samples.mean() - mu) < 0.01 * sigma
assert abs(samples.std() - sigma) < 0.01 * sigma
`}

The same distribution with plotting, and in PyTorch (these two run on your own machine):

```python title="normal_distribution_numpy.py" {4-6,9-10}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=0)
mu, sigma = 0.0, 1.0
samples = rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=10_000)

x = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
pdf = (1 / (sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))) * np.exp(
    -0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma) ** 2
)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.hist(samples, bins=60, density=True, alpha=0.6, label="Samples")
plt.plot(x, pdf, "r-", linewidth=2, label="PDF")
plt.xlabel("x")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.title(f"Normal distribution (mu={mu}, sigma={sigma})")
plt.legend()
plt.show()
```

```python title="normal_distribution_pytorch.py" {4-6}
import torch
from torch.distributions import Normal

torch.manual_seed(0)
mu, sigma = torch.tensor(0.0), torch.tensor(1.0)
dist = Normal(mu, sigma)

# Sample and compute log-probability
samples = dist.sample((10_000,))
log_probs = dist.log_prob(samples)

print(f"Sample mean:  {samples.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Sample std:   {samples.std():.4f}")
print(f"Mean log-prob: {log_probs.mean():.4f}")

# Useful in loss functions: negative log-likelihood
nll = -dist.log_prob(torch.tensor(1.5))
print(f"NLL at x=1.5: {nll:.4f}")
```

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="My data is normally distributed.">
    Probably not. Most real-world data has skew, heavy tails, or bounded support. Check with a Q-Q plot or a Shapiro-Wilk test before assuming normality. Many ML methods are robust to mild non-normality, but the assumption still matters for classical inference.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The mean equals the median for every distribution.">
    Only for symmetric distributions. The normal distribution is symmetric, so its mean, median and mode coincide, but skewed distributions like income or survival times break this.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Standard deviation and variance are interchangeable.">
    They're related ($\text{Var} = \vsigma^2$) but have different units: variance is in squared units, standard deviation in the original units. Frameworks use one or the other inconsistently, so check which one a function expects.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The curve touches zero at ±3σ.">
    It doesn't. The normal distribution extends from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$; the density at $\pm 3\vsigma$ is small but not zero. This matters for tail risk and anomaly detection.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Batch normalisation makes activations normally distributed.">
    It standardises their mean and variance over each batch, which is a z-score, not a change of shape.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
