## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Heights can never be negative, yet the bell's tails stretch forever in both directions, so the bell is an approximation that works well near the middle. Mix two groups with different average heights, such as children and adults, and you get two humps instead of one bell.">

Line up everyone in a class by height. A few are very short, a few are very tall, and most stand somewhere in the middle. Count how many people fall into each 5 cm step and stack them into columns, and the columns rise in the middle and fall away evenly on both sides. With enough people, that outline becomes a smooth bell. That bell is the normal distribution.

A peg board shows why it happens. Drop a ball through rows of pegs and it bounces left or right at each one. Most balls collect a mix of lefts and rights and land near the middle; only a lucky few go left every time. Many small, independent nudges added together produce the bell.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Neural network weights are usually initialized by sampling from a normal distribution, and the scale of that distribution decides whether training gets started at all. Diffusion models generate images by starting from pure Gaussian noise and learning to remove it step by step. Linear regression, t-tests and ANOVA assume normally distributed errors when they report exact p-values and intervals. And the central limit theorem says averages of many independent measurements are approximately normal, which is why the bell appears even when the underlying data isn't.
</WhyThisMatters>

## What is the normal distribution?

The normal distribution is the shape you get when a measurement is the sum of many small, independent effects. Heights within one sex, small manufacturing errors and repeated lab measurements all come close to it.

Two numbers describe it completely. The **mean** (<Var name="mu" />) is where the bell is centered, and the **standard deviation** (<Var name="sigma" />) is how wide it is. A small <Var name="sigma" /> gives a tall, narrow bell, with values packed close to the mean. A large <Var name="sigma" /> gives a low, wide bell, with values spread out.

<Simpler label="the same idea with dice">

Roll one die and every number from 1 to 6 is equally likely: a flat shape, not a bell. Roll two dice and add them, and 7 is the most common total (6 ways out of 36) while 2 and 12 are the rarest (1 way each). Add ten dice and the totals pile up around 35 in an almost perfect bell. Each die is one small independent nudge; adding many of them is what makes the bell.

</Simpler>

### The 68-95-99.7 rule

The 68-95-99.7 rule says how much of a normal distribution lies within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean:

- about **68%** (68.27%) lies within 1σ of the mean
- about **95%** (95.45%) lies within 2σ
- about **99.7%** (99.73%) lies within 3σ

The exact band that holds 95% is ±1.96σ, which is why 1.96 appears in so many confidence intervals. A value more than 3σ from the mean happens about 3 times in 1,000, so it is either rare or a sign that something unusual is going on.

<GoDeeper label="where the three numbers come from">

Let $\Phi$ be the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal. The share within $k$ standard deviations is $\Phi(k) - \Phi(-k) = 2\Phi(k) - 1 = \operatorname{erf}(k/\sqrt{2})$. For $k = 1, 2, 3$ this gives 0.6827, 0.9545 and 0.9973. The rule holds for every normal distribution because standardizing (see the derivations below) turns any of them into the standard one.

</GoDeeper>

### Why does it appear everywhere?

The central limit theorem is the reason. Add up $n$ independent, identically distributed variables with mean $\vmu$ and finite variance $\vsigma^2$. As $n$ grows, the standardized sum $(S_n - n\vmu)/(\vsigma\sqrt{n})$ approaches the standard normal distribution. Equivalently, the average of $n$ draws is approximately normal, centered on $\vmu$ with spread $\vsigma/\sqrt{n}$.

<GoDeeper label="why the finite variance condition matters">

The theorem needs the variance to be finite. The Cauchy distribution has tails so heavy that its variance doesn't exist, and the average of $n$ Cauchy draws has exactly the same Cauchy distribution as a single draw, however large $n$ is. No bell ever appears. Heavy-tailed data in practice, possibly including some gradient noise (see the research frontier), can behave much more like this than like the textbook case.

</GoDeeper>

None of this means all data is normal. Incomes, city sizes, earthquake magnitudes and post-ReLU activations are all far from it. The bell appears where effects **add up**; where they **multiply**, you tend to see log-normal or power-law shapes instead.

<SideNote>
You will also see it called the Gaussian distribution, after Carl Friedrich Gauss, who used it to model errors in astronomical measurements.
</SideNote>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["mu", "sigma", "sigma2", "normal", "sim"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

For any real $\vmu$ and any $\vsigma > 0$, a random variable $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\vmu, \vsigma^2)$ has density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\vsigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \vmu)^2}{2\vsigma^2}\right), \qquad x \in \mathbb{R},
$$

with $\mathbb{E}[X] = \vmu$ and $\operatorname{Var}[X] = \vsigma^2$. The case $\vsigma = 0$ is excluded: it would put all the probability on the single point $\vmu$, which has no density.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

**Standardizing.** Any normal random variable can be turned into a standard normal one by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation:

$$
Z = \frac{X - \vmu}{\vsigma} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

This is the **z-score**. It lets one reference table, or `scipy.stats.norm`, serve every normal distribution.

<StepThrough title="Why the constant is 1/√(2π)">

1. Let $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx$. Square it and write it as a double integral: $I^2 = \iint e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy$.
2. Switch to polar coordinates, where $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$: $I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\, r\,dr\,d\theta$.
3. The inner integral is $\left[-e^{-r^2/2}\right]_0^\infty = 1$, so $I^2 = 2\pi$ and $I = \sqrt{2\pi}$.
4. Dividing by $\sqrt{2\pi}$, and by $\vsigma$ after rescaling $x$, makes the total area exactly 1.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="The moment generating function">

1. By definition $M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \int e^{tx} f(x)\,dx$.
2. Combine the exponents and complete the square in $x$: $tx - \frac{(x - \vmu)^2}{2\vsigma^2} = -\frac{(x - \vmu - \vsigma^2 t)^2}{2\vsigma^2} + \vmu t + \frac{\vsigma^2 t^2}{2}$.
3. The first term is the exponent of a normal density centered at $\vmu + \vsigma^2 t$, which integrates to 1, leaving $M_X(t) = \exp\!\left(\vmu t + \tfrac{1}{2}\vsigma^2 t^2\right)$.
4. Differentiating at $t = 0$ produces the moments. The $n$-th moment about the mean is zero for odd $n$, by symmetry, and $\vsigma^n (n-1)!!$ for even $n$.

</StepThrough>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="How unusual is a height?">

Suppose adult heights in a group are normal with $\vmu = 165$ cm and $\vsigma = 7$ cm. A person 179 cm tall has $z = (179 - 165)/7 = 2$: two standard deviations above the mean. Only about 2.3% of the group is taller.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="Where does the top 5% start?">

The 95th percentile of a standard normal is $z = 1.645$. For the same group, that is $165 + 1.645 \times 7 \approx 176.5$ cm.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="A weight initialization" faded>

He initialization draws weights from $\mathcal{N}(0, 2/n_{\text{in}})$. For a layer with $n_{\text{in}} = 512$ inputs, what is the standard deviation of the weights?

<div slot="solution">

The second argument is the variance: $2/512 \approx 0.0039$. The standard deviation is its square root, $\sqrt{2/512} = 0.0625$.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Where the normal distribution shows up in ML

### Weight initialization

How weights start decides whether signals and gradients survive the trip through a deep network. **He initialization** <Cite id="he2015" /> draws weights from $\mathcal{N}(0,\, 2/n_{\text{in}})$, giving a standard deviation of $\sqrt{2/n_{\text{in}}}$, and was derived for ReLU layers. **Xavier (Glorot) initialization** <Cite id="glorot2010" /> uses $\mathcal{N}(0,\, 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}))$ and was derived for tanh, sigmoid and linear layers. Glorot and Bengio's paper used the uniform version $U(-a, a)$ with $a = \sqrt{6/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}$, which has the same variance and is Keras's default. In both cases the second argument is the variance.

### Batch normalization

Batch normalization <Cite id="ioffe2015" /> standardizes each feature over a mini-batch and then rescales it with two learned parameters:

$$
\hat{x} = \frac{x - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y = \gamma \hat{x} + \beta
$$

Here $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ are the batch mean and variance. This is a z-score: it fixes the mean and variance, not the shape, so the activations are not made Gaussian. The learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$ can even undo the scaling. It still makes training more stable and tolerates higher learning rates.

### Gaussian naive Bayes

Gaussian naive Bayes assumes that, within each class $c$, every feature $x_j$ is independently normal:

$$
p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c) = \prod_j \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_j;\ \mu_{jc},\ \sigma_{jc}^2\right)
$$

It predicts the class with the largest $P(y = c)\, p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c)$, which by [Bayes' theorem](/probability/bayes-theorem/) is the class with the largest posterior $P(y = c \mid \mathbf{x})$. So it stores one mean and one variance per feature per class, plus the class priors. It suits continuous features such as flower measurements or sensor readings; for word counts in text, multinomial or Bernoulli naive Bayes fit better.

### VAE latent spaces

Variational autoencoders place a $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ prior on the latent space. The encoder outputs $\mu$ and $\sigma$ parameters, and the reparameterization trick <Cite id="kingma2013" /> ($z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$) lets gradients flow through the sampling step.

### Gaussian processes

A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />. Writing $f \sim \mathcal{GP}(m, k)$ means that for any inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the values $(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n))$ are jointly normal with mean vector $m$ and covariance matrix $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$. Gaussian processes give predictions with uncertainty attached, which is well calibrated when the kernel and noise model fit the data, and makes them useful for Bayesian optimization and active learning.

## Check the 68-95-99.7 rule in code

This runs in your browser, uses the same standard normal as the board's starting position, and stops with an error if a result is off:

<PyRunner

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=0)
mu, sigma = 0.0, 1.0
samples = rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=100_000)

expected = {1: 0.6827, 2: 0.9545, 3: 0.9973}
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    inside = np.mean(np.abs(samples - mu) < k * sigma)
    print(f"within {k} sigma of the mean: {inside:.2%} (exact {expected[k]:.2%})")
    assert abs(inside - expected[k]) < 0.005

print(f"sample mean {samples.mean():.3f}, sample std {samples.std():.3f}")
assert abs(samples.mean() - mu) < 0.01 * sigma
assert abs(samples.std() - sigma) < 0.01 * sigma
`}

```python title="normal_distribution_numpy.py" {4-6,9-10}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=0)
mu, sigma = 0.0, 1.0
samples = rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=10_000)

x = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
pdf = (1 / (sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))) * np.exp(
    -0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma) ** 2
)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.hist(samples, bins=60, density=True, alpha=0.6, label="Samples")
plt.plot(x, pdf, linewidth=2, label="Density")
plt.xlabel("x")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.title(f"Normal distribution (mu={mu}, sigma={sigma})")
plt.legend()
plt.show()
```

```python title="normal_distribution_pytorch.py" {4-6}
import torch
from torch.distributions import Normal

torch.manual_seed(0)
mu, sigma = torch.tensor(0.0), torch.tensor(1.0)
dist = Normal(mu, sigma)  # Normal takes the standard deviation, not the variance

# Sample and compute log-probability
samples = dist.sample((10_000,))
log_probs = dist.log_prob(samples)

print(f"Sample mean:   {samples.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Sample std:    {samples.std():.4f}")
print(f"Mean log-prob: {log_probs.mean():.4f}")

# Used in loss functions: negative log-likelihood of one observation
nll = -dist.log_prob(torch.tensor(1.5))
print(f"NLL at x=1.5:  {nll:.4f}")
```

## Research frontier

<ResearchFrontier reviewed="2026-11-23">

**Wide neural networks behave like Gaussian processes.** As the layers of a deep network grow infinitely wide, the network at initialization becomes exactly a Gaussian process, which allows fully Bayesian prediction with deep networks <Cite id="lee2017" />. In the same limit, training by gradient descent is described by a fixed kernel, the neural tangent kernel <Cite id="jacot2018" />. How closely these infinite-width results describe the finite networks used in practice is an active area of research.

**Generative models built on Gaussian noise.** Diffusion models corrupt data with small amounts of Gaussian noise over many steps and learn to reverse the process <Cite id="ho2020" />. Score-based models describe the same idea as a continuous stochastic differential equation, which links the noising and denoising directions <Cite id="song2020" />.

**Is gradient noise really Gaussian?** Analyses of stochastic gradient descent often assume the noise in each step is Gaussian, an appeal to the central limit theorem. Şimşekli, Sagun and Gürbüzbalaban argue it is heavy-tailed instead, model it with an α-stable distribution, and find the noise strongly non-Gaussian in their experiments <Cite id="simsekli2019" />. If so, the finite-variance condition above fails, and conclusions built on the Gaussian assumption need rechecking.

</ResearchFrontier>

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="You widen the bell from σ = 1 to σ = 3. What happens to the peak height?">
It falls to one third. The peak is $1/(\vsigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, so it scales with $1/\vsigma$; the area has to stay 1.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="What share of a normal distribution lies between μ − σ and μ + 2σ?">
About 82% (81.86%). Half of the 68.27% band lies between μ − σ and μ, and half of the 95.45% band lies between μ and μ + 2σ: 34.13% + 47.72%, or exactly $\Phi(2) - \Phi(-1) = 0.8186$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Does batch normalization make a layer's activations normally distributed?">
No. It sets each feature's batch mean to 0 and variance to 1 (before $\gamma$ and $\beta$), but a skewed distribution stays skewed.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that the mean of X ~ 𝒩(μ, σ²) is μ, using only the symmetry of the density.">
Write $\mathbb{E}[X] - \vmu = \int (x - \vmu) f(x)\,dx$ and substitute $y = x - \vmu$. The density is symmetric about $\vmu$, so in terms of $y$ it is an even function, while $y$ itself is odd. Their product is odd, and the integral of an odd function over the whole real line is 0, provided it converges, which it does here because the tails fall off like $e^{-y^2/2\vsigma^2}$. So $\mathbb{E}[X] = \vmu$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="My data is normally distributed.">
    Probably not. Most real-world data has skew, heavy tails or limits on its range. Check with a Q-Q plot or a Shapiro-Wilk test before assuming normality. Many ML methods cope with mild departures, but classical inference depends on the assumption.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A wider bell is a taller bell.">
    It is a lower one. The area under the curve is always 1, so when $\vsigma$ grows the peak $1/(\vsigma\sqrt{2\pi})$ has to fall. Try it on the board above.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Standard deviation and variance are interchangeable.">
    They're related ($\operatorname{Var} = \vsigma^2$) but have different units: variance is in squared units, standard deviation in the original ones. Libraries differ too: $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ notation uses the variance, while `numpy.random.normal` and `torch.distributions.Normal` take the standard deviation. Check which one a function expects.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The curve touches zero at ±3σ.">
    It doesn't. The normal distribution extends from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$; the density at $\pm 3\vsigma$ is small but not zero. This matters for tail risk and anomaly detection.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Batch normalization makes activations normally distributed.">
    It standardizes their mean and variance over each batch, which is a z-score, not a change of shape. See the batch normalization section above.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
