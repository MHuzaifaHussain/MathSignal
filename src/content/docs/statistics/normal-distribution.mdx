## In everyday terms

Line up everyone in a class by height. A few are very short, a few are very tall, and most stand somewhere in the middle. Count how many people fall into each 5 cm step and stack them into columns, and the columns rise in the middle and fall away evenly on both sides. With enough people, that outline becomes a smooth bell. That bell is the normal distribution.

A peg board shows why it happens. Drop a ball through rows of pegs and it bounces left or right at each one. Most balls collect a mix of lefts and rights and land near the middle; only a lucky few go left every time. Many small, independent nudges added together produce the bell.

**Where this picture breaks.** Heights can never be negative, yet the bell's tails stretch forever in both directions, so the bell is an approximation that works well near the middle. Mix two groups with different average heights, such as children and adults, and you get two humps instead of one bell.

<WhyThisMatters>
Neural network weights are usually initialised by sampling from a normal distribution, and the scale of that distribution decides whether training gets started at all. Diffusion models generate images by starting from pure Gaussian noise and learning to remove it step by step. Linear regression, t-tests and ANOVA assume normally distributed errors when they report exact p-values and intervals. And the central limit theorem says averages of many independent measurements are approximately normal, which is why the bell appears even when the underlying data isn't.
</WhyThisMatters>

## What is the normal distribution?

The normal distribution is the shape you get when a measurement is the sum of many small, independent effects. Heights within one sex, small manufacturing errors and repeated lab measurements all come close to it.

Two numbers describe it completely. The **mean** (<Var name="mu" />) is where the bell is centred, and the **standard deviation** (<Var name="sigma" />) is how wide it is. A small <Var name="sigma" /> gives a tall, narrow bell, with values packed close to the mean. A large <Var name="sigma" /> gives a low, wide bell, with values spread out.

### The 68-95-99.7 rule

The 68-95-99.7 rule says how much of a normal distribution lies within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean:

- about **68%** (68.27%) lies within 1σ of the mean
- about **95%** (95.45%) lies within 2σ
- about **99.7%** (99.73%) lies within 3σ

The exact band that holds 95% is ±1.96σ, which is why 1.96 appears in so many confidence intervals. A value more than 3σ from the mean happens about 3 times in 1,000, so it is either rare or a sign that something unusual is going on.

### Why does it appear everywhere?

The central limit theorem is the reason. Add up $n$ independent, identically distributed variables with mean $\vmu$ and finite variance $\vsigma^2$. As $n$ grows, the standardised sum $(S_n - n\vmu)/(\vsigma\sqrt{n})$ approaches the standard normal distribution. Equivalently, the average of $n$ draws is approximately normal, centred on $\vmu$ with spread $\vsigma/\sqrt{n}$.

The finite variance condition matters. Averages of draws from a Cauchy distribution never settle into a bell, however many you take.

None of this means all data is normal. Incomes, city sizes, earthquake magnitudes and post-ReLU activations are all far from it. The bell appears where effects **add up**; where they **multiply**, you tend to see log-normal or power-law shapes instead.

<SideNote>
You will also see it called the Gaussian distribution, after Carl Friedrich Gauss, who used it to model errors in astronomical measurements.
</SideNote>

## Notation

| Symbol | Say it | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\vmu$ | "mu" | Mean: the centre of the bell |
| $\vsigma$ | "sigma" | Standard deviation: the spread, in the data's units |
| $\vsigma^2$ | "sigma squared" | Variance: the spread in squared units |
| $\mathcal{N}(\vmu, \vsigma^2)$ | "normal with mean mu and variance sigma squared" | A normal distribution. The second argument is the **variance**, not the standard deviation |
| $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\vmu, \vsigma^2)$ | "X is distributed normally with…" | The random variable $X$ follows that distribution |

**Precise statement.** For any real $\vmu$ and any $\vsigma > 0$, a random variable $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\vmu, \vsigma^2)$ has density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\vsigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \vmu)^2}{2\vsigma^2}\right), \qquad x \in \mathbb{R},
$$

with $\mathbb{E}[X] = \vmu$ and $\operatorname{Var}[X] = \vsigma^2$.

<FormalDefinition>
**Standardising.** Any normal random variable can be turned into a standard normal one by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation:

$$
Z = \frac{X - \vmu}{\vsigma} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

This is the **z-score**. It lets one reference table, or `scipy.stats.norm`, serve every normal distribution.

**Why the constant is $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ (sketch).** Let $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx$.

1. Square it and write it as a double integral: $I^2 = \iint e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy$.
2. Switch to polar coordinates, where $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$: $I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\, r\,dr\,d\theta$.
3. The inner integral is $\left[-e^{-r^2/2}\right]_0^\infty = 1$, so $I^2 = 2\pi$ and $I = \sqrt{2\pi}$.

Dividing by $\sqrt{2\pi}$ (and by $\vsigma$ after rescaling) makes the total area exactly 1.

**Moment generating function.** Completing the square in the exponent of $\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$ gives

$$
M_X(t) = \exp\!\left(\vmu t + \frac{\vsigma^2 t^2}{2}\right).
$$

Differentiating at $t = 0$ produces the moments. The $n$-th moment about the mean is zero for odd $n$, by symmetry, and $\vsigma^n (n-1)!!$ for even $n$.

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Mean, median, mode | $\vmu$ |
| Variance | $\vsigma^2$ |
| Skewness | 0 |
| Excess kurtosis | 0 |
| Entropy | $\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi e \vsigma^2)$ |
| Moment generating function | $\exp(\vmu t + \vsigma^2 t^2 / 2)$ |
</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

**1. How unusual is a height?** Suppose adult heights in a group are normal with $\vmu = 165$ cm and $\vsigma = 7$ cm. A person 179 cm tall has $z = (179 - 165)/7 = 2$: two standard deviations above the mean. Only about 2.3% of the group is taller.

**2. Where does the top 5% start?** The 95th percentile of a standard normal is $z = 1.645$. For the same group, that is $165 + 1.645 \times 7 \approx 176.5$ cm.

**3. Your turn: a weight initialisation.** He initialisation draws weights from $\mathcal{N}(0, 2/n_{\text{in}})$. For a layer with $n_{\text{in}} = 512$ inputs, what is the standard deviation of the weights? (Answer: the variance is $2/512 \approx 0.0039$, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{2/512} = 0.0625$.)

## In practice: machine learning applications

### Weight initialisation

How weights start decides whether signals and gradients survive the trip through a deep network. **He initialisation** draws weights from $\mathcal{N}(0,\, 2/n_{\text{in}})$, giving a standard deviation of $\sqrt{2/n_{\text{in}}}$, and was derived for ReLU layers. **Xavier (Glorot) initialisation** uses $\mathcal{N}(0,\, 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}))$ and was derived for tanh, sigmoid and linear layers. Glorot and Bengio's paper used the uniform version $U(-a, a)$ with $a = \sqrt{6/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}$, which has the same variance and is Keras's default. In both cases the second argument is the variance (see the third common mistake below).

### Batch normalisation

Batch normalisation standardises each feature over a mini-batch and then rescales it with two learned parameters:

$$
\hat{x} = \frac{x - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y = \gamma \hat{x} + \beta
$$

Here $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ are the batch mean and variance. This is a z-score: it fixes the mean and variance, not the shape, so the activations are not made Gaussian. The learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$ can even undo the scaling. It still makes training more stable and tolerates higher learning rates.

### Gaussian naive Bayes

Gaussian naive Bayes assumes that, within each class $c$, every feature $x_j$ is independently normal:

$$
p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c) = \prod_j \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_j;\ \mu_{jc},\ \sigma_{jc}^2\right)
$$

It predicts the class with the largest $P(y = c)\, p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c)$, so it stores one mean and one variance per feature per class, plus the class priors. It suits continuous features such as flower measurements or sensor readings; for word counts in text, multinomial or Bernoulli naive Bayes fit better.

### VAE latent spaces

Variational autoencoders place a $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ prior on the latent space. The encoder outputs $\mu$ and $\sigma$ parameters, and the reparameterisation trick ($z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$) lets gradients flow through the sampling step.

### Gaussian processes

A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions. Writing $f \sim \mathcal{GP}(m, k)$ means that for any inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the values $(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n))$ are jointly normal with mean vector $m$ and covariance matrix $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$. Gaussian processes give predictions with uncertainty attached, which is well calibrated when the kernel and noise model fit the data, and makes them useful for Bayesian optimisation and active learning.

The same distribution with a plot, and in PyTorch. These two run on your own machine:

```python title="normal_distribution_numpy.py" {4-6,9-10}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=0)
mu, sigma = 0.0, 1.0
samples = rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=10_000)

x = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
pdf = (1 / (sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))) * np.exp(
    -0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma) ** 2
)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.hist(samples, bins=60, density=True, alpha=0.6, label="Samples")
plt.plot(x, pdf, linewidth=2, label="Density")
plt.xlabel("x")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.title(f"Normal distribution (mu={mu}, sigma={sigma})")
plt.legend()
plt.show()
```

```python title="normal_distribution_pytorch.py" {4-6}
import torch
from torch.distributions import Normal

torch.manual_seed(0)
mu, sigma = torch.tensor(0.0), torch.tensor(1.0)
dist = Normal(mu, sigma)  # Normal takes the standard deviation, not the variance

# Sample and compute log-probability
samples = dist.sample((10_000,))
log_probs = dist.log_prob(samples)

print(f"Sample mean:   {samples.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Sample std:    {samples.std():.4f}")
print(f"Mean log-prob: {log_probs.mean():.4f}")

# Used in loss functions: negative log-likelihood of one observation
nll = -dist.log_prob(torch.tensor(1.5))
print(f"NLL at x=1.5:  {nll:.4f}")
```

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="My data is normally distributed.">
    Probably not. Most real-world data has skew, heavy tails or limits on its range. Check with a Q-Q plot or a Shapiro-Wilk test before assuming normality. Many ML methods cope with mild departures, but classical inference depends on the assumption.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A wider bell is a taller bell.">
    It is a lower one. The area under the curve is always 1, so when $\vsigma$ grows the peak $1/(\vsigma\sqrt{2\pi})$ has to fall. Try it on the board above.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Standard deviation and variance are interchangeable.">
    They're related ($\operatorname{Var} = \vsigma^2$) but have different units: variance is in squared units, standard deviation in the original ones. Libraries differ too: $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ notation uses the variance, while `numpy.random.normal` and `torch.distributions.Normal` take the standard deviation. Check which one a function expects.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The curve touches zero at ±3σ.">
    It doesn't. The normal distribution extends from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$; the density at $\pm 3\vsigma$ is small but not zero. This matters for tail risk and anomaly detection.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Batch normalisation makes activations normally distributed.">
    It standardises their mean and variance over each batch, which is a z-score, not a change of shape. See the batch normalisation section above.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
