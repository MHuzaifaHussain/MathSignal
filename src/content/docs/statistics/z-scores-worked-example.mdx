## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="A stick of one standard deviation is a typical distance, not a fixed rule. Two classes with the same balance point and the same stick can be shaped very differently, so 'one stick above' doesn't say how many children are taller. Real growth charts don't use one plain stick per age either: they use three numbers for each age.">

Two classes stand along two height tapes on the floor: one of nine-year-olds, one of eleven-year-olds. Mark each class's balance point, its mean, on its own tape, and cut each class a measuring stick as long as its standard deviation: 8 cm for the nine-year-olds, 12 cm for the eleven-year-olds. "Taller for your age" asks how many of your own class's sticks you stand above its balance point. A 140 cm nine-year-old and a 150 cm eleven-year-old each stand 6 cm above theirs. Who stands more sticks above?

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Models see features in different units, such as a house's area in square meters and its age in years. Standardizing each feature to z-scores puts them on one scale, and batch and layer normalization standardize values inside the network. Taking the mean or the standard deviation from the wrong data, such as the test set, silently changes what the model sees.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Taller for their age? Read it on the board

To compare children of different ages, measure each against their own class: subtract the class's mean, then divide by its standard deviation. The nine-year-olds are our class from the [variance page](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/), with mean 134 cm and standard deviation 8 cm; the eleven-year-olds are a second class, with mean 144 cm and standard deviation 12 cm. Both classes are made up.

Before you touch anything: the 140 cm nine-year-old and the 150 cm eleven-year-old both stand 6 cm above their class's mean. Is one of them taller for their age, or are they the same? Going further: with standard deviations of 8 and 12 cm, which child's z-score is bigger, and by what factor?

The Height slider moves the ochre point (arrow keys 1 cm, Page Up and Page Down 5 cm), or drag it. Table view shows the steps as a table; Reset hides the z ruler and the answer again.

**The answer.** The nine-year-old. After Check the board adds a z ruler under the centimeters, and its verdict, whatever you picked, gives "z = 6 / 8 = 0.75 for the nine-year-old and only z = 6 / 12 = 0.5 for the eleven-year-old." The same 6 cm divided by a smaller standard deviation gives a z-score 0.75 / 0.5 = 3/2 times as big. In centimeters the eleven-year-old is still taller.

**The shares.** With Shade the share below on, the board reads "If heights in this group follow a normal curve, about 77.3% are below 140 cm: Φ(0.75) ≈ 0.7734." Φ is the share of a standard normal, the normal curve with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, at or below a given z. For the eleven-year-old it reads "about 69.1% are below 150 cm: Φ(0.5) ≈ 0.6915", a smaller area for a smaller z.

**Same shape, new ruler.** Press Standard normal, z = 0.75. The centimeter ruler goes, and the shaded area stays exactly the nine-year-old's: "About 77.3% of a standard normal is below 0.75: Φ(0.75) ≈ 0.7734." Standardizing, turning every value into its z-score, only relabels the ruler, from centimeters to standard deviations.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["data-value", "sample-size", "mu", "sigma", "z", "normal", "sim", "standard-normal", "std-normal-cdf", "cdf", "expectation", "variance", "sample-sd"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be real numbers with mean $\vmu$ and standard deviation $\vsigma > 0$, both dividing by $n$ as on the [variance page](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/). The z-score of $x_i$, and the value with z-score $z$, are

$$
z_i = \frac{x_i - \vmu}{\vsigma}, \qquad x = \vmu + z\,\vsigma.
$$

The $z_i$ have no units, average 0 and have standard deviation 1. The same holds for a random variable $X$, such as a randomly chosen child's height, with $\mathbb{E}[X] = \vmu$ and $\operatorname{Var}[X] = \vsigma^2 > 0$, the variable $Z = (X - \vmu)/\vsigma$ has mean 0 and variance 1, and if $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\vmu, \vsigma^2)$, then $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $F_X(x) = \Phi\big((x - \vmu)/\vsigma\big)$.

Boundary case: if $\vsigma = 0$, every value is equal and no z-score exists. Convention: some tools divide by $n - 1$, using $s$ in place of $\vsigma$; this page uses $\vsigma$ unless it says otherwise.

</PreciseStatement>

## Three steps to a z-score

Find the group's mean and standard deviation, subtract the mean from the value, and divide the difference by the standard deviation.

<StepThrough title="The 140 cm nine-year-old, step by step" rigor="full">

1. **Mean and standard deviation.** The nine heights, in the order the children arrived, are 133, 122, 140, 128, 148, 125, 137, 131 and 142 cm. They add up to 1,206 cm, so <Var name="mu" /> = 1,206 / 9 = 134 cm. Their squared distances from 134 add up to 576, so <Var name="sigma" /> = √(576 / 9) = √64 = 8 cm, as on the [variance page](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#how-far-from-the-mean-on-average).
2. **Subtract.** 140 − 134 = +6 cm. The plus sign says above the mean.
3. **Divide.** 6 / 8 = 0.75. Centimeters over centimeters cancel, so z has no unit.

</StepThrough>

So the child stands three quarters of a standard deviation above the mean.

### What the sign and the size tell you

A positive z-score is above the mean, a negative one below, and its size counts standard deviations. In arrival order, our class's z-scores are −1/8, −3/2, 3/4, −3/4, 7/4, −9/8, 3/8, −3/8 and 1: the tallest child, 148 cm, has z = 1.75, the shortest, 122 cm, has z = −1.5, and the 142 cm child stands exactly one standard deviation above. As for any list, they add up to 0 and their squares average 1 (proved below).

A z-score beyond 2 or 3 in size often flags an outlier, a value far from the rest; [outlier detection: z-score vs IQR](/statistics/outlier-detection-z-score-vs-iqr/) covers when that fails, and the [variance page's research frontier](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#research-frontier) shows an outlier this rule misses.

<Simpler label="z-scores as a second ruler" id="second-ruler">

Under a centimeter ruler for our class, draw a second ruler with a mark every 8 cm from the mean: 126, 134, 142 and 150 cm, labeled −1, 0, 1 and 2. A height read on the second ruler is its z-score: 140 cm sits three quarters of the way from 0 to 1. The eleven-year-olds' marks are 12 cm apart, so 6 cm covers only half a gap.

</Simpler>

### Comparing across groups

Compare z-scores, not raw values, when the groups have different means or different spreads. A bigger gap in centimeters can still be the smaller z: a 155 cm eleven-year-old stands 11 cm above the mean, z = 11/12 ≈ 0.92, while a 142 cm nine-year-old stands only 8 cm above, z = 8/8 = 1.

Units don't matter: in meters, (1.40 − 1.34) / 0.08 = 0.75 again. A change of units scales the value, the mean and <Var name="sigma" /> alike. Adding the same amount to every height moves the value and the mean together and leaves <Var name="sigma" /> alone. This is the [shift and scale rule](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#shifting-and-stretching-the-whole-class) applied to the top and the bottom of the fraction.

<SideNote>
Growth charts use the same idea with more care: the CDC computes the z-score of a height $X$ from three numbers, $L$, $M$ and $S$, published for each sex and age, as $((X/M)^L - 1)/(LS)$ when $L \ne 0$ <Cite id="cdc-growth-lms" />.
</SideNote>

<GoDeeper label="what z says when the data are not normal" id="without-normality">

For any list and any $k > 0$, at most $1/k^2$ of the values have $|z| \ge k$. This is Chebyshev's inequality, Grinstead and Snell's Theorem 8.1 (p. 305) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />, $P(|X - \vmu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \operatorname{Var}[X]/\varepsilon^2$, with $\varepsilon = k\vsigma$ and each value picked with chance $1/n$. In our class 2 of the 9 children have $|z| \ge 1.5$, and $2/9 \le 1/1.5^2 = 4/9$. So z limits how rare a value can be, but an exact share needs a model.

</GoDeeper>

## From z to a share: the normal model

If the group's values follow a normal curve, the share below a value with z-score z is Φ(z), the same for every normal group, because standardizing only relabels the ruler. Grinstead and Snell call $Z = (X - \vmu)/\vsigma$ the standardized version of the random variable $X$ (§5.2, p. 214) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. The Normal page gives the [68-95-99.7 shares](/statistics/normal-distribution/#the-68-95-997-rule) and the [standardizing step](/statistics/normal-distribution/#formal-definition).

**Reading a printed table.** Many tables, including Grinstead and Snell's Appendix A (p. 500), give the area from 0 to z, not the share below z <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. For z = 0.75 such a table reads .2734; add the left half, 0.5, to get Φ(0.75) ≈ 0.7734. Other tables print Φ itself; the picture at the top of a table shows which kind it is.

**Going backward.** The inverse of Φ turns a share into z; Python has it as `NormalDist().inv_cdf` <Cite id="python-statistics" />. The 90th percentile, the height with 90% of the group below it, has z ≈ 1.2816, so under a normal model it is 134 + 1.2816 × 8 ≈ 144.25 cm for the nine-year-olds and 144 + 1.2816 × 12 ≈ 159.38 cm for the eleven-year-olds.

<SideNote>
The 77% describes a normal model of all nine-year-olds, not our 9 children: in the class itself, 6 of the 9 are below 140 cm, 6/9 ≈ 0.67.
</SideNote>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="z-scores of any list average 0 and have standard deviation 1" rigor="full">

1. $\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i = \frac{1}{\vsigma} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \vmu) = 0$, because the distances from the mean add up to 0 ([variance page](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#formal-definition)).
2. So the mean of the $z_i$ is 0.
3. $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i^2 = \frac{1}{\vsigma^2} \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \vmu)^2 = \frac{\vsigma^2}{\vsigma^2} = 1$.
4. Their mean is 0, so this average of squares is their variance, and their standard deviation is 1.
5. Check: our class's z-scores add up to 0, and their squares add up to $576/64 = 9$, which over 9 children is 1.

</StepThrough>

Grinstead and Snell set the same fact for a random variable as Exercise 12 of §6.2 (p. 264): the standardized random variable $(X - \vmu)/\vsigma$ has expected value 0 and variance 1 <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

<StepThrough title="New units, same z-scores" rigor="full">

1. Let $y_i = a x_i + b$ with $a \ne 0$.
2. The $y_i$ have mean $a\vmu + b$ and standard deviation $|a|\,\vsigma$ ([variance page](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#shifting-and-stretching-the-whole-class)).
3. So $\dfrac{y_i - (a\vmu + b)}{|a|\,\vsigma} = \dfrac{a}{|a|}\, z_i$.
4. For $a > 0$, such as centimeters to meters with $a = 1/100$, every z-score is unchanged.
5. For $a < 0$ every sign flips: "200 cm minus the height" ranks the children the other way.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="Why one table serves every normal group" rigor="full">

1. Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\vmu, \vsigma^2)$ with $\vsigma > 0$.
2. Because $\vsigma > 0$, $X \le x$ exactly when $(X - \vmu)/\vsigma \le (x - \vmu)/\vsigma$.
3. The left side is $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (the [Normal page's formal definition](/statistics/normal-distribution/#formal-definition)).
4. So $F_X(x) = P(X \le x) = \Phi\big((x - \vmu)/\vsigma\big)$.

</StepThrough>

| Rule | Formula | The 140 cm nine-year-old |
| --- | --- | --- |
| z from x | $z = (x - \vmu)/\vsigma$ | $(140 - 134)/8 = 0.75$ |
| x from z | $x = \vmu + z\,\vsigma$ | $z = 1$: $134 + 1 \times 8 = 142$ |
| Share below (normal model only) | $P(X \le x) = \Phi(z)$ | $\Phi(0.75) \approx 0.7734$ |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="The whole class at once">

Find every z-score of the eleven-year-olds: 150, 138, 161, 126, 147, 134, 158, 129 and 153 cm. The heights add up to 1,296, so the mean is 144 cm. The distances from 144 are +6, −6, +17, −18, +3, −10, +14, −15 and +9; their squares add up to 1,296, so σ = √(1,296 / 9) = 12 cm. Dividing by 12 gives 1/2, −1/2, 17/12, −3/2, 1/4, −5/6, 7/6, −5/4 and 3/4; the tallest, 161 cm, has z = 17/12 ≈ 1.42. Check: they add up to 0, and their squares add up to 9.

</WorkedExample>

(a) Which eleven-year-old height has z = 2? Use $x = \vmu + z\vsigma$: 144 + 2 × 12 = 168 cm, taller than anyone in the class. That is fine: a z-score can describe a height nobody has.

(b) Your turn: which height has z = −1.25 in each class?

<div slot="solution">

Nine-year-olds: 134 − 1.25 × 8 = 124 cm. Eleven-year-olds: 144 − 1.25 × 12 = 129 cm, which is child 8 of that class.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Find the slip" faded>

Four students work on the 140 cm nine-year-old.

(a) "(140 − 134) / 64 = 3/32 ≈ 0.094."

(b) "(140 − 144) / 8 = −0.5, so the child is below average."

(c) "`NormalDist.from_samples(heights).zscore(140)` gives 0.7071, so the board's 0.75 is wrong."

(d) "The table reads .2734 at z = 0.75, so about 27% of nine-year-olds are shorter than 140 cm."

What went wrong in each?

<div slot="solution">

(a) Divided by the variance, 64 cm², not by <Var name="sigma" /> = 8 cm; cm over cm² is not unitless. Correct: 6/8 = 0.75.

(b) Used the other class's mean. Measure each child against their own: (140 − 134)/8 = 0.75.

(c) A different convention, not an error. `from_samples` divides by n − 1, so the divisor is s = √72 ≈ 8.49 and 6/√72 = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071. `scipy.stats.zscore`, whose default is `ddof=0` <Cite id="scipy-zscore" />, and `StandardScaler` divide by n and give 0.75. Say which you used; the variance page explains the [two versions](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#two-versions-the-whole-group-or-a-sample).

(d) That table gives the area between 0 and z: 0.5 + 0.2734 = 0.7734, about 77%.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={4} title="A share between two heights" faded>

Under a normal model for the nine-year-olds (mean 134 cm, standard deviation 8 cm), what share is between 130 and 146 cm?

<div slot="solution">

The z-scores are −0.5 and 1.5, so the share is Φ(1.5) − Φ(−0.5) ≈ 0.9332 − 0.3085 = 0.6247. With a "0 to z" table, the area from −0.5 to 0 equals the area from 0 to 0.5: .4332 + .1915 = .6247.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={5} title="Standardize a new house with the training numbers" faded>

A model's training houses have mean area 120 m² and standard deviation 30 m². A scaler turns each input into its z-score with the training numbers. (a) What does it send to the model for a new 75 m² house? (b) Which area becomes 2.5? (c) A classmate refits the scaler on new houses with mean 90 m² (standard deviation still 30 m²) and gets a different input for the same house. Why is that wrong?

<div slot="solution">

(a) (75 − 120)/30 = −1.5. (b) 120 + 2.5 × 30 = 195 m². (c) Refitting gives (75 − 90)/30 = −0.5, so the same house reaches the model as a different input. The model learned on the training scale; `StandardScaler` stores the training mean and standard deviation at `fit` and reuses them in `transform` <Cite id="sklearn-standardscaler" />. The numbers are illustrative.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Where z-scores show up in ML

### Standardizing features with the training set's numbers

scikit-learn's `StandardScaler` computes z = (x − u)/s, where u and s are the mean and standard deviation of the training samples, stored at `fit` and reused in `transform`, so new data are measured against the training group, as in worked example 5. Its s is our σ, dividing by n, and a feature with zero variance keeps scale 1 <Cite id="sklearn-standardscaler" />. The variance page compares both versions of s for [feature scaling](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#feature-scaling).

### Normalization layers compute z-scores inside the network

Batch normalization standardizes each activation with its mini-batch's mean and variance (Algorithm 1, p. 3) <Cite id="ioffe2015" />, and layer normalization uses the mean and standard deviation over one layer's hidden units for a single training case <Cite id="ba2016" />. The Normal page shows [batch normalization as a z-score](/statistics/normal-distribution/#batch-normalization), and the variance page shows [which variance each one uses](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#batch-normalization-uses-both-versions).

import math
import statistics
from fractions import Fraction
from statistics import NormalDist


def mean(xs):
    return sum(xs, Fraction(0)) / len(xs)


def variance(xs):
    mu = mean(xs)
    return sum(((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs), Fraction(0)) / len(xs)


def exact_root(q):
    """The exact square root of a fraction whose top and bottom are perfect squares, else None."""
    top, bottom = math.isqrt(q.numerator), math.isqrt(q.denominator)
    return Fraction(top, bottom) if top * top == q.numerator and bottom * bottom == q.denominator else None


def sigma(xs):
    """Divide by n. Exact when the variance is the square of a fraction, else a float."""
    var = variance(xs)
    if var == 0:
        raise ValueError("a z-score needs spread, but σ is 0")
    root = exact_root(var)
    return root if root is not None else math.sqrt(var)


def z(x, xs):
    return (x - mean(xs)) / sigma(xs)


def phi(value):
    """The share of a standard normal at or below value, checked against the erf formula."""
    share = NormalDist().cdf(value)
    assert abs(share - 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(value / math.sqrt(2)))) < 1e-12
    return share

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="A 118 cm nine-year-old and a 120 cm eleven-year-old: which is shorter for their age?">
Neither. Both have z = −2: (118 − 134)/8 = −16/8 and (120 − 144)/12 = −24/12. The eleven-year-old is 24 cm below a class whose standard deviation is 12 cm, which is the same number of standard deviations as 16 cm below a class with 8 cm.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Which height in each class has z = 0.5?">
138 cm for the nine-year-olds (134 + 0.5 × 8) and 150 cm for the eleven-year-olds (144 + 0.5 × 12). 150 cm is the board's eleven-year-old, so a 138 cm nine-year-old is exactly as tall for their age.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Under a normal model for the eleven-year-olds (mean 144 cm, standard deviation 12 cm), about what share is shorter than 156 cm?">
About 84%. The z-score is (156 − 144)/12 = 1, and Φ(1) ≈ 0.8413.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that if every height is written in meters, every z-score stays the same.">
Every height is multiplied by 1/100, so the mean and $\vsigma$ are too. Then $z = (x/100 - \vmu/100)/(\vsigma/100) = (x - \vmu)/\vsigma$, because the factor 1/100 cancels. This is the second derivation in the formal definition with $a = 1/100$ and $b = 0$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="The taller child is taller for their age.">
    Compare z-scores: 0.75 for the nine-year-old against 0.5 for the taller eleven-year-old.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A z-score of 1 always means about 84% are below.">
    Only under a normal model, where Φ(1) ≈ 0.8413. In our class the 142 cm child has z = 1 and 7 of the 9 children are below, 7/9 ≈ 78%.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Any standard deviation will do.">
    Dividing by n and by n − 1 give 0.75 and about 0.7071 for the same child. Use one version throughout, and say which.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Standardize the test data with its own mean and standard deviation.">
    Use the training set's mean and standard deviation for all later data, as `StandardScaler` does with `fit` and `transform`.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
