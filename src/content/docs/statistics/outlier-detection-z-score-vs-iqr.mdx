## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Judge Middle is not always right. If three of the nine pumpkins were giants, the middle half would reach a giant and nothing would be flagged. And a flag only means look again: the giant may be a real prize-winner.">

Nine pumpkins are weighed at a fair. Eight weigh 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7 and 7 kg; the ninth weighs 30 kg. Two judges decide which pumpkins are unusual.

Judge Average works out the typical weight and how far pumpkins usually sit from it, then flags any pumpkin more than 3 of those usual distances away. Judge Middle looks only at the middle half of the line, from 5 kg to 7 kg, a gap of 2 kg, and flags anything more than one and a half gaps past it: above 10 kg or below 2 kg.

Do the two judges agree about the giant? That is the first question in the next section.

Judge Middle has a blind spot too. In a class's pocket money most children get a little and a few get much more, with no mistake anywhere, yet Judge Middle flags those few.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Before training, data are checked for values that look wrong: a sensor that reported 999, a price with an extra zero, a mislabeled example. The cheapest checks are these two rules, and they don't always agree about which entries look wrong. An IQR rule can flag hundreds of regular rows in a skewed feature such as latency.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Weigh the giant pumpkin

Judge Average uses the z-score rule: a pumpkin's z-score is its distance from the mean in standard deviations; |z| drops the sign. Judge Middle uses the IQR rule: the interquartile range, IQR, is the width of the middle half, and the rule flags anything more than 1.5 IQR past it.

Decide first, because the board shows each verdict as soon as you move the giant or press a preset. A 30 kg giant joins eight pumpkins of 4 to 7 kg. Will |z| > 3 flag it? If a second 30 kg giant replaces a 7 kg pumpkin, will |z| > 2 flag either giant? How heavy must one giant be before |z| > 3 flags it?

## Two yardsticks for "far from the rest"

### The z-score rule measures with the mean and σ

The z-score rule flags x when its z-score, z = (x − <Var name="mu" />)/<Var name="sigma" />, is above a cutoff in size: usually 3, sometimes 2 or 2.5. The cutoff is a convention. On normal data |z| > 2 happens about 4.55% of the time and |z| > 3 about 0.27% ([the 68-95-99.7 rule](/statistics/normal-distribution/#the-68-95-997-rule)). Rousseeuw and Hubert use 2.5 as their example cutoff, with s, the standard deviation that divides by n − 1, in place of <Var name="sigma" /> <Cite id="rousseeuw2017" />.

### The IQR rule measures with the quartiles

The first quartile Q₁ sits a quarter of the way along the sorted list and the third quartile Q₃ three quarters of the way; for the pumpkins they are the 3rd and 7th values, 5 and 7 kg ([box plots](/statistics/reading-box-plots-quartiles-iqr/) show them). The IQR rule flags x when it lies below Q₁ − 1.5 × IQR or above Q₃ + 1.5 × IQR, where IQR = Q₃ − Q₁. These cutoffs are the fences; a value on a fence is not flagged. NIST calls a point beyond an inner fence a mild outlier, and one beyond an outer fence, at 3 × IQR, an extreme one <Cite id="nist-outlier-fences" />. The pumpkins' outer fences are −1 and 13 kg, so a 30 kg giant is extreme and an 11 kg one mild.

On normal data the rules nearly agree. Q₃ sits 0.6745 standard deviations above the mean and Q₁ as far below, so the upper fence sits 0.6745 + 1.5 × 2 × 0.6745 = 4 × 0.6745 ≈ 2.70 standard deviations out, and the IQR rule acts like |z| > 2.70 and flags about 0.70% of values.

### Why a giant hides from z-scores

The giant is included in the mean and <Var name="sigma" /> that judge it, so the heavier it gets, the more it stretches its own ruler. Rousseeuw and Hubert call this masking: outliers can pull classical estimates so far that they no longer stand out <Cite id="rousseeuw2017" />. The [median is not pulled this way](/statistics/mean-vs-median-vs-mode/). The variance page shows [masking with a typo in five measurements](/statistics/variance-and-standard-deviation/#research-frontier).

The giant's z-score creeps toward a ceiling of √(n − 1) = √8 ≈ 2.83 that it never reaches (the fold below proves it). pandas' `std` divides by n − 1 by default <Cite id="pandas-std" />. With that s, every z-score shrinks by √((n − 1)/n) = √(8/9) ≈ 0.943, so the giant scores ≈ 2.65 and the ceiling is (n − 1)/√n = 8/3 ≈ 2.67. NIST warns that z-scores mislead on small samples because no z-score can pass (n − 1)/√n <Cite id="nist-outlier-detection" />. Two equal giants share the stretch: their ceiling is √((9 − 2)/2) = √3.5 ≈ 1.871, just above their z of ≈ 1.865 at 30 kg, so |z| > 2 never flags them, at any weight.

<GoDeeper id="most-z-can-reach" label="how far can a z-score go? a full proof">

<StepThrough title="k equal values can't pass √((n − k)/k)" rigor="full">

1. Take n values, k of them equal to a, with k at least 1 and smaller than n, and d = a − μ nonzero; σ divides by n.
2. The distances from the mean add up to 0, so the other n − k distances add up to −kd.
3. The mean of squares is at least the square of the mean, so their squares add up to at least (kd)²/(n − k).
4. So nσ² ≥ kd² + k²d²/(n − k) = kd² × n/(n − k).
5. Divide: z² = d²/σ² ≤ (n − k)/k, with equality only when the other values are all equal.
6. k = 1 gives √(n − 1), which is √8 for 9 pumpkins; k = 2 gives √3.5. With s, multiply by √((n − 1)/n): for k = 1 that is NIST's (n − 1)/√n.

</StepThrough>

The eight ordinary pumpkins are not all equal, so the bound is never reached, which is why the board says "can never".

</GoDeeper>

### When the IQR rule is fooled too

The quartiles resist a few outliers, not many, and assume a symmetric middle.

**Too many outliers.** Make both 7 kg pumpkins giants too: 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 30, 30, 30. Now Q₃ = 30, IQR = 25 and the upper fence is 67.5 kg, so nothing is flagged. The board holds two giants at most; the code checks this case. Rousseeuw and Hubert give the IQR a breakdown value of 25%, the share of outliers that can carry it arbitrarily far <Cite id="rousseeuw2017" />: 2 giants of 9 (22%) are survived, 3 of 9 (33%) are not.

**Skewed data.** The IQR rule is how a boxplot marks outliers, and the boxplot assumes symmetry, so on skewed data it flags many regular points; Rousseeuw and Hubert point to a boxplot adjusted for skewness <Cite id="rousseeuw2017" />. For exponential data, a common right-skewed shape for waiting times, about 4.81% of values are flagged, all past the upper fence (the lower fence is below 0), against 0.70% for normal data, 0.35% on each side.

**The quartile method.** NumPy numbers nine quantile methods in Hyndman and Fan's order, with 'linear', number 7, as the default, and adds four variations of it <Cite id="numpy-percentile" />. With one giant the five methods the second program checks all give the pumpkins 5 and 7, but worked example 2 shows a list where the verdict changes.

A third rule, the modified z-score, keeps the z-score's shape with robust parts: the median for the mean, and the MAD, the median distance from the median, for <Var name="sigma" />.

| | z-score rule | IQR rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Center and yardstick | Mean μ and standard deviation σ | Quartiles Q₁, Q₃ and the IQR |
| Flag when | \|z\| > 3 (or 2, 2.5) | Below Q₁ − 1.5 IQR or above Q₃ + 1.5 IQR |
| Outlier inside its own yardstick? | Yes, it moves μ and σ | Barely, until about a quarter of the data are outliers |

| Two 30 kg pumpkins | z ≈ 1.87 each: never flagged | Both flagged |
| Three 30 kg pumpkins | z ≈ 1.41 each: never flagged | Fence at 67.5 kg: nothing flagged |
| Largest possible \|z\| with 9 values | √8 ≈ 2.83 with σ, 8/3 ≈ 2.67 with s | No ceiling |
| Share of normal data flagged | 0.27% at 3, 4.55% at 2 | About 0.70% (fence ≈ 2.70σ) |
| Assumes | Roughly normal data, n large enough | A symmetric middle and a stated quartile method |
| Robust upgrade | Median and MAD (the modified z-score) | A boxplot adjusted for skewness |

<GoDeeper id="median-and-mad" label="a third rule: the median and the MAD">

NIST gives Iglewicz and Hoaglin's modified z-score, Mᵢ = 0.6745 (xᵢ − median)/MAD, and their advice to flag |Mᵢ| > 3.5 <Cite id="nist-outlier-detection" />.

With one, two or three giants the pumpkins' median stays 6 and their MAD stays 1, so every giant scores 0.6745 × 24 = 16.188 and is flagged, even where both other rules fail. Rousseeuw and Hubert's robust score multiplies the MAD by 1.4826 instead <Cite id="rousseeuw2017" />, and 0.6745 ≈ 1/1.4826.

</GoDeeper>

### When to use which

- **Large, roughly symmetric data, one suspect at most:** |z| > 3 is fine.
- **Small samples or several suspects:** use the IQR rule or the modified z-score. The ceiling passes 3 only from 11 values on, so with 10 or fewer |z| > 3 never fires, with <Var name="sigma" /> or s.
- **Skewed features such as latencies:** log-transform first, or use an adjusted boxplot.
- **Always:** a flag starts a check. Outliers may be errors, or "valuable nuggets of information" <Cite id="rousseeuw2017" />.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["data-value", "sample-size", "mu", "sigma", "sample-sd", "z", "absolute-value", "median", "quantile-level", "quantile-position", "first-quartile", "third-quartile", "iqr", "fences", "mad", "modified-z"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be real numbers with $n \ge 2$, mean $\vmu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} x_j$ and $\vsigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} (x_j - \vmu)^2 > 0$.

**z-score rule.** For a cutoff such as 2, 2.5 or 3, $x_i$ is flagged when $|z_i|$ is above it, where

$$
z_i = \frac{x_i - \vmu}{\vsigma}.
$$

With $s$ in place of $\vsigma$, every $z_i$ shrinks by $\sqrt{(n - 1)/n}$.

**IQR rule.** With $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ from NumPy's 'linear' method (position $h = (n - 1)p$ in the sorted list, counting from 0, interpolating) and $\operatorname{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$, $x_i$ is flagged when

$$
x_i < Q_1 - 1.5\,\operatorname{IQR} \quad \text{or} \quad x_i > Q_3 + 1.5\,\operatorname{IQR}.
$$

Boundary cases. If every value is equal, $\vsigma = 0$, $z$ is undefined and the IQR rule flags nothing. A value on a fence is not flagged. No $|z_i|$ passes $\sqrt{n - 1}$, and it equals it only when the other values are all equal, so a cutoff such as 3 never fires when $n - 1 \le 3^2$, that is, with 10 or fewer values. With $\operatorname{IQR} = 0$ every value off $Q_1$ is flagged: in 5, 5, 5, 5, 6 the 6 is flagged.

</PreciseStatement>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="Eight seedlings, the z-score rule">

Seedling heights in cm: 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9. The mean is $40/8 = 5$. The squared distances 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4 and 16 add up to 32, so $\vsigma^2 = 4$ and $\vsigma = 2$. The tallest seedling has $z = (9 - 5)/2 = 2$ exactly: not flagged at $|z| > 2$, because the rule is strict. The shortest has $z = -1.5$. With $s^2 = 32/7$ the tallest scores only $z \approx 1.87$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="The same seedlings, the IQR rule">

With NumPy's 'linear' method, $Q_1$ sits at position $1.75$, between 4 and 4, so $Q_1 = 4$; $Q_3$ sits at position $5.25$, a quarter of the way from 5 to 7, so $Q_3 = 5.5$. Then $\operatorname{IQR} = 1.5$ and the fences are 1.75 and 7.75: the 9 cm seedling is flagged, the 2 cm one is not. NumPy's 'weibull' method, number 6 <Cite id="numpy-percentile" />, counts positions from 1, so $Q_3$ sits at position $(8 + 1) \times 0.75 = 6.75$, between the 6th and 7th values: $Q_3 = 5 + 0.75 \times (7 - 5) = 6.5$, an upper fence of 10.25, and no flag. Same data, same rule, a different verdict: always say which quartile method you used.

</WorkedExample>

A model server logs nine response times in ms: 120, 125, 125, 130, 130, 130, 135, 900, 950. Find (a) the mean and $\vsigma$; (b) the z-scores of 900 and 950; (c) the quartiles and fences; (d) which rule flags what, and why.

<div slot="solution">

(a) The mean is 305 ms and $\vsigma^2 = 329{,}950/3$, so $\vsigma \approx 331.6$ ms. (b) $z(900) \approx 1.79$ and $z(950) \approx 1.94$: neither passes $|z| > 2$, because the slow requests mask each other like the two giants. (c) $Q_1 = 125$, $Q_3 = 135$, $\operatorname{IQR} = 10$, fences 110 and 150. (d) The IQR rule flags both, and so does the modified z-score: median 130, MAD 5, and $M = 0.6745 \times 770/5 \approx 103.9$ for 900 ms. Whether they are errors or a real slow path is a question for the logs.

## Outlier rules in machine learning

### Cleaning training data, one feature at a time

A per-feature screen with either rule is a standard first check, and masking lets a z-score screen pass several corrupted entries. Rousseeuw and Hubert also name swamping, where an outlier-pulled fit makes good points look outlying <Cite id="rousseeuw2017" />. So flag, then inspect; never delete automatically.

### Robust scaling uses the IQR as its yardstick

scikit-learn's `RobustScaler` subtracts the median and divides by the IQR by default, because outliers can distort the mean and variance <Cite id="sklearn-robustscaler" />. It turns the 30 kg pumpkin into (30 − 6)/2 = 12, where standardizing gives ≈ 2.81. Comparing scalers belongs to [standardization vs min-max scaling](/statistics/standardization-vs-min-max-scaling/).

### Beyond one feature: outlier and novelty detection

scikit-learn separates outlier detection, where the training data contain outliers, from novelty detection, where they are clean and the question is whether a new point is an outlier <Cite id="sklearn-outlier-detection" />. `IsolationForest` isolates points with random splits, and anomalies get shorter paths <Cite id="sklearn-outlier-detection" />.

## Check both rules in code

The first program checks every number on this page with exact fractions.

<PyRunner
  title="outlier_rules.py"
  code={`
import math
import random
import statistics
from fractions import Fraction


def mean(values):
    return Fraction(sum(values), len(values))


def pop_var(values):
    """sigma squared: divide by n, like np.std."""
    mu = mean(values)
    return sum(((x - mu) ** 2 for x in values), Fraction(0)) / len(values)


def sample_var(values):
    """s squared: divide by n - 1, like pandas."""
    return pop_var(values) * Fraction(len(values), len(values) - 1)


def z_squared(x, values):
    """The exact square of the z-score of x, with sigma."""
    sigma2 = pop_var(values)
    if sigma2 == 0:
        raise ValueError("z is undefined: every value is equal, so sigma = 0")
    return (x - mean(values)) ** 2 / sigma2


def z_value(x, values):
    """The z-score as a float, with its sign."""
    return math.copysign(math.sqrt(z_squared(x, values)), x - mean(values))


def at_position(ordered, h):
    """The value at position h of a sorted list (counting from 0), interpolating."""
    low = math.floor(h)
    if low >= len(ordered) - 1:
        return Fraction(ordered[-1])
    return ordered[low] + (h - low) * (ordered[low + 1] - ordered[low])


def quantile_linear(values, p):
    """NumPy's default 'linear' method (Hyndman and Fan type 7): position (n - 1)p."""
    return at_position(sorted(values), (len(values) - 1) * Fraction(p))


def quantile_weibull(values, p):
    """NumPy's 'weibull' method (type 6): position (n + 1)p counting from 1."""
    h = (len(values) + 1) * Fraction(p) - 1
    return at_position(sorted(values), max(h, Fraction(0)))


def fences(values, k=Fraction(3, 2), quantile=quantile_linear):
    q1, q3 = quantile(values, Fraction(1, 4)), quantile(values, Fraction(3, 4))
    return q1 - k * (q3 - q1), q3 + k * (q3 - q1)


def iqr_flags(values, quantile=quantile_linear):
    """The values strictly beyond a fence: on the fence is not flagged."""
    low, high = fences(values, quantile=quantile)
    return [x for x in values if x < low or x > high]


def z_flags(values, cutoff):
    return [x for x in values if z_squared(x, values) > cutoff * cutoff]


def raw_mad(values):
    middle = statistics.median(values)
    return statistics.median(abs(x - middle) for x in values)


def modified_z(x, values):
    """NIST's modified z-score: 0.6745 (x - median) / MAD, with the raw MAD."""
    return Fraction(6745, 10000) * (x - statistics.median(values)) / Fraction(raw_mad(values))


def pumpkins(giant, giants):
    """The board's pumpkins: a second giant replaces a 7 kg pumpkin."""
    base = [4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7]
    if giants == 1:
        return base + [giant]
    if giants == 2:
        return base[:-1] + [giant, giant]
    raise ValueError("the board holds 1 or 2 giants")


assert pumpkins(8, 1) == [4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8]
assert pumpkins(30, 2) == [4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 30, 30]

# One giant of 30 kg among 9 pumpkins.
one = pumpkins(30, 1)
mu, sigma2 = mean(one), pop_var(one)
assert mu == Fraction(76, 9) and round(float(mu), 2) == 8.44
assert sigma2 == Fraction(4772, 81) and round(math.sqrt(sigma2), 2) == 7.68
assert z_squared(30, one) == Fraction(9409, 1193) and 4 < z_squared(30, one) < 9
assert round(z_value(30, one), 2) == 2.81 and round(z_value(4, one), 2) == -0.58
assert quantile_linear(one, Fraction(1, 4)) == 5 and quantile_linear(one, Fraction(1, 2)) == 6
assert quantile_linear(one, Fraction(3, 4)) == 7
assert fences(one) == (2, 10) and fences(one, k=3) == (-1, 13)
assert iqr_flags(one) == [30] and z_flags(one, 2) == [30] and z_flags(one, 3) == []
print("one giant: mean", round(float(mu), 2), " sigma", round(math.sqrt(sigma2), 2), " z", round(z_value(30, one), 2), " fences", [int(f) for f in fences(one)])

# No giant (8 kg): nothing flagged.
plain = pumpkins(8, 1)
assert mean(plain) == 6 and pop_var(plain) == Fraction(4, 3)
assert max(z_squared(x, plain) for x in plain) == 3 and round(math.sqrt(3), 2) == 1.73
assert iqr_flags(plain) == [] and z_flags(plain, 2) == []

# The slider: 9 and 10 kg pass |z| > 2 but not the fence; 11 kg passes both.
assert z_squared(9, pumpkins(9, 1)) == Fraction(169, 38) and iqr_flags(pumpkins(9, 1)) == []
assert z_squared(10, pumpkins(10, 1)) == Fraction(289, 53) and iqr_flags(pumpkins(10, 1)) == []
assert z_squared(11, pumpkins(11, 1)) == Fraction(49, 8) and iqr_flags(pumpkins(11, 1)) == [11]
for g, shown in ((9, 2.11), (10, 2.34), (11, 2.47), (60, 2.82)):
    assert 4 < z_squared(g, pumpkins(g, 1)) < 9 and round(z_value(g, pumpkins(g, 1)), 2) == shown
assert z_squared(60, pumpkins(60, 1)) == Fraction(47089, 5903)

# The ceiling for one giant: z^2 stays below n - 1 = 8 with sigma, below (n - 1)^2 / n = 64/9 with s.
for g in range(8, 10001):
    zz = z_squared(g, pumpkins(g, 1))
    assert zz < 8 and zz * Fraction(8, 9) < Fraction(64, 9)
assert round(math.sqrt(8), 2) == 2.83 and round(8 / 3, 2) == 2.67
z_s_squared = z_squared(30, one) * Fraction(8, 9)

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Nine values, one of them very large. Can making it even larger ever push its |z| above 3, with σ?">
No. With 9 values no z-score can pass $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$, and it equals it only if the other eight are all equal. Making the value larger also drags the mean and $\vsigma$ along, so its z-score only creeps toward the ceiling.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Values 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 20. With NumPy's linear quartiles, which values does the IQR rule flag? Does |z| > 2 flag the 20?">
$Q_1 = 4$, $Q_3 = 6$, $\operatorname{IQR} = 2$, so the fences are 1 and 9 and only 20 is flagged. The mean is $58/9 \approx 6.44$ and $\vsigma^2 = 1928/81$, so $\vsigma \approx 4.88$ and $z(20) \approx 2.78$: flagged at $|z| > 2$, not at 3. The rules agree here because there is one outlier, and it doesn't stretch $\vsigma$ enough to hide.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Two equal giants among 9 values: what is the largest z either can have, with σ?">
$\sqrt{(9 - 2)/2} = \sqrt{3.5} \approx 1.87$, and it is reached only if the other seven are all equal. So $|z| > 2$ never flags them.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that one value among n can't have z² ≥ n − 1, with σ, unless the other n − 1 values are all equal.">
Let the value be $a$ with $d = a - \vmu \ne 0$. The other $n - 1$ distances add up to $-d$, so their squares add up to at least $d^2/(n - 1)$, with equality only when they are all equal. Then $n\vsigma^2 \ge d^2 + d^2/(n - 1) = d^2\,n/(n - 1)$, so $z^2 = d^2/\vsigma^2 \le n - 1$. This is the proof in the fold above with $k = 1$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="|z| > 3 will catch any real outlier.">
    With 10 or fewer values it can never fire; the 30 kg pumpkin among 9 has z ≈ 2.81.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="If two values are outliers, the z-score rule flags both.">
    They inflate <Var name="sigma" /> together: two 30 kg giants among 9 get z ≈ 1.87 each.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The IQR rule can't be fooled.">
    Three giants among nine push the fence to 67.5 kg, and on exponential data it flags about 4.8% of regular values.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A value on the fence is an outlier.">
    Only points beyond a fence are flagged: 10 kg is not, 11 kg is.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Flagged means wrong, so delete it.">
    A flag means look again. An outlier may be an error or the most informative point in the data.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
