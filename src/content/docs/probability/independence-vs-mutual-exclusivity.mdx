## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="A day can be cloudy, so 'rain all day' and 'sun all day' can both be false: mutually exclusive is not the same as 'one or the other must happen'. And a picture can show you whether two events overlap, but not whether they are independent. That needs the numbers.">

Pick a day of a school holiday at random. "Rain all day" and "sun all day" can't both be true of the same day, so they are mutually exclusive. Now someone tells you it rained all day. The chance of sun all day drops to 0: the news rules it out completely. So the two events are far from independent: one tells you everything about the other.

Add a friend who tossed a coin that morning. Hearing that it rained tells you nothing about whether the coin came up heads. Rain and heads are independent, and they can easily happen together on the same day.

The weather picture only shows events that can't happen together. The coin shows the other case: events that happen together can still be independent.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Choosing between one softmax and one sigmoid per label is choosing between these two ideas. The wrong choice either forces tags that go together, such as "dog" and "outdoor", to compete, or scores each label alone when two labels always come as a pair.
</WhyThisMatters>

The overlapping pair is independent. The board says "Independent: yes.", "Mutually exclusive: no, A and B share 1 outcome, \{(6,1)\}." and "P(A and B) = 1/36, and P(A) × P(B) = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36: equal." Six rolls make 7, one from each first face, so learning that the first die is 6 leaves exactly one way in six.

The pair with no overlap is dependent: "Independent: no.", "Mutually exclusive: yes, A and B share no outcome." and "P(A and B) = 0, but P(A) × P(B) = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36 (≈ 0.03): not equal." Once you know the roll is a double, a sum of 7 is impossible. The chance of A once you know B happened, written P(A | B), drops to 0: "P(A | B) = 0, while P(A) = 1/6 (≈ 0.17)".

Overlapping pairs can also be dependent. Press Sum is at least 10 for A and First die is 6 for B. They share three rolls, and the board says "P(A and B) = 1/12 (≈ 0.08), but P(A) × P(B) = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36 (≈ 0.03): not equal." with "P(A | B) = 1/2 (0.50)". Finally press Sum is 7 for A and Clear for B, so B can't happen. The board says "Independent: yes, but only because B can't happen." The product test passes, 0 = 1/6 × 0, while the board also says "P(A | B) is undefined, because B can't happen.": there is nothing left to divide by.

Overlap is not the same as dependence. Whether two events overlap is visible in the grid. Whether they are independent needs the two numbers.

## Two different questions about the same pair of events

Mutual exclusivity asks whether A and B share an outcome. Independence asks whether knowing that B happened changes the probability of A. The [independence page](/probability/independence/) defines the second idea; here the two are set side by side.

| | Mutually exclusive | Independent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | A ∩ B = ∅ (no shared outcome) | P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B) |
| Depends on | Only the sets | The probabilities |
| P(A and B) | 0 | P(A) P(B) |
| P(A or B) | P(A) + P(B) | P(A) + P(B) − P(A) P(B) |
| Learning B happened, P(B) > 0 | P(A \| B) = 0 | P(A \| B) = P(A) |
| Seen in the grid? | Yes: no shared square | No: you must compute |
| Dice | Doubles and sum 7 | First die 6 and sum 7 |
| Cards | Hearts and spades | King and diamond |
| ML | Softmax classes | One sigmoid per label, given the input |

The "or" row comes from [adding, then taking the overlap off once](/probability/probability-axioms/#a-or-b-add-then-take-the-overlap-off-once). For doubles and sum 7 it gives 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3; for first die 6 and sum 7, 1/6 + 1/6 − 1/36 = 11/36.

### Exclusive events with positive probability are always dependent

If A and B can't happen together, P(A ∩ B) = 0, but P(A) P(B) is above 0 whenever both events are possible. So the product test fails, and learning that B happened drops P(A) to 0. Kelly and Zwiers call the concept of mutually exclusive events, except in certain pathological cases, "the exact antithesis of the concept of independent events" (§3) <Cite id="kelly1986" />.

### Independent events with positive probability must overlap

The same fact read backward: if A and B are independent and both possible, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B) > 0, so they share at least one outcome. First die 6 and second die 6 share (6,6), with 1/36 = 1/6 × 1/6.

### When are they both true?

Only when P(A) = 0 or P(B) = 0. Grinstead and Snell's Definition 4.1 counts two events as independent when at least one of them has probability 0 (p. 139) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. An impossible event is exclusive of and independent of everything, which is why the board's Clear gives both verdicts.

### When to use which

- Use exclusivity to add probabilities, as in [events that can't happen together add up](/probability/probability-axioms/#events-that-cant-happen-together-add-up), and for one-of-K labels.
- Use independence to multiply probabilities: separate dice, coins or devices, and draws with replacement.
<SideNote>De Moivre defined independent events as ones where "the happening of one neither forwards nor obstructs" the other (quoted in Grinstead and Snell, p. 148) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. Exclusive events obstruct each other completely.</SideNote>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["sample-space", "event-collection", "event", "intersection", "union", "empty-set", "probability", "conditional", "independent", "sigmoid"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space and $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$.

(i) $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, or disjoint, if $A \cap B = \emptyset$. Then $P(A \cap B) = 0$ and $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

(ii) $A$ and $B$ are independent, written $A \indep B$, if $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. If $P(B) > 0$, this is equivalent to $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$.

Boundary cases: an event of probability 0 is independent of every event, itself included, and $A$ is independent of itself exactly when $P(A) \in \{0, 1\}$. Exclusive does not mean complementary. For three or more events, independence of every pair is not enough; see the [independence page](/probability/independence/).

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="Exclusive and independent forces a probability of 0" rigor="full">

1. Assume $A \cap B = \emptyset$ and $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$.
2. Then $P(A \cap B) = P(\emptyset) = 0$.
3. So $P(A)\,P(B) = 0$.
4. A product of two real numbers is 0 only if one of them is, so $P(A) = 0$ or $P(B) = 0$.
5. Contrapositive: if $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$, exclusive events are dependent, and independent events overlap with $P(A \cap B) > 0$.

</StepThrough>

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="Hearts and spades">

Draw one card from 52. $P(\text{heart}) = P(\text{spade}) = 13/52 = 1/4$. No card is both, so $P(\text{both}) = 0$ against $1/4 \times 1/4 = 1/16$: exclusive and dependent, with $P(\text{heart} \mid \text{spade}) = 0$. And $P(\text{heart or spade}) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2$, so half the deck is neither.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="A king and a diamond">

$P(\text{king}) = 4/52 = 1/13$, $P(\text{diamond}) = 13/52 = 1/4$, and $P(\text{king of diamonds}) = 1/52 = 1/13 \times 1/4$: they overlap and are independent. Among the 13 diamonds, one is a king, so $P(\text{king} \mid \text{diamond}) = 1/13 = P(\text{king})$. Molnar reports a teacher calling exactly this pair dependent because the king of diamonds is in both <Cite id="molnar2016" />.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Three tags on 200 photos" faded>

Of 200 photos, 60 show a dog, 120 are outdoor and the other 80 indoor, 20 are beach photos (all outdoor), and 36 show a dog outdoors. Pick a photo at random. For each pair, dog and outdoor, beach and outdoor, indoor and outdoor, say whether it is exclusive and whether it is independent.

<div slot="solution">

- Dog and outdoor: $36/200 = 0.18 = 0.3 \times 0.6$, so they overlap and are independent, with $P(\text{outdoor} \mid \text{dog}) = 36/60 = 3/5 = P(\text{outdoor})$.
- Beach and outdoor: $20/200 = 0.1$ against $0.1 \times 0.6 = 0.06$, so they overlap and are dependent, with $P(\text{outdoor} \mid \text{beach}) = 1$.
- Indoor and outdoor: $0$ against $0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.24$, so they are exclusive and dependent.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Exclusive classes and independent labels in ML

A classifier's output layer commits to one of the two ideas. The [probability axioms page](/probability/probability-axioms/#softmax-outputs) shows why softmax outputs form one distribution and why [one sigmoid per label](/probability/probability-axioms/#one-sigmoid-per-label) does not; this section uses the same logits, the raw scores the model gives before softmax or sigmoid: $(2, 1, 0)$ for cat, dog and bird, to look at dependence.

### Softmax: classes that exclude each other

Softmax turns the logits into $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, which add up to 1. "The class is cat" and "the class is dog" are mutually exclusive by construction, so for an input image $x$, $P(\text{cat and dog} \mid x) = 0$ and the classes are dependent. Raise the cat logit from 2 to 3 and the output becomes $(0.844, 0.114, 0.042)$: dog falls although its own score did not change. Goodfellow and coauthors note that because the outputs sum to 1, an increase in one must lower the others (§6.2.2.3) <Cite id="goodfellow2016" />.

### One sigmoid per label: labels that are independent given the input

The same logits through $\operatorname{sigmoid}(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ give $(0.881, 0.731, 0.500)$. Raise cat to 3 and the outputs become $(0.953, 0.731, 0.500)$: dog doesn't move. Treating each label as its own yes-or-no that ignores the others for this input means multiplying: $P(\text{cat and dog} \mid x) = 0.881 \times 0.731 = 0.644$, $P(\text{no label} \mid x) = 0.016$ and $P(\text{exactly one label} \mid x) = 0.178$. That is independence given the input $x$, which is [conditional independence](/probability/conditional-independence/), not independence over the whole dataset.

### When the labels are linked

Over a dataset, labels can be dependent, like beach and outdoor in worked example 3. scikit-learn describes multiclass classification as giving each sample one and only one label (§1.12.1), and fitting one binary classifier per label as treating each label independently (§1.12.2). Classifier chains instead combine binary classifiers into a model that can exploit correlations among targets (§1.12.2.3) <Cite id="sklearn-multiclass" />. Two sigmoid outputs can still rise together across many images, because they look at the same picture. What one sigmoid per label can't capture is a link between labels for one fixed image.

    return sum((weight[outcome] for outcome in event), F(0))

    """Exclusive comes from the sets; independent from the probabilities."""
    exclusive = len(a & b) == 0
    independent = prob(a & b, space, weight) == prob(a, space, weight) * prob(b, space, weight)
    return exclusive, independent

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Two events can't both happen. Does learning that one happened tell you anything about the other?">
Yes, everything: it rules the other out, so P(B | A) = 0. When both events are possible, that is as far from independent as two events can be.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="P(A) = 0.3 and P(B) = 0.5. Find P(A and B) and P(A or B) if A and B are mutually exclusive, then if they are independent.">
Exclusive: $P(A \cap B) = 0$ and $P(A \cup B) = 0.3 + 0.5 = 0.8$. Independent: $P(A \cap B) = 0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15$ and $P(A \cup B) = 0.8 - 0.15 = 0.65$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Toss a fair coin six times. 'The last two tosses are heads' and 'exactly three heads' clearly overlap. Are they independent?">
No. 16 of the 64 sequences end in two heads, so the chance is 1/4. Exactly three heads happens in 20 sequences, and only 4 of those end in two heads, so given three heads the chance is 4/20 = 1/5. Kelly and Zwiers use this example (§2) <Cite id="kelly1986" />.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that A is independent of itself exactly when P(A) is 0 or 1.">
$A \cap A = A$, so the product test reads $P(A) = P(A)^2$, that is $P(A)\,(1 - P(A)) = 0$. A product is 0 only when a factor is, so $P(A) = 0$ or $P(A) = 1$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Mutually exclusive events have nothing to do with each other, so they are independent.">
    One rules the other out. Doubles and sum 7 give $0$ against $1/36$. Kelly and Zwiers report that students very often confuse the two ideas (§3) <Cite id="kelly1986" />.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Events that overlap can't be independent.">
    First die 6 and sum 7 overlap and are independent, and so do a king and a diamond. In Molnar's study, "No, not mutually exclusive" was the most common wrong answer, given by 10 of 25 teachers <Cite id="molnar2016" />.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="You can see independence in the picture.">
    The fair and loaded die have the same sets and different verdicts. The picture shows overlap; independence needs the probabilities.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Independent means neither event causes the other.">
    Independence is a statement about probabilities only. Three of Molnar's teachers argued from a lack of effect <Cite id="molnar2016" />, and Kelly and Zwiers warn against reading dependence as cause (§2) <Cite id="kelly1986" />.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Mutually exclusive means one of them must happen.">
    A cloudy day is neither all rain nor all sun, and hearts and spades leave half the deck. Kelly and Zwiers stress that exclusive events need not be complementary (§1) <Cite id="kelly1986" />.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
