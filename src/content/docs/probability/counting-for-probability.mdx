## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="The three friends are all different. If two of them were twins in matching clothes, some photos would look the same and the count would drop. And a count only becomes a chance if every photo is equally likely, as when the order is drawn from a hat.">

Ana, Ben and Cai want a photo of the three of them standing in a row. Swap any two of them and you get a different photo. How many different photos can they take? Then the coach needs two of them for a team. A team of Ana and Ben is the same team as Ben and Ana. How many different teams are there?

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Many probabilities in machine learning are counts in disguise: how many ways a test set can be split off, or how many candidate rules a learner picks among, which sets how many examples it needs before you can trust its pick. But a count is only half a probability; the other half is checking that the outcomes are equally likely.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Line up three friends, then pick two

Keep your two answers from above. One more question before you check: if the order in the photo is drawn from a hat, what is the chance that Ana stands in the middle? If the team is drawn from a hat, what is the chance that Ana is on it? Are the two chances the same?

Your task: write down your two counts and whether Ana is more likely to be in the middle of the photo or on the team, then open the list and check that every photo appears exactly once and every team twice.

<Fold kind="answer" label="every photo and every team, listed">

| Photo (left, middle, right) | First two in line | The team they make |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A B C | A then B | \{A, B\} |
| B A C | B then A | \{A, B\} |
| A C B | A then C | \{A, C\} |
| C A B | C then A | \{A, C\} |
| B C A | B then C | \{B, C\} |
| C B A | C then B | \{B, C\} |

Any of the 3 can stand on the left, then 2 are left for the middle, then 1 for the right: 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 photos. The first two in line give 3 × 2 = 6 ordered pairs, but each team appears twice, so there are 3 teams.

The two chances differ. Ana is in the middle of 2 of the 6 photos, a chance of 1/3, and on 2 of the 3 teams, a chance of 2/3. Each is fair only because the hat makes every photo, and every team, equally likely.

</Fold>

Six photos but only three teams: putting things in order and choosing them give different counts, and the rest of this page builds on that.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["sample-space", "event", "cardinality", "probability", "item-count", "pick-count", "factorial", "falling-factorial", "binomial-coefficient"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $n$ and $k$ be whole numbers and a set hold $n$ different items; the first three counts take $k \le n$. Line-ups of all $n$: $n!$. Ordered picks of $k$ without repeats: $(n)_k = n!/(n-k)!$. Unordered picks of $k$, the subsets of size $k$: $\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$. Ordered picks of $k$ with repeats allowed: $n^k$.

Conventions: $0! = 1$, $(n)_0 = \binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1$, and $\binom{n}{k} = (n)_k = 0$ for $k > n$. On a finite sample space $\Omega$ of equally likely outcomes, $P(A) = |A|/|\Omega|$, with $A$ and $\Omega$ both counted ordered or both unordered.

</PreciseStatement>

## Two questions decide the count

Before counting, ask two questions: does the order of the picks matter, and can the same item be picked twice? The answers choose the formula.

| Order matters? | Repeats allowed? | Count | Example on this page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yes | yes | $n^k$ | a 3-dial lock: $10^3 = 1{,}000$ codes; two coins: $2^2 = 4$ |
| yes | no | $(n)_k = n!/(n-k)!$ | first and second player from 3 friends: $3 \times 2 = 6$ |
| no | no | $\binom{n}{k}$ | a team of 2 from 3 friends: 3 |
| no | yes | $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ (fold below) | two dice read without order: 21 results, but a double comes from 1 of the 36 ordered rolls and a mixed pair from 2 |

### Multiply the choices at each step

When a task happens in steps, and each step has the same number of choices whatever was chosen before, the number of ways is the product of the choices; this is the counting principle. A lock with 3 dials of 10 digits has 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 codes, and 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 of them repeat no digit. Grinstead and Snell state it for a task done in stages (§3.1) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

The choices themselves can change; only how many there are must stay the same. After Ana takes the left, the middle is Ben or Cai; after Ben takes the left, Ana or Cai. Either way there are 2.

<SideNote>
The counting principle multiplies numbers of choices; [the multiplication rule](/probability/conditional-probability/#the-multiplication-rule) multiplies probabilities. They meet on equally likely outcomes: for two red marbles from 3 red and 2 blue, 3/5 × 2/4 is 3 × 2 over 5 × 4.
</SideNote>

</Simpler>

### When order matters: permutations

A permutation is an arrangement in order: $n$ different items line up in $n!$ ways, and an ordered pick of $k$ of them can be made in $(n)_k = n!/(n-k)!$ ways. Five children line up in 5! = 120 ways, and first and second place can go 5 × 4 = 20 = 5!/3! ways. The [birthday problem](/probability/birthday-problem/) puts $(n)_k$ to work. Grinstead and Snell prove both counts in §3.1 and write $(n)_k$, read "n down k" <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. And 0! = 1: there is one way to line up nobody.

### When order doesn't matter: combinations

A combination is a choice where order doesn't matter: the number of ways to choose $k$ items from $n$ is $\binom{n}{k}$, the ordered count divided by the $k!$ orders of each group. Teams of 2 from 5 children: 20 ordered picks over 2! = 2 gives 10. Teams of 3: 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 ordered picks over 3! = 6 gives 10 again, because choosing 3 to play is choosing 2 to sit out: $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$. Grinstead and Snell prove the formula by noting that each subset of size k can be put in order in k! ways (§3.2) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

<GoDeeper label="the fourth row: unordered picks with repeats" id="unordered-with-repeats">

Unordered picks of $k$ from $n$ with repeats number $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$. Two dice ($n = 6$, $k = 2$) give $\binom{7}{2} = 21$: 6 doubles and 15 pairs of different faces.

</GoDeeper>

### Same counting on top and bottom

A probability by counting is the number of favorable outcomes divided by the number of all outcomes, and both must be counted the same way. The rule P(A) = |A|/|Ω| holds only when [every outcome is equally likely](/probability/sample-spaces-and-events/#counting-works-only-when-outcomes-are-equally-likely).

Draw two marbles from a bag of 3 red and 2 blue, without putting the first back. Counted in order, 3 × 2 = 6 of the 5 × 4 = 20 ordered pairs are red-red: 6/20 = 3/10. Counted without order, $\binom{3}{2} = 3$ of the $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ pairs are red-red: 3/10 again. They agree because every unordered pair is exactly 2 ordered pairs, on top and on bottom alike.

The slip is to mix the two: 6 ordered pairs over 10 unordered pairs gives 3/5, twice too big.

Both counts are fair because a random draw makes every ordered pair equally likely, and so every unordered pair too, since each is 2 ordered pairs. That step fails for two dice read without order: a double such as two 6s is 1 of the 36 ordered rolls, while a 1 and a 2 is 2.

### Every subset, and Pascal's triangle

Adding $\binom{n}{k}$ over every $k$ from 0 to $n$ gives $2^n$, the number of subsets of $n$ items, because each item is either in or out. Three friends form 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8 = 2³ groups, from nobody to everyone. Grinstead and Snell note that each row of Pascal's triangle, where row $n$ lists $\binom{n}{0}$ through $\binom{n}{n}$, adds up to $2^n$ (§3.2) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

Without the empty group and the $n$ groups of one, $2^n - n - 1$ groups of two or more remain. [Mutual independence](/probability/independence/#three-or-more-events) needs one product equation for each such group, so 4 equations for three events and 11 for four. The same $2^n$ counts the networks [dropout](/probability/independence/#dropout-drops-each-unit-on-its-own) can make from $n$ units, since each unit is either kept or dropped.

<GoDeeper label="why each number is the sum of the two above it" id="pascals-rule">

For $0 < k < n$, Pascal's rule says $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1}$. Fix Ana. A team of $k$ either leaves her out, with all $k$ from the other $n - 1$, or includes her, with the other $k - 1$ from the $n - 1$. So $\binom{5}{2} = 6 + 4 = 10$. Grinstead and Snell prove it the same way, splitting on one element (§3.2) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

| $n$ | $\binom{n}{0}, \binom{n}{1}, \ldots, \binom{n}{n}$ | Sum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | 1 |
| 1 | 1, 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 1, 2, 1 | 4 |
| 3 | 1, 3, 3, 1 | 8 |
| 4 | 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 | 16 |
| 5 | 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1 | 32 |

</GoDeeper>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="Ordered picks: (n)ₖ = n!/(n − k)!" rigor="full">

1. Fill $k$ places in turn. The first has $n$ choices, the second $n - 1$ whatever was chosen, down to $n - k + 1$ for the $k$-th.
2. By the counting principle there are $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = (n)_k$ ordered picks.
3. Multiply top and bottom by $(n-k)!$: $(n)_k = n!/(n-k)!$. With $k = n$, $(n)_n = n!/0! = n!$, which is why $0! = 1$.
4. Check: $(5)_2 = 20 = 120/6$.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="Unordered picks: n choose k = (n)ₖ / k!" rigor="full">

1. Every ordered pick of $k$ items is one subset of size $k$ put in some order.
2. Each subset can be ordered in exactly $k!$ ways (the first derivation with $n = k$), and different subsets never give the same ordered pick.
3. So the $(n)_k$ ordered picks fall into groups of exactly $k!$, one per subset: $(n)_k = k! \binom{n}{k}$.
4. Divide by $k!$: $\binom{n}{k} = (n)_k / k! = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$. Check: $\binom{3}{2} = 6/2 = 3$ and $\binom{5}{3} = 60/6 = 10$.

</StepThrough>

Dividing works only because every group has the same size, $k!$. With repeated items it fails: "a 1 and a 1" has 1 order, not 2.

| Property | Statement | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ends of a row | $\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1$ | $\binom{3}{0} = \binom{3}{3} = 1$ |
| Symmetry | $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$ | $\binom{8}{3} = \binom{8}{5} = 56$ |
| Pascal's rule | $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1}$, for $0 < k < n$ | $10 = 6 + 4$ |
| Row sum | adding the whole row, $\binom{n}{0} + \cdots + \binom{n}{n} = 2^n$ | $1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8$ |
| Too many | $\binom{n}{k} = (n)_k = 0$ for $k > n$ | $\binom{3}{4} = 0$ |
| Pick a captain | $k \binom{n}{k} = n \binom{n-1}{k-1}$, for $1 \le k \le n$ | $3 \times 10 = 5 \times 6 = 30$ |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="Line-ups and teams of five">

Five children. Line-ups (ordered, all placed): 5! = 120. Gold and silver (ordered, no repeats): $(5)_2 = 20$. Teams of 2 (unordered): $\binom{5}{2} = 10$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="Two marbles from the bag" faded>

Draw 2 marbles from the bag of 3 red and 2 blue, without putting back. Step 1: all pairs, $\binom{5}{2} = 10$. Step 2: both red, $\binom{3}{2} = 3$ pairs, 3/10; both blue, 1 pair, 1/10. Your turn: the chance of one of each, and check that the three chances add up to 1.

<div slot="solution">

One of each: 3 × 2 = 6 pairs, so 6/10 = 3/5, and 3 + 1 + 6 = 10. The [multiplication rule](/probability/conditional-probability/#the-multiplication-rule) agrees on red-red: 3/5 × 2/4 = 3/10.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Exactly two heads in four coin flips" faded>

Flip 4 fair coins: 2⁴ = 16 equally likely sequences. Exactly 2 heads means choosing which 2 flips are heads. Your turn: how many sequences, what chance, and what chance of at least one head?

<div slot="solution">

$\binom{4}{2} = 6$: HHTT, HTHT, HTTH, THHT, THTH, TTHH. So 6/16 = 3/8. The row for 0 to 4 heads is 1, 4, 6, 4, 1, and at least one head is 1 − 1/16 = 15/16. Two coins give 1, 2, 1 out of 4, the chances on the [random variables page](/probability/random-variables-and-distributions/#equally-likely-outcomes-unequal-values). Unfair coins need the [binomial distribution](/probability/binomial-distribution/).

</div>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={4} title="Find the slip" faded>

(a) "Two dice show 21 results without order, so P(a 1 and a 2) = 1/21."

(b) "Red then red can happen 3 × 2 = 6 ways, out of $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ pairs, so two red marbles is 6/10."

(c) "There are 5!/3! = 20 teams of 2 from 5 children."

What went wrong in each?

<div slot="solution">

(a) The 21 results are not equally likely. Of the 36 ordered rolls, (1, 2) and (2, 1) count, so 2/36 = 1/18; two 6s is 1/36.

(b) Ordered on top, unordered below. Use 6/20 or 3/10, which are equal.

(c) 5!/3! counts ordered picks, so each team is counted twice: 10.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={5} title="Three children from the class of 12" faded>

The class from the [conditional probability page](/probability/conditional-probability/) has 12 children, 5 with hats. Pick 3 at random. What is the chance that exactly 2 wear hats, and that at least 1 does?

<div slot="solution">

There are $\binom{12}{3} = 220$ groups. Exactly 2 hats: 2 of the 5 and 1 of the other 7, 10 × 7 = 70 groups, so 7/22.

| Hats in the group | Groups |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | $\binom{7}{3} = 35$ |
| 1 | $5 \times \binom{7}{2} = 105$ |
| 2 | $\binom{5}{2} \times 7 = 70$ |
| 3 | $\binom{5}{3} = 10$ |
| Total | 220 |

At least one hat is 1 − 35/220 = 37/44. The 10 all-hat groups give 10/220 = 1/22, the same as the conditional page's 5/12 × 4/11 × 3/10.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={6} title="How many splits does leave-p-out take?" faded>

Leave-p-out cross-validation tests on every possible set of $p$ examples and trains on the rest. How many splits for 10 examples with $p = 2$, and for 100 with $p = 2$ and $p = 3$? In how many of the 10-example splits is a given example tested?

<div slot="solution">

A split is an unordered choice of $p$ test examples: $\binom{n}{p}$ splits. scikit-learn's example, 4 samples with $p = 2$, has $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ <Cite id="sklearn-leavepout" />. Then $\binom{10}{2} = 45$, $\binom{100}{2} = 4{,}950$ and $\binom{100}{3} = 161{,}700$. A given example is tested with each of the other 9, in 9 of the 45 splits, a share of 1/5 = p/n. Five-fold splitting cuts the 10 examples into 5 groups of 2 and tests each group once: only 5 splits.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Where counting shows up in machine learning

### Counting splits for cross-validation

Exhaustive cross-validation schemes are counting problems: leave-p-out uses every subset of $p$ examples as a test set, $\binom{n}{p}$ splits in all. scikit-learn's `LeavePOut` documentation warns that the number of iterations "grows combinatorically with the number of samples", suggests `KFold` or `ShuffleSplit` for large data sets, and notes that `LeavePOut(p)` is not `KFold(n_splits=n_samples // p)`, whose test sets never overlap <Cite id="sklearn-leavepout" />.

### Counting the rules a learner can choose from

The simplest guarantee that a learned rule works on new data depends on how many rules the learner could have picked, and only through the logarithm of that count.

<NotationKey symbols={["hypothesis-class", "training-size", "error-tolerance", "failure-chance"]} />

Take a finite set $\mathcal{H}$ of rules. The learner sees $m$ examples, drawn independently, and returns any rule with no mistakes on them. A rule is bad if its error rate on new data is above $\epsilon$. A fixed bad rule gets all $m$ examples right with probability below $(1 - \epsilon)^m$. The [union bound](/probability/probability-axioms/#formal-definition), P(A₁ or A₂ or …) ≤ P(A₁) + P(A₂) + ⋯, caps the chance that some bad rule fits them all at $|\mathcal{H}|(1-\epsilon)^m \le |\mathcal{H}|e^{-\epsilon m}$. The second step uses $1 - \epsilon \le e^{-\epsilon}$, which makes the bound easy to solve for $m$. Schapire's lecture notes (§1 and §5.1) turn this into enough examples to keep that chance at most $\delta$ <Cite id="schapire2003-cos511" />:

$$
m \ge \frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right).
$$

Finding $|\mathcal{H}|$ is counting. A rule like "feature 2 and feature 5 and feature 7", over $n$ yes-or-no features, is fixed by the subset of features it uses: $2^n$ rules. A yes-or-no function of $n$ features answers each of the $2^n$ inputs its own way: $2^{2^n}$ functions. The notes give these as $\ln|\mathcal{H}| = n \ln 2$ and $|\mathcal{H}| = 2^{2^n}$, and read the bound as Occam's razor (§5.2) <Cite id="schapire2003-cos511" />.

With $n = 10$, $\epsilon = 1/10$ and $\delta = 1/20$, the bound for the first class is met once $m \ge 10(\ln 1024 + \ln 20) \approx 99.27$, so 100 examples are enough. All functions of 10 features have $\ln|\mathcal{H}| = 1024 \ln 2 \approx 709.78$, and about 7,128 are enough: only 71 times as many, because the bound grows with the logarithm. It assumes some rule fits the data perfectly, and it says nothing about infinite classes (§5.1, §5.3) <Cite id="schapire2003-cos511" />.

## Check every count in code

This runs in your browser, lists the outcomes and checks every count and probability, the probabilities as exact fractions.

<PyRunner
  title="counting.py"
  code={`
import math
from collections import Counter
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations, combinations_with_replacement, permutations, product

# Nothing here is random, so there is no seed: every outcome is listed.
# math.perm and math.comb are only cross-checks; the listings are the evidence.


def count(outcomes):
    """How many outcomes an iterator lists."""
    return len(list(outcomes))


def prob(favorable, outcomes):
    """Favorable over all, for equally likely outcomes listed the same way."""
    if len(outcomes) == 0:
        raise ValueError("no outcomes to divide by")
    return Fraction(len(favorable), len(outcomes))


def falling(n, k):
    """(n)_k = n! / (n - k)!, and 0 when k > n."""
    return math.factorial(n) // math.factorial(n - k) if k <= n else 0


def choose(n, k):
    """n choose k = n! / (k! (n - k)!), and 0 when k > n."""
    return math.factorial(n) // (math.factorial(k) * math.factorial(n - k)) if k <= n else 0


# Ana, Ben and Cai: photos and teams.
friends = "ABC"
photos = list(permutations(friends))
assert len(photos) == 6 == math.factorial(3) == 3 * 2 * 1
middle_ana = [p for p in photos if p[1] == "A"]
assert middle_ana == [("B", "A", "C"), ("C", "A", "B")]
assert prob(middle_ana, photos) == Fraction(1, 3)
teams = list(combinations(friends, 2))
assert len(teams) == 3
assert prob([t for t in teams if "A" in t], teams) == Fraction(2, 3)
pairs = list(permutations(friends, 2))
assert len(pairs) == 6 == 3 * 2
assert set(Counter(frozenset(p) for p in pairs).values()) == {2}
print("photos:", len(photos), " teams:", len(teams), " Ana in the middle:", prob(middle_ana, photos))

# Every formula against a listing, for n up to 8 and k one past n.
for n in range(9):
    for k in range(n + 2):
        ordered = count(permutations(range(n), k))
        unordered = count(combinations(range(n), k))
        assert ordered == falling(n, k) == math.perm(n, k)
        assert unordered == choose(n, k) == math.comb(n, k)
        assert ordered == math.factorial(k) * unordered
# With repeats allowed the listings grow as n^k, so stop at k = 4.
for n in range(9):
    for k in range(5):
        assert count(product(range(n), repeat=k)) == n**k

# Boundaries and properties.
assert math.factorial(0) == 1 and falling(5, 5) == math.factorial(5)
assert count(combinations("ABC", 4)) == 0 == choose(3, 4) == falling(3, 4)
for n in range(9):
    assert choose(n, 0) == choose(n, n) == 1
    all_groups = sum(count(combinations(range(n), k)) for k in range(n + 1))
    assert sum(choose(n, k) for k in range(n + 1)) == 2**n == all_groups
    assert sum(choose(n, k) for k in range(2, n + 1)) == 2**n - n - 1
    for k in range(n + 1):
        assert choose(n, k) == choose(n, n - k)
        if 0 < k < n:
            assert choose(n, k) == choose(n - 1, k) + choose(n - 1, k - 1)
        if k >= 1:
            assert k * choose(n, k) == n * choose(n - 1, k - 1)
assert 2**3 - 3 - 1 == 4 and 2**4 - 4 - 1 == 11
assert [choose(3, k) for k in range(4)] == [1, 3, 3, 1]
assert choose(10, 3) == choose(10, 7) == 120
assert choose(5, 2) == choose(4, 2) + choose(4, 1) == 6 + 4 == 10
assert 2 * choose(5, 2) == 5 * choose(4, 1) == 20
assert choose(8, 3) == choose(8, 5) == 56
assert 3 * choose(5, 3) == 5 * choose(4, 2) == 3 * 10 == 5 * 6 == 30
rows = [[choose(n, k) for k in range(n + 1)] for n in range(6)]
assert rows == [[1], [1, 1], [1, 2, 1], [1, 3, 3, 1], [1, 4, 6, 4, 1], [1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1]]
assert [sum(row) for row in rows] == [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32]
assert falling(5, 2) == 20 == 120 // 6 and choose(3, 2) == 6 // 2 and choose(5, 3) == 60 // 6 == 10

# The lock.
assert count(product(range(10), repeat=3)) == 1000
assert count(permutations(range(10), 3)) == 720 == 10 * 9 * 8

# Worked example 1: five children.
children = range(5)

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Is the number of ways to choose 3 books from 10 the same as the number of ways to choose 7?">
Yes, 120 both ways. Choosing 3 books to take is the same as choosing the 7 to leave, so every choice of 3 matches exactly one choice of 7: $\binom{10}{3} = \binom{10}{7}$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A code uses 4 different digits from 0 to 9. How many codes are there, and what is the chance that a random one is in increasing order?">
$(10)_4 = 10 \times 9 \times 8 \times 7 = 5{,}040$ codes. Each set of 4 digits appears in $4! = 24$ orders and exactly one of them is increasing, so 210 codes are increasing and the chance is $210/5{,}040 = 1/24$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Five fair coins: what is the chance of exactly 3 heads?">
$\binom{5}{3} = 10$ of the $2^5 = 32$ equally likely sequences have exactly 3 heads, so the chance is $10/32 = 5/16$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that k × (n choose k) = n × (n − 1 choose k − 1) by counting teams with a captain two ways.">
Pick the team first, then its captain: $\binom{n}{k}$ teams times $k$ captains. Or pick the captain first, then the other $k - 1$ players from the $n - 1$ left: $n \binom{n-1}{k-1}$. Both count the same teams-with-captain, so they are equal. For 5 children and teams of 2: $10 \times 2 = 5 \times 4 = 20$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Use combinations whenever you pick some items.">
    First ask whether order matters. Gold and silver for 2 of 3 friends is ordered, 3 × 2 = 6 ways; a team is not, 3.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Count the favorable outcomes one way and all outcomes another.">
    Top and bottom use the same convention. Ordered red-red over unordered pairs gives 6/10, not 3/10.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="If a list has 21 possible results, each has probability 1/21.">
    Counting needs equally likely outcomes, and unordered dice results are not. Count the 36 ordered rolls.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Add the choices at each step.">
    Steps multiply. A 3-dial lock has 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 codes, not 30.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The counting principle is the multiplication rule for probabilities.">
    One multiplies numbers of choices, the other multiplies probabilities. The probability rule always holds; a ratio of counts gives the same chance only on equally likely outcomes.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
