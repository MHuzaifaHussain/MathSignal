## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="The trick multiplies the clues as if knowing a fruit is yellow tells you nothing more about whether it is long, once you know it is a banana. In a real bowl that is only roughly true. It also gives apple exactly 0 because no apple in the bowl was long, so one unseen clue rules a fruit out forever.">

A bowl holds 50 bananas, 30 lemons and 20 apples. A friend picks one in the dark and says: it is yellow, and it is long. Of the bananas, 4 in 5 are yellow and 9 in 10 long; of the lemons, 9 in 10 yellow and 1 in 10 long; of the apples, 1 in 5 yellow and none long.

Multiply each fruit's share of the bowl by one clue rate at a time. Banana: 0.5 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.36. Lemon: 0.3 × 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.027. Apple: 0.2 × 0.2 × 0 = 0. As shares of the total 0.387: banana 40/43 ≈ 0.930, lemon 3/43 ≈ 0.070, apple 0. "Yellow" alone gives only banana 40/71 ≈ 0.563, lemon 27/71 ≈ 0.380, apple 4/71 ≈ 0.056.

The blind spot is clues that repeat each other. Two friends tell you the same rumor; it feels like twice the evidence, but both heard it from one person, so it is one clue, and multiplying counts it twice.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Naive Bayes is a standard first baseline for text classification. It trains in one pass of counting, with far fewer numbers to estimate than a full table <Cite id="mitchell2020" />. Its probabilities are usually too close to 0 or 1, which matters whenever you act on the number itself, for example by flagging everything above 0.9 or by weighing it against a cost.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Count the same clue twice

Then you see the back half is wet too. Both halves always get wet together, so the back half tells you nothing new. Treat it as a second clue anyway: does rain become more likely than not? Sharper: the odds of rain are P(rain) / P(no rain); by what factor do they change?

## From Bayes' theorem to one multiplication per feature

Write y for the class (banana, spam) and x₁, …, xₙ for the n features, the clues (yellow, long, each word). Naive Bayes applies Bayes' theorem to every class and replaces the hard part, P(x₁, …, xₙ | y), the chance of seeing all the clues together, with the product P(x₁ | y) × … × P(xₙ | y).

With n yes-or-no features, a full table of P(x | y) for two classes needs 2(2ⁿ − 1) numbers: for n = 30, 2,147,483,646. Naive Bayes needs 2n = 60 for P(x | y), plus the class shares (§1.1 and §2.2, equation 1) <Cite id="mitchell2020" />.

The assumption is that the features are [conditionally independent](/probability/conditional-independence/) given the class: once you know y, one feature says nothing more about another. That is not the same as plain independence across the whole bowl. Counting the whole bowl, 71 fruits are yellow (40 + 27 + 4) and 48 are long (45 + 3 + 0), but 41 are both (38 bananas, 3 lemons). So P(yellow and long) = 0.41, against P(yellow) × P(long) = 0.71 × 0.48 = 0.3408: strongly linked. Within bananas they are close to independent: 38 of the 50 are both, 0.76, against 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Banana | 38 | 2 | 7 | 3 |
| Lemon | 3 | 24 | 0 | 3 |
| Apple | 0 | 4 | 0 | 16 |

Of the 41 yellow, long fruits, 38 are bananas: exactly 38/41 ≈ 0.927, against the naive 40/43 ≈ 0.930.

</Simpler>

## Training is counting

To train naive Bayes, count each class's share of the training set and how often each feature appears within each class: the maximum-likelihood estimates (§2.3, equations 6 and 8) <Cite id="mitchell2020" />.

One count of 0 vetoes a class: the apple's "long" rate of 0 makes its product 0 whatever else is seen. Add-one (Laplace) smoothing adds 1 to every count and adds the number of possible values to the total, here 2 (yes or no). The apple's "long" rate becomes (0 + 1)/(20 + 2) = 1/22 instead of 0, and the banana's "yellow" rate becomes (40 + 1)/(50 + 2) = 41/52. The 1 is called α. With α = 1 the bowl gives banana ≈ 0.909, lemon ≈ 0.086, apple ≈ 0.005 <Cite id="mitchell2020" />.

## Add logs instead of multiplying

Real classifiers add log probabilities, because a product of many small numbers rounds to zero: the product of 1,000 factors of 0.001 is exactly 0.0 in 64-bit floating point, while 1000 × log 0.001 = −6907.755 (natural log, as everywhere on this page; any base picks the same winner). Log is increasing, so the winner is unchanged: pick the y with the largest log P(y) + Σₖ log P(xₖ | y). For two classes this is the [odds form](/probability/bayes-theorem/#odds-form) of Bayes' theorem, one log likelihood ratio per feature.

## Right class, wrong probabilities

When features are correlated, naive Bayes counts the same evidence more than once, so its probabilities drift toward 0 and 1, while its pick of class often survives.

An exact example: classes + and − each have probability 1/2, and four yes-or-no features are each "yes" with probability 0.7 in class + and 0.3 in class −, independent given the class. Copy feature 1 so the classifier sees it several times, and score all 16 patterns. Each pattern is weighted by how often it occurs. When the classifier says exactly 1/2, it is scored as half right, as if it flipped a coin.

Accuracy is how often the chosen class is right. Mean confidence is the probability naive Bayes gives its chosen class, on average. Log loss and the Brier score are penalties for the probabilities (lower is better; a confident wrong answer costs most). AUC is the chance that a random + case scores higher than a random − case, counting ties as half.

| Feature 1 seen | Accuracy | Mean confidence | Log loss | Brier score | AUC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| once (no copy) | 0.784 | 0.784 | 0.429 | 0.138 | 0.874 |
| twice | 0.784 | 0.854 | 0.476 | 0.154 | 0.863 |
| 3 times | 0.742 | 0.885 | 0.600 | 0.189 | 0.847 |
| 5 times | 0.700 | 0.970 | 0.998 | 0.262 | 0.842 |

Seen twice, accuracy does not move: the only patterns whose class changes are the ties (two yeses, two nos), where the truth is exactly 1/2. Naive Bayes now picks a side there and is right half the time, the same as the coin. Confidence, log loss, Brier score and AUC all get worse. Accuracy first drops at three copies; AUC (ranking) falls with every copy. Take the pattern (yes, no, no, no). Its true P(+) is 9/58 ≈ 0.155, which naive Bayes matches when feature 1 is seen once. It says 3/10 at two copies, 1/2 at three, and 49/58 ≈ 0.845 at five: the wrong class. Calibration, or dropping redundant features, helps.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["class-label", "feature-vector", "probability", "conditional", "conditionally-independent", "product", "sum", "proportional", "argmax", "log", "pseudocount", "feature-count", "log-odds-weights", "density", "z"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $Y$ take values in a finite set of classes with $P(Y = y) > 0$ for each, and let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ be values of discrete features $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ that are mutually independent given $Y$:

$$
P(x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid y) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(x_k \mid y) \quad \text{for every } y \text{ and every } \mathbf{x}.
$$

Then for any $\mathbf{x}$ with $P(\mathbf{x}) > 0$,

$$
P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{P(y) \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(x_k \mid y)}{\sum_{y'} P(y') \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(x_k \mid y')} \propto P(y) \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(x_k \mid y),
$$

and under 0-1 loss the best prediction is $\arg\max_y P(y) \prod_{k} P(x_k \mid y)$. Continuous features use densities $p(x_k \mid y)$, normal ones in Gaussian naive Bayes. Without the assumption the formula still defines a classifier, not the true posterior. Boundary cases, with rates estimated from data: if every class scores 0 the posterior is 0/0, undefined; a class with a 0 factor is never predicted; ties are broken arbitrarily.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="The naive Bayes rule from Bayes' theorem" rigor="full">

1. Bayes' theorem over the classes, for $P(\mathbf{x}) > 0$, puts $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\,P(y)$ over a sum that does not depend on $y$.
2. The chain rule: $P(x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid y) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(x_k \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{k-1}, y)$, for a class with $P(\mathbf{x}, y) > 0$; a class with $P(\mathbf{x}, y) = 0$ gets posterior 0 on both sides, so the identity holds trivially.
3. Conditional independence given $y$ turns each factor into $P(x_k \mid y)$.
4. The argmax can ignore that sum.
5. log is strictly increasing, so $\arg\max_y \big[\log P(y) + \sum_k \log P(x_k \mid y)\big]$ picks the same class.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="Naive Bayes is linear in log-odds" rigor="full">

1. Take classes + and −, features $x_k \in \{0, 1\}$, $\theta_k = P(x_k = 1 \mid +)$ and $\phi_k = P(x_k = 1 \mid -)$, all strictly between 0 and 1, and $0 < P(+) < 1$.
2. Each likelihood is $\theta_k^{x_k} (1 - \theta_k)^{1 - x_k}$ in class + and the same with $\phi_k$ in class −.
3. Divide the two scores, take logs and collect the terms that multiply $x_k$:

$$
\log \frac{P(+ \mid \mathbf{x})}{P(- \mid \mathbf{x})} = b + \sum_{k=1}^{n} w_k x_k, \quad w_k = \log \frac{\theta_k (1 - \phi_k)}{\phi_k (1 - \theta_k)}, \quad b = \log \frac{P(+)}{P(-)} + \sum_{k=1}^{n} \log \frac{1 - \theta_k}{1 - \phi_k}.
$$

4. "Log-odds > 0" is a straight cut through feature space. Domingos and Pazzani show the same for any nominal attributes in the proof of their Theorem 4 (§6) <Cite id="domingos1997" />, and Ng and Jordan write the same sum as their equation 2 <Cite id="ng2001" />.

</StepThrough>

| Rule | Statement | Fruit or spam numbers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assumption | $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) = \prod_k P(x_k \mid y)$ | Banana: $0.8 \times 0.9 = 0.72$ |
| Posterior | Each class's score over the total | Banana: $40/43$ |
| Smoothing | $(N_{y,w} + \alpha) / (\text{words in } y + \alpha \cdot \text{vocabulary})$ | "win" in ham: $1/13$ |
| Log form | $\log P(y) + \sum_k \log P(x_k \mid y)$ | Spam minus ham: $\log 3 = 1.0986$ |
| Parameter count | $2n$ against $2(2^n - 1)$ | $n = 30$: 60 against 2,147,483,646 |
| Zero factor | The class can never win | Apple, with a "long" rate of 0 |

</FormalDefinition>

<GoDeeper label="why the best fit is the relative frequency" id="mle-is-counting">

<StepThrough title="Maximum likelihood gives the relative frequency" rigor="sketch">

1. Fix a class y with at least one training word, and let $N_{y,w}$ be word w's count in it. Maximize $\sum_w N_{y,w} \log P(w \mid y)$ over the words, subject to Σ P(w | y) = 1.
2. A Lagrange multiplier λ gives $N_{y,w} / P(w \mid y) = \lambda$ for every word with $N_{y,w} > 0$.
3. Summing fixes λ as the class's word total, so $P(w \mid y) = N_{y,w}$ / (all words in class y).
4. A count of 0 gives probability 0: the veto smoothing removes.

</StepThrough>

Mitchell's equation 7 adds l to the count of each of a feature's J possible values: lJ imagined examples spread evenly. It is a MAP estimate under a Dirichlet prior, and l = 1 is Laplace smoothing; this page writes α for l <Cite id="mitchell2020" />.

</GoDeeper>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="The fruit bowl, three classes">

The scores are banana $9/25$, lemon $27/1000$ and apple 0, totaling $387/1000$: naive Bayes gives $40/43$, $3/43$ and 0. Counting the bowl directly, 41 fruits are yellow and long: 38 bananas and 3 lemons, so exactly $38/41 \approx 0.927$ and $3/41$. Same winner.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="A five-email spam filter, with and without smoothing">

Spam: "win money now", "win prize now". Ham: "meeting now", "lunch money", "meeting notes". So $P(\text{spam}) = 2/5$ and $P(\text{ham}) = 3/5$. Spam counts win 2, money 1, now 2, prize 1; ham counts meeting 2, now 1, lunch 1, money 1, notes 1. Each class has 6 words and the vocabulary has 7 different words. With $\alpha = 1$ each word's count gets +1 and each total gets $1 \times 7$, so every denominator is $6 + 7 = 13$ and "win" in spam is $(2 + 1)/13 = 3/13$.

For "win money now", spam scores $\frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{3}{13} \cdot \frac{2}{13} \cdot \frac{3}{13} = 36/10985$ and ham $\frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{1}{13} \cdot \frac{2}{13} \cdot \frac{2}{13} = 12/10985$, so $P(\text{spam}) = 3/4$. In logs, $-5.7208$ against $-6.8194$, a gap of $\log 3 = 1.0986$. Unsmoothed, ham never saw "win" and scores 0 against spam's $1/135$, so $P(\text{spam}) = 1$.

For "win prize meeting", unsmoothed, spam never saw "meeting" and ham never saw "win", so both score 0 and the posterior is $0/0$, undefined. Smoothed, $12/10985$ against $9/10985$: $P(\text{spam}) = 4/7 \approx 0.571$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Gaussian naive Bayes: is a 12 cm yellow fruit a banana?" faded>

Banana lengths are normal with mean 18 cm and standard deviation 2 cm, lemon lengths with mean 8 cm and standard deviation 1 cm. Priors are 0.5 and 0.3, yellow rates 0.8 and 0.9. With the [normal density](/statistics/normal-distribution/), find P(banana) for a 12 cm yellow fruit, banana against lemon.

<div slot="solution">

Banana has $z = (12 - 18)/2 = -3$ and lemon $z = (12 - 8)/1 = 4$ (how many standard deviations 12 cm is from each mean), giving densities $0.002216$ and $0.0001338$. The scores are $0.5 \times 0.8 \times 0.002216 = 0.000886$ and $0.3 \times 0.9 \times 0.0001338 = 0.0000361$. In the ratio of densities the $\sqrt{2\pi}$ cancels, the standard deviations give $1/2$, and the exponents give $e^{-(-3)^2/2 + 4^2/2} = e^{-4.5 + 8}$, so the ratio is $\tfrac{1}{2} e^{3.5}$, so the odds are $\tfrac{20}{27} e^{3.5} \approx 24.53$, the log-odds $\log(20/27) + 3.5 \approx 3.1999$, and $P(\text{banana}) \approx 0.9608$.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Where naive Bayes is used in ML

### Text classification with word counts

Multinomial naive Bayes reads an email as its words $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, repeats included:

$$
\arg\max_y \Big[ \log P(y) + \sum_{k=1}^{n} \log P(x_k \mid y) \Big].
$$

Mitchell's §2.3 estimates each $P(x_k \mid y)$ by counting within the class <Cite id="mitchell2020" />; for words, with $N_{y,w}$ the count of word w in class y:

$$
P(w \mid y) = \frac{N_{y,w} + \alpha}{(\text{all words in class } y) + \alpha \cdot (\text{vocabulary size})}.
$$

### A linear classifier and its discriminative partner

Logistic regression fits the same linear log-odds form,

$$
\log \frac{P(+ \mid \mathbf{x})}{P(- \mid \mathbf{x})} = b + \sum_{k=1}^{n} w_k x_k,
$$

choosing b and the $w_k$ directly instead of from counts (§2, equation 3). Naive Bayes is generative (it models how each class produces features); logistic regression is discriminative (it models P(y | x) directly) (§1) <Cite id="ng2001" />. Its limiting error is never higher (Proposition 1), and it needs on the order of n examples to approach it (Proposition 2); naive Bayes's parameters settle after on the order of log n (Lemma 3), and under stated conditions its error follows (Theorem 4, Corollary 6) <Cite id="ng2001" />. On 15 UCI datasets naive Bayes usually led with few examples and logistic regression caught up or overtook it later; the few datasets where it never caught up were mostly very small (§4, Figure 1) <Cite id="ng2001" />. Gaussian naive Bayes in which each feature has the same variance in every class gives exactly the logistic form (§3.1) <Cite id="mitchell2020" />.

### Calibrating its scores

Calibration refits a model's scores so that a predicted 0.8 is right about 80% of the time. Naive Bayes needs it: models with unrealistic independence assumptions, such as naive Bayes, push predicted probabilities toward 0 and 1 (abstract) <Cite id="niculescu2005" />. Platt scaling passes a score $s$ through a sigmoid with two numbers $\gamma$ and $\delta$ fitted by maximum likelihood on a separate calibration set (§2.1, equation 1) <Cite id="niculescu2005" />:

$$
P(y = 1 \mid s) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\gamma s + \delta}}.
$$

A reliability plot compares predicted probability with how often the class really occurs. For naive Bayes it bends the opposite way from an S-shaped sigmoid curve, so Platt's S-curve is the wrong correction, and isotonic regression, which fits any increasing curve, does better (§4) <Cite id="niculescu2005" />.

## Train and score the classifier in code

This runs in your browser and checks every number on this page, with exact fractions where it can.

<PyRunner
  title="naive_bayes.py"
  code={`
import math
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product

import numpy as np


def readout(value):
    """Three decimals, as the board and the page show them."""
    return f"{float(value):.3f}"


def posterior(prior, likelihood, false_positive):
    """The board's math: P(A | B) by Bayes' theorem."""
    return likelihood * prior / (likelihood * prior + false_positive * (1 - prior))


def nb_scores(priors, rates, clues):
    """P(y) times the product of P(clue | y), for every class."""
    scores = {}
    for label, prior in priors.items():
        score = prior
        for clue in clues:
            score *= rates[label][clue]
        scores[label] = score
    return scores


def normalize(scores):
    total = sum(scores.values())
    if total == 0:
        raise ValueError("every class scores 0: the posterior is 0/0")
    return {label: score / total for label, score in scores.items()}


# The fruit bowl: shares, and the rates of each clue within each fruit.
priors = {"banana": Fraction(50, 100), "lemon": Fraction(30, 100), "apple": Fraction(20, 100)}
rates = {
    "banana": {"yellow": Fraction(40, 50), "long": Fraction(45, 50)},
    "lemon": {"yellow": Fraction(27, 30), "long": Fraction(3, 30)},
    "apple": {"yellow": Fraction(4, 20), "long": Fraction(0, 20)},
}
scores = nb_scores(priors, rates, ["yellow", "long"])
assert scores == {"banana": Fraction(9, 25), "lemon": Fraction(27, 1000), "apple": 0}
assert sum(scores.values()) == Fraction(387, 1000)
fruit = normalize(scores)
assert fruit == {"banana": Fraction(40, 43), "lemon": Fraction(3, 43), "apple": 0}
assert (readout(fruit["banana"]), readout(fruit["lemon"])) == ("0.930", "0.070")
yellow_only = normalize(nb_scores(priors, rates, ["yellow"]))
assert yellow_only == {"banana": Fraction(40, 71), "lemon": Fraction(27, 71), "apple": Fraction(4, 71)}
assert [readout(yellow_only[f]) for f in ("banana", "lemon", "apple")] == ["0.563", "0.380", "0.056"]
assert nb_scores(priors, rates, []) == priors  # no clues: the answer is the prior
print("fruit, naive:", fruit)

# The joint counts: (yellow and long, yellow and short, long not yellow, neither).
joint = {"banana": (38, 2, 7, 3), "lemon": (3, 24, 0, 3), "apple": (0, 4, 0, 16)}
for label, (yl, ys, nl, ns) in joint.items():
    size = priors[label] * 100
    assert yl + ys + nl + ns == size
    assert Fraction(yl + ys, size) == rates[label]["yellow"]
    assert Fraction(yl + nl, size) == rates[label]["long"]
both = sum(counts[0] for counts in joint.values())
assert both == 41
assert Fraction(38, both) == Fraction(38, 41) and readout(Fraction(38, 41)) == "0.927"
assert Fraction(3, both) == Fraction(3, 41)
# Linked overall, nearly independent within bananas.
assert sum(yl + ys for yl, ys, _, _ in joint.values()) == 71 and sum(yl + nl for yl, _, nl, _ in joint.values()) == 48
assert Fraction(both, 100) == Fraction(41, 100) and Fraction(71, 100) * Fraction(48, 100) == Fraction(3408, 10000)
assert Fraction(38, 50) == Fraction(76, 100) and Fraction(8, 10) * Fraction(9, 10) == Fraction(72, 100)

# Smoothed fruit rates: alpha = 1, two values per clue.
smoothed_rates = {
    label: {clue: (rate * priors[label] * 100 + 1) / (priors[label] * 100 + 2) for clue, rate in clue_rates.items()}
    for label, clue_rates in rates.items()
}
smoothed_fruit = normalize(nb_scores(priors, smoothed_rates, ["yellow", "long"]))
for label, expected in (("banana", 0.909), ("lemon", 0.086), ("apple", 0.005)):
    assert abs(float(smoothed_fruit[label]) - expected) < 0.001
assert smoothed_rates["apple"]["long"] == Fraction(1, 22) and smoothed_rates["banana"]["yellow"] == Fraction(41, 52)

# Copying feature 1: exact accuracy, confidence, log loss, Brier score and AUC.
def duplicate_experiment(copies, yes_plus=Fraction(7, 10), yes_minus=Fraction(3, 10)):
    rows = []
    for pattern in product((1, 0), repeat=4):
        odds = Fraction(1)
        for index, value in enumerate(pattern):
            ratio = yes_plus / yes_minus if value else (1 - yes_plus) / (1 - yes_minus)
            odds *= ratio ** (copies if index == 0 else 1)
        naive = odds / (1 + odds)
        for label in (1, 0):
            weight = Fraction(1, 2)
            for value in pattern:
                rate = yes_plus if label == 1 else yes_minus
                weight *= rate if value else 1 - rate
            rows.append((naive, label, weight))
    accuracy = confidence = brier = Fraction(0)
    log_loss = 0.0
    for naive, label, weight in rows:
        on_truth = naive if label == 1 else 1 - naive
        accuracy += weight * (Fraction(1, 2) if naive == Fraction(1, 2) else (1 if on_truth > Fraction(1, 2) else 0))
        confidence += weight * max(naive, 1 - naive)
        brier += weight * (1 - on_truth) ** 2
        log_loss -= float(weight) * math.log(on_truth)
    auc = Fraction(0)
    for plus_score, plus_label, plus_weight in rows:
        for minus_score, minus_label, minus_weight in rows:
            if plus_label == 1 and minus_label == 0:
                win = 1 if plus_score > minus_score else (Fraction(1, 2) if plus_score == minus_score else 0)
                auc += plus_weight * minus_weight * win
    return accuracy, confidence, log_loss, brier, auc * 4

import math
import torch

# Four yes-or-no features, each "yes" 7 times in 10 in class + and 3 in 10 in class -.
theta, phi, prior_plus = 0.7, 0.3, 0.5
w = math.log(theta * (1 - phi) / (phi * (1 - theta)))
b = math.log(prior_plus / (1 - prior_plus)) + 4 * math.log((1 - theta) / (1 - phi))

layer = torch.nn.Linear(4, 1)
with torch.no_grad():
    layer.weight.fill_(w)
    layer.bias.fill_(b)

x = torch.tensor([[1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]])
print(torch.sigmoid(layer(x)).item())  # P(+ | x): the 9/58 the exact code checks

## Research frontier

<ResearchFrontier reviewed="2026-11-24">

**Classifying well is not estimating well.** In Domingos and Pazzani's §4, the classes are equally likely, a third attribute C is independent of A given the class, and attribute B is a copy of A. The optimal choice is + when $P(+ \mid C) > 1 - P(+ \mid A)$; naive Bayes, counting A twice, picks + when $P(+ \mid C) > \frac{(1 - P(+ \mid A))^2}{P(+ \mid A)^2 + (1 - P(+ \mid A))^2}$. They disagree only between the curves of their Figure 1 <Cite id="domingos1997" />, as at $P(+ \mid A) = 0.7$, $P(+ \mid C) = 0.2$, where the naive threshold is $9/58 \approx 0.155$ instead of $0.3$. Their Theorem 1 gives the exact condition for naive Bayes to be optimal on one example under 0-1 loss; by Corollary 1 it holds in half the space of possible probability values, while under squared-error loss it is optimal only where independence holds (§5) <Cite id="domingos1997" />. It beat the C4.5 decision tree on 19 of 28 UCI datasets (79.1% against 77.2% on average), and measured dependence barely predicted the gap, $R^2 = 0.04$ (§3) <Cite id="domingos1997" />.

**Why dependence often leaves the decision alone.** Zhang argues this does not explain why strong dependencies fail to flip the decision (Related Work) <Cite id="zhang2004" />. He writes the true classifier as naive Bayes times a product of local dependence ratios (Theorem 1) and states exactly when the two agree (Theorem 2): dependencies spread evenly across classes, or canceling, leave the decision alone. Theorem 3 gives a correlated Gaussian case where naive Bayes stays optimal; his abstract still calls the true reason open <Cite id="zhang2004" />.

**What stays unsettled.** These are characterizations, not tests: checking the global condition on a test sample is in general not possible without the true class probabilities, and for a whole domain the work grows exponentially with the number of attributes (§6) <Cite id="domingos1997" />. Nor do good decisions bring good probabilities: calibration cannot fully correct naive Bayes (§4) <Cite id="niculescu2005" />.

</ResearchFrontier>

(b). The assumption is about dependence within a class. Correlation across the bowl is expected: the class links the features.

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that seeing a yes-or-no feature c times multiplies its terms in the log-odds by c, and say when that changes the predicted class.">
Each copy repeats feature 1's terms: the log-odds becomes $b' + c\,w_1 x_1 + \sum_{k \ge 2} w_k x_k$, with feature 1's share of $b$ counted $c$ times in $b'$. The class changes when the copies flip the sign of the log-odds. For (yes, no, no, no) the log-odds reaches 0 at $c = 3$ (posterior $1/2$) and turns positive at $c = 4$ ($7/10$); at $c = 5$ it is $49/58$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Naive Bayes assumes the features are independent.">
    Independent given the class. Overall they can be linked: 0.41 against 0.3408 in the bowl.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Its accuracy is good, so its probabilities are good.">
    With a clue seen 5 times, confidence is 0.970 and accuracy 0.700. Calibrate first.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A word never seen in spam proves an email is not spam.">
    That is a count of 0 vetoing a class. Smoothed, "win money now" gets $3/4$, not 1, and "win prize meeting" gets $4/7$.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Multiply the probabilities.">
    Add logs: $0.001^{1000}$ rounds to 0.0.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="More features always help naive Bayes.">
    Redundant features are counted again: four extra copies of one take accuracy from 0.784 to 0.700.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
