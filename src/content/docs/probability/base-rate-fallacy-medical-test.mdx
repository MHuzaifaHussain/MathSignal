This page builds on [Bayes' theorem](/probability/bayes-theorem/#test-positive-for-a-rare-condition) and practices its 1,000-people count, which finds what a positive means, until it is automatic.

## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Real tests also miss some true cases, and real groups don't have exactly 2 in 50 with lice. The count gives a share among children like these, not a certainty about the child in front of you.">

Fifty children line up for a head-lice check. Two of them really have lice. The nurse's comb finds both, but it also wrongly finds lice on 6 of the other 48 children, 1 in 8. So 8 children get sent home, and only 2 of those 8 have lice. The comb never misses, yet most children it flags are fine, because there were far more children without lice for it to get wrong.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
A classifier that flags rare events, such as fraud, a rare disease on a scan or a defect on a production line, runs on the same arithmetic. Its precision, the share of flagged cases that are real, depends on how rare the positive class is where the model is used, not only on the model.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Find what a positive means on the board

A screening test catches 95 in 100 people who have a condition and correctly clears 90 in 100 who don't. 2 in 100 people screened have it. You test positive. Write down your estimate of the chance that you have it, to the nearest 10%.

A second, optional guess: how common would the condition have to be for a positive to be an even chance?

The note under the buttons describes the preset, not your settings. Your goal: read P(A | B), then check your second guess: raise P(A) one step at a time until it passes 0.500.

About 16%, which rounds to 20%: far below the 95% the test's accuracy suggests. The readout says "P(A | B) = 0.162   P(B) = 0.117"; P(B) is the share who test positive, 117 in 1,000. Raising P(A), the readout reaches 0.484 at 0.09 and 0.514 at 0.10, so the even-chance point, your second guess, lies between them: exactly 2/21 ≈ 0.095, as [how common the condition has to be](#how-common-does-the-condition-have-to-be) shows. Now set P(A) to 0.30, as in a clinic for people with symptoms: "P(A | B) = 0.803   P(B) = 0.355". The test never changed; only who was tested did.

## Count four groups, then read one column

To find what a positive means, split a round number of people by the condition, then by the test, and read only the positives. The prevalence, or base rate, is how common the condition is. The sensitivity is how often the test is positive for people who have it. The specificity is how often the test is negative for people who don't. Below, D is the event "has the condition", + and − are the two test results, sens and spec are short for sensitivity and specificity, and P(D) is the prevalence.

1. Pick a population that makes every count whole: 1,000 people.
2. Split by the prevalence: 2% of 1,000 is 20 people who have it, and 980 who don't.
3. Split each group by the test. The sensitivity is 95%, so 19 of the 20 test positive (true positives) and 1 is missed (a false negative). The specificity is 90%, so the test correctly clears 90% of the 980; the other 10%, the false positive rate (1 − specificity), test positive. That is 98 false positives and 882 true negatives.
4. Read the positive column: 19 + 98 = 117 positives, and 19 of them have the condition. So a positive means 19/117 ≈ 0.162.

|  | Tests positive | Tests negative | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Has it** | 19 | 1 | 20 |
| **Doesn't have it** | 98 | 882 | 980 |
| **Total** | 117 | 883 | 1,000 |

The negative column reads the same way: of the 883 negatives only 1 has the condition, so a negative means no condition with probability 882/883 ≈ 0.9989. These two shares are the positive and negative predictive values, PPV and NPV.

Now the clinic, where 30 in 100 patients have the condition. Fill in steps 2 to 4 for 1,000 patients with the same test: how many have it, how many true positives, how many false positives?

<Simpler label="the clinic's four counts">

300 have it and 700 don't. Of the 300, 95% test positive: 285 true positives and 15 false negatives. Of the 700, 10% test positive: 70 false positives and 630 true negatives. That makes 285 + 70 = 355 positives, so the PPV is 285/355 = 57/71 ≈ 0.803, and the NPV is 630/645 = 42/43 ≈ 0.977.

</Simpler>

### Accuracy is not what a positive means

A test's accuracy is the share of everyone it gets right: 19 + 882 = 901 of 1,000 for the screening test, an accuracy of 0.901, yet a positive means 0.162. The 882 true negatives dominate the accuracy and hide the 98 false positives. Reading 0.95 or 0.901 as the answer while ignoring that only 2 in 100 people have the condition is the base rate fallacy. The general reason is in [the base rate decides a lot](/probability/bayes-theorem/#the-base-rate-decides-a-lot) on the Bayes page.

### How common does the condition have to be?

A positive is an even chance when the true positives equal the false positives: sens × P(D) = (1 − spec) × (1 − P(D)). Multiply out the right side and move every P(D) term to the left: P(D) × (sens + 1 − spec) = 1 − spec. So P(D) = (1 − spec) / (sens + 1 − spec). For the screening test that is 0.10 / (0.95 + 0.10) = 0.10 / 1.05 = 2/21 ≈ 0.095. Count 2,100 people to check: 200 have it, and 190 of them test positive; 1,900 don't, and 190 of them test positive too.

<GoDeeper label="the same answer with odds" id="odds-shortcut">

The [odds form of Bayes' theorem](/probability/bayes-theorem/#odds-form) says posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. For a positive, the likelihood ratio is sens / (1 − spec) = 0.95 / 0.10 = 9.5. In screening the prior odds are 2:98, and 2:98 × 9.5 = 19:98, a probability of 19/117.

</GoDeeper>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["sample-space", "event-collection", "event", "complement", "probability", "conditional", "sensitivity", "specificity", "ppv", "npv"]} />

In machine learning, sensitivity is called recall and the PPV is called precision.

<PreciseStatement>

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space, $D \in \mathcal{F}$ the event "has the condition" with $0 < P(D) < 1$, and $+ \in \mathcal{F}$ the event "tests positive", with complement $-$, "tests negative". Define $\text{sens} = P(+ \mid D)$ and $\text{spec} = P(- \mid D^\complement)$. If $P(+) > 0$,

$$
\text{PPV} = P(D \mid +) = \frac{\text{sens}\,P(D)}{\text{sens}\,P(D) + (1-\text{spec})\,(1-P(D))}.
$$

If $P(-) > 0$,

$$
\text{NPV} = P(D^\complement \mid -) = \frac{\text{spec}\,(1-P(D))}{\text{spec}\,(1-P(D)) + (1-\text{sens})\,P(D)}.
$$

Boundary cases. If $\text{spec} = 1$ and $\text{sens} > 0$, there are no false positives and $\text{PPV} = 1$. If $\text{sens} + \text{spec} = 1$ with $\text{sens} > 0$, the test carries no information and $\text{PPV} = P(D)$, as in [the Bayes page's boundary cases](/probability/bayes-theorem/#precise-statement). If $\text{sens} = 0$ and $\text{spec} = 1$, then $P(+) = 0$ and there is no PPV. Throughout, sens and spec are assumed not to depend on $P(D)$; the ML section below names this assumption.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="PPV and NPV from sensitivity, specificity and prevalence" rigor="full">

1. By the law of total probability ([add up every way the evidence can happen](/probability/bayes-theorem/#add-up-every-way-the-evidence-can-happen)), $P(+) = P(+ \mid D)\,P(D) + P(+ \mid D^\complement)\,P(D^\complement)$.
2. The complement rule for $P(\cdot \mid D^\complement)$ gives $P(+ \mid D^\complement) = 1 - P(- \mid D^\complement) = 1 - \text{spec}$. So $P(+) = \text{sens}\,P(D) + (1 - \text{spec})(1 - P(D))$.
3. If $P(+) > 0$, Bayes' theorem gives $P(D \mid +) = \text{sens}\,P(D) / P(+)$, which is the PPV formula.
4. The same steps with $-$ in place of $+$ and $D^\complement$ in place of $D$, using $P(- \mid D) = 1 - \text{sens}$, give $P(-) = \text{spec}\,(1 - P(D)) + (1 - \text{sens})\,P(D)$ and, if $P(-) > 0$, the NPV formula.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="The even-chance prevalence, and why PPV rises with prevalence" rigor="full">

1. Assume $P(+) > 0$. Then $\text{PPV} > 1/2$ exactly when the top of the PPV fraction is more than half the bottom: $\text{sens}\,P(D) > (1 - \text{spec})(1 - P(D))$.
2. Collect the $P(D)$ terms: $P(D)\,(\text{sens} + 1 - \text{spec}) > 1 - \text{spec}$.
3. $\text{sens} + 1 - \text{spec} > 0$ unless $\text{sens} = 0$ and $\text{spec} = 1$, the case with no positives. Dividing by it: $\text{PPV} > 1/2$ exactly when $P(D) > (1 - \text{spec}) / (\text{sens} + 1 - \text{spec})$, with equality at the threshold.
4. PPV. Assume $\text{sens} > 0$ and $\text{spec} < 1$. Dividing top and bottom by $\text{sens}\,P(D)$ gives $\text{PPV} = 1 / \big(1 + \tfrac{1 - \text{spec}}{\text{sens}} \cdot \tfrac{1 - P(D)}{P(D)}\big)$. The first fraction is a positive constant and $(1 - P(D))/P(D)$ strictly decreases as $P(D)$ grows, so the PPV strictly increases.
5. NPV. Assume $\text{spec} > 0$ and $\text{sens} < 1$. Dividing top and bottom by $\text{spec}\,(1 - P(D))$ gives $\text{NPV} = 1 / \big(1 + \tfrac{1 - \text{sens}}{\text{spec}} \cdot \tfrac{P(D)}{1 - P(D)}\big)$. Now $P(D)/(1 - P(D))$ strictly increases with $P(D)$, so the NPV strictly decreases.

</StepThrough>

| Rule | Statement | Screening test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total positives | $P(+) = \text{sens}\,P(D) + (1 - \text{spec})(1 - P(D))$ | $0.019 + 0.098 = 0.117$ |
| PPV | $\text{sens}\,P(D) / P(+)$ | $19/117 \approx 0.162$ |
| NPV | $\text{spec}\,(1 - P(D)) / P(-)$ | $882/883 \approx 0.9989$ |
| Even-chance prevalence | $(1 - \text{spec}) / (\text{sens} + 1 - \text{spec})$ | $2/21 \approx 0.095$ |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="A symptom counted by hand">

Gigerenzer and Hoffrage describe a physician who has seen 1,000 people: 10 had a disease, 8 of those showed a symptom, and 95 of the 990 without the disease showed it too. A new patient has the symptom. Then $P(\text{disease} \mid \text{symptom}) = 8/(8 + 95) = 8/103 \approx 0.078$ <Cite id="gigerenzer1995" />. Their mammography problem states the same numbers as percentages (1% have the disease, 80% of those test positive, and 9.6% of the healthy test positive too) and gives the same 0.078 <Cite id="gigerenzer1995" />.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="A rare cancer and a better test">

In Grinstead and Snell's Example 4.17, 1 woman in 1,000 has a cancer, and the test has sensitivity 99% and specificity 95% <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. Count 100,000 women: 100 have it and 99 test positive; 99,900 don't, and 5% of them, 4,995, test positive. The PPV is $99/5094 = 11/566 \approx 0.019$, so 98.1% of positives are false. The probability rose nearly twentyfold, from 0.001, yet stays small.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Find the wrong step: dividing by the wrong group">

Prevalence 5%, sensitivity 80%, specificity 95%, 2,000 people. A student writes: "100 have it, and 80 of them test positive. So a positive means 80/100 = 0.80." Which step is wrong? The last one. 80/100 is the sensitivity, $P(+ \mid D)$; the question asks for $P(D \mid +)$, whose denominator is every positive. The 1,900 who don't have it give 5% false positives, 95 people, so there are 175 positives and the PPV is $80/175 = 16/35 \approx 0.457$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={4} title="Find the wrong step: specificity used as the false positive rate">

Prevalence 1 in 50, sensitivity 90%, specificity 97%, 5,000 people. A student writes: "100 have it, so 90 true positives. 97% of the 4,900 healthy people test positive, so 4,753 false positives. PPV = 90/4,843 ≈ 0.019." The wrong step is the second: specificity is the share of healthy people who test negative. False positives are 3% of 4,900 = 147, and the PPV is $90/237 = 30/79 \approx 0.380$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={5} title="A fraud detector on a balanced test set and in use">

A fraud model has recall (sensitivity) 0.90 and a false positive rate of 1% (specificity 0.99). On a validation set of 200 transactions, 100 of them fraud, it catches 90 and wrongly flags 1 of the 100 legitimate ones, so its precision is $90/91 \approx 0.989$. In use, 1 transaction in 500 is fraud. Of 1,000,000 transactions, 2,000 are fraud and 1,800 of them are caught; 998,000 are legitimate and 9,980 of them are flagged. That is 11,780 alerts, and the precision is $1800/11780 = 90/589 \approx 0.153$. Same model, yet about 85% of alerts, 9,980 of 11,780, are now false.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={6} title="When is a positive a coin flip?" faded>

A test has sensitivity 80% and specificity 90%. At what prevalence is a positive an even chance, and what is the NPV at that prevalence? Then check the crossing on the board with Medical test and P(B | A) set to 0.80.

<div slot="solution">

The even-chance prevalence is $(1 - 0.9) / (0.8 + 0.1) = 0.1/0.9 = 1/9 \approx 0.111$. Check with 900 people: 100 have it and 80 test positive; 800 don't, and 80 of them test positive. The negatives are 20 people with the condition and 720 without, so the NPV is $720/740 = 36/37 \approx 0.973$. On the board, P(A) = 0.11 reads 0.497 and 0.12 reads 0.522: the crossing lies between them, because $1/9$ is just above 0.11.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## The same arithmetic in machine learning

A classifier's precision is the PPV and its recall is the sensitivity, so everything above carries over.

### Precision falls when positives are rare

Precision moves with the ratio of positives to negatives, while recall and specificity are each computed within one class and do not. Saito and Rehmsmeier give a small example in which a balanced and an imbalanced data set both have accuracy 0.6, but precision is 0.6 on the balanced one and 0.33 on the imbalanced one <Cite id="saito2015" />. Of the four evaluation plots they compare, only the precision-recall plot, which draws precision against recall, changes with the class ratio <Cite id="saito2015" />.

### When the base rate moves after deployment

Lipton, Wang and Smola open with a pneumonia predictor, trained when 0.1% of patients had pneumonia, that months later predicts 5% <Cite id="lipton2018" />. They call the case where the share of each label changes, while how the inputs look within each label does not, label shift, and call that assumption strong but reasonable in medical diagnosis, where diseases cause symptoms (Section 3) <Cite id="lipton2018" />. Under label shift, a fixed predictor's sensitivity and specificity stay the same (their Lemma 1, Section 4) <Cite id="lipton2018" />. Only the predictive values move. For example, with sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.99 the PPV is $10/121 \approx 0.083$ at prevalence 0.001 and $90/109 \approx 0.826$ at prevalence 0.05. To correct a model's scores for a new prevalence, see [when the base rate shifts](/probability/bayes-theorem/#when-the-base-rate-shifts).

## Check every answer in code

This runs in your browser with exact fractions and stops with an error if a result is off. It recounts every population on this page, checks each board readout with the board's own floating-point arithmetic, and screens 100,000 simulated people.

<PyRunner
  title="base_rate.py"
  code={`
import random
from fractions import Fraction


def positives(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity):
    """P(+) by the law of total probability: sens P(D) + (1 - spec)(1 - P(D))."""
    return sensitivity * prevalence + (1 - specificity) * (1 - prevalence)


def ppv(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity):
    """P(D | +); an error when no one tests positive."""
    total = positives(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity)
    if total == 0:
        raise ValueError("PPV is undefined: no one tests positive (P(+) = 0)")
    return sensitivity * prevalence / total


def npv(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity):
    """P(not D | -); an error when no one tests negative."""
    total = specificity * (1 - prevalence) + (1 - sensitivity) * prevalence
    if total == 0:
        raise ValueError("NPV is undefined: no one tests negative (P(-) = 0)")
    return specificity * (1 - prevalence) / total


def even_chance(sensitivity, specificity):
    """The prevalence at which a positive is an even chance: (1 - spec) / (sens + 1 - spec)."""
    return (1 - specificity) / (sensitivity + 1 - specificity)


def counts(people, prevalence, sensitivity, specificity):
    """Whole numbers of people: (has it, true pos, false neg, doesn't, false pos, true neg)."""
    has = people * prevalence
    true_positive = has * sensitivity
    healthy = people - has
    false_positive = healthy * (1 - specificity)
    numbers = (has, true_positive, has - true_positive, healthy, false_positive, healthy - false_positive)
    assert all(number.denominator == 1 for number in numbers), "pick a population with whole counts"
    return tuple(int(number) for number in numbers)


def three(value):
    """Three decimals, as the board shows them."""
    return f"{float(value):.3f}"


def board_readout(prior, likelihood, false_positive):
    """The board's own float arithmetic: (P(A | B), P(B)) to three decimals."""
    evidence = likelihood * prior + false_positive * (1 - prior)
    return f"{likelihood * prior / evidence:.3f}", f"{evidence:.3f}"

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="The same test is used in a screening program and in a clinic for people with symptoms. Where does a positive mean more, and why?">
In the clinic. Sensitivity and specificity are the same, but far more of the clinic's patients have the condition, so true positives make up more of the positives: 0.803 against 0.162.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific. 1 person in 10,000 has the condition. Out of 1,000,000 people, how many test positive, and what does a positive mean?">
10,098 positives, and a positive means about 1%. 100 people have the condition and 99 of them test positive; 999,900 don't, and 1% of them, 9,999, test positive. So the PPV is $99/10098 = 1/102 \approx 0.010$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that if sens > 0 and spec < 1, the PPV strictly increases with the prevalence.">
Use odds. Bayes' theorem in odds form gives posterior odds $= \frac{\text{sens}}{1 - \text{spec}} \cdot \frac{P(D)}{1 - P(D)}$, and the first factor is a positive constant. The prior odds $P(D)/(1 - P(D))$ strictly increase as $P(D)$ grows, so the posterior odds do too. A probability is odds $/ (1 + \text{odds})$, which strictly increases with the odds, so the PPV strictly increases.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="To make positives trustworthy, improve the sensitivity.">
    At 2 in 100, raising the screening test's sensitivity from 95% to 100% only moves the PPV from $19/117 \approx 0.162$ to $10/59 \approx 0.169$. The false positives come from the healthy majority, so the specificity matters more: at 95% specificity the PPV is $19/68 \approx 0.279$.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Specificity 97% means 97% of healthy people test positive.">
    Specificity is the share of healthy people who test negative. The false positive rate is $1 - \text{spec} = 3\%$, as worked example 4 shows: 147 false positives, not 4,753.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A positive predictive value of 16% means the test is useless.">
    A positive raised the chance about eightfold, from 0.02 to 0.162, and a negative leaves only 1 in 883 with the condition. A second, independent test can follow, as in [a second test: the posterior becomes the prior](/probability/bayes-theorem/#a-second-test-the-posterior-becomes-the-prior).
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="A model's precision on a balanced test set is the precision it will have in use.">
    Precision depends on how common positives are where the model runs. The fraud model in worked example 5 has precision $0.989$ on a balanced set and $0.153$ where 1 transaction in 500 is fraud.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
