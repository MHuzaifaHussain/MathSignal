## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Knowing the weather removes the link only if the weather is the whole story. If some hot days are also beach holidays, and holidays both sell more ice cream and burn more people, the two stay linked even among hot days. And 'given the weather' means checking hot days and cool days separately, not only one of them.">

Does a day with high ice-cream sales tell you anything about sunburn at the clinic? Ice cream burns nobody, but both might follow the weather. Ask a narrower question: on hot days only, does knowing about ice cream tell you anything extra about sunburn? Repeat for cool days. If the answer is no both times, the two events are independent given the weather. That is conditional independence.

Learning something can also create a link. Two coin tosses are unrelated. Wire a light that turns on when either lands heads, see it lit, and the coins become linked (see [how a common effect creates a link](#learning-about-a-common-effect-can-create-a-link)).

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Naive Bayes, Bayesian networks and repeated diagnostic tests all multiply probabilities that are assumed independent given something, such as the class or the true condition. Whether that assumption holds decides whether a piece of evidence is counted once or twice, and so how confident the final answer is.
</WhyThisMatters>

## A summer of 100 days

Before you read the tables, guess. On days with high ice-cream sales, is sunburn more likely, less likely or the same as on an average day? A second guess: if ice cream and sunburn are independent on hot days and also on cool days, are they independent over the whole summer?

Here is the summer: 100 days, 40 hot and 60 cool. On each day we note whether ice-cream sales were high (call it I) and whether the local clinic saw sunburn (call it S). A hot day is H, so a cool day is "not H". The counts are made up for teaching.

**Hot days (40)**

| Days | Sunburn | No sunburn | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **High ice cream** | 15 | 15 | 30 |
| **Low ice cream** | 5 | 5 | 10 |
| **Total** | 20 | 20 | 40 |

**Cool days (60)**

| Days | Sunburn | No sunburn | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **High ice cream** | 2 | 18 | 20 |
| **Low ice cream** | 4 | 36 | 40 |
| **Total** | 6 | 54 | 60 |

**The whole summer (100)**

| Days | Sunburn | No sunburn | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **High ice cream** | 17 | 33 | 50 |
| **Low ice cream** | 9 | 41 | 50 |
| **Total** | 26 | 74 | 100 |

More likely. Over the summer, sunburn happens on 26 of 100 days, 0.26. On high-ice-cream days it happens on 17 of 50, 0.34, and on the other days on 9 of 50, 0.18. So ice cream and sunburn are not independent overall.

Inside each kind of day the link is gone. On hot days sunburn happens on 20 of 40 days and on 15 of the 30 high-ice-cream days: 1/2 both ways. The cool days pass too, as you will check below, so the answer to the second question is no: independent within every group, dependent overall.

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["sample-space", "event-collection", "event", "complement", "intersection", "probability", "conditional", "independent", "conditionally-independent", "conditional-distribution"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space and $A, B, C \in \mathcal{F}$ with $P(C) > 0$. Then $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$, written $A \indep B \mid C$, if

$$
P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\,P(B \mid C).
$$

If also $P(B \cap C) > 0$, this is equivalent to $P(A \mid B \cap C) = P(A \mid C)$. For discrete random variables $X, Y, Z$, the variables $X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $Z$ if $p(x, y \mid z) = p(x \mid z)\,p(y \mid z)$ for all $x, y$ and every $z$ with $P(Z = z) > 0$. Zhang et al., §2.6.4, give this product rule for random variables <Cite id="zhang2023" />. The clause about every value of $z$ with positive probability spells out what "given $Z$" means.

Boundary cases. If $P(C) = 0$ the definition doesn't apply. "Given a variable" means given every value of it, so for the weather it means given $H$ and given $H^\complement$. Neither independence nor conditional independence implies the other: the summer tables show conditional independence without independence, and the coins in [learning about a common effect](#learning-about-a-common-effect-can-create-a-link) show independence without conditional independence.

</PreciseStatement>

## Check it inside each group

To check whether A and B are independent given C, fix one value of C, compute P(A | C), P(B | C) and P(A ∩ B | C) from that group's counts alone, and compare the product with the joint. Then repeat for every other value of C. Here are the three steps on the hot days:

<StepThrough title="Hot days: are ice cream and sunburn independent given H?" rigor="full">

1. Keep only the 40 hot days. Every probability below divides by 40, as in [conditional probability](/probability/conditional-probability/#conditioning-is-a-probability).
2. Read the three shares: $P(I \mid H) = 30/40 = 3/4$, $P(S \mid H) = 20/40 = 1/2$ and $P(I \cap S \mid H) = 15/40 = 3/8$.
3. Compare: $3/4 \times 1/2 = 3/8$, equal to the joint. On hot days the two events are independent.

</StepThrough>

The weather has a second value, so the check isn't finished. Take the same three steps on the cool days before you open the answer.

<StepThrough title="Cool days: your turn" rigor="full">

1. Keep only the 60 cool days.
2. Read $P(I \mid H^\complement)$, $P(S \mid H^\complement)$ and $P(I \cap S \mid H^\complement)$ from the cool-day table.
3. Compare the product with the joint.

</StepThrough>

<Fold kind="answer" label="the cool-day check">

$P(I \mid H^\complement) = 20/60 = 1/3$, $P(S \mid H^\complement) = 6/60 = 1/10$ and $P(I \cap S \mid H^\complement) = 2/60 = 1/30$. The product is $1/3 \times 1/10 = 1/30$, equal to the joint. Both values of the weather pass, so ice cream and sunburn are independent given the weather.

</Fold>

## Why a common cause links them overall

A common cause links two events overall because pooling mixes groups where both rates are high with groups where both are low, so the pooled numbers move together. A high-ice-cream day is more likely to be a hot day, which makes sunburn more likely.

Over the summer, $P(I \cap S) = 17/100 = 0.17$, while $P(I) = 50/100 = 0.5$ and $P(S) = 0.26$, so $P(I)\,P(S) = 0.13$. On cool days, $P(I \mid H^\complement) = 20/60 = 1/3$ and $P(S \mid H^\complement) = 6/60 = 1/10$. The weather moves ice cream from $1/3$ to $3/4$ and sunburn from $1/10$ to $1/2$.

With two groups, the gap between the joint and the product is the share of hot days times the share of cool days, times those two moves (derived in [the formal definition](#formal-definition)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(I \cap S) - P(I)\,P(S) &= 0.17 - 0.13 = 0.04 \\
&= 0.4 \times 0.6 \times \left(\tfrac{3}{4} - \tfrac{1}{3}\right)\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{10}\right) \\
&= 0.24 \times \tfrac{5}{12} \times \tfrac{2}{5}.
\end{aligned}
$$
<Simpler label="the same 100 days as a strip" id="same-days-two-ways">

Line up the 100 days as a strip and sort them by weather: 40 hot days on the left, 60 cool on the right. Mark the high-ice-cream days and the sunburn days. Both kinds of mark bunch in the hot block. Now ask what each mark tells you about the weather. Of the 50 high-ice-cream days, 30 are hot, so $P(H \mid I) = 3/5$, up from $0.4$. Of the 26 sunburn days, 20 are hot, so $P(H \mid S) = 10/13$. Of the 17 days with both, 15 are hot, so $P(H \mid I \cap S) = 15/17$. Each clue raises the chance that it was hot, and that shared rise is what links the two.

</Simpler>

## Learning about a common effect can create a link

Learning about a common effect can make two independent events dependent. Toss two fair coins, and wire a light that turns on when at least one lands heads. Without the light, the coins are [independent](/probability/independence/): $P(\text{first heads} \mid \text{second tails}) = P(\text{first heads}) = 1/2$.

Now you see the light is on. Three of the four outcomes, HH, HT and TH, turn it on, so $P(\text{first heads} \mid \text{light}) = 2/3$. If you then learn the second coin is heads, the light is already explained, and $P(\text{first heads} \mid \text{light} \cap \text{second heads}) = 1/2$. If you learn the second is tails, only HT is left, and the first is certainly heads: probability 1. Given the light, the second coin tells you about the first. This is called explaining away. Kuleshov and Ermon use rain, a sprinkler and a wet lawn: given a wet lawn, learning the sprinkler was off makes rain the explanation <Cite id="kuleshov-ermon-cs228" />.

<GoDeeper label="forks, chains and colliders in a graph" id="forks-chains-colliders">

A Bayesian network draws each variable as a node and arrows from causes to effects. With three nodes there are three shapes <Cite id="kuleshov-ermon-cs228" />:

- **Common parent** $X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y$ (the weather and its two effects): $X$ and $Y$ can be dependent, and are independent given $Z$.
- **Cascade** $X \rightarrow Z \rightarrow Y$: the same pattern, because $Z$ carries everything $X$ passes to $Y$.
- **V-structure** $X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow Y$ (the coins and the light): $X$ and $Y$ are independent, and can become dependent given $Z$, or given any descendant of $Z$.

A path is active when every three-node stretch along it lets dependence through: a common parent or cascade does when its middle node is unobserved, and a v-structure does when its middle node, or one of its descendants, is observed. Two sets of nodes are d-separated when no path between them is active. Graphs with the same skeleton (links, ignoring direction) and the same v-structures encode the same independencies <Cite id="kuleshov-ermon-cs228" />. So conditional independencies alone can't tell a chain from a fork over the same three nodes, but can pick out a collider.

</GoDeeper>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="The two forms of the definition agree" rigor="full">

1. Assume $P(C) > 0$ and $P(B \cap C) > 0$, so every conditional probability below is defined.
2. By the definition of conditional probability, $P(A \mid B \cap C) = P(A \cap B \cap C) / P(B \cap C)$.
3. Divide the top and the bottom by $P(C) > 0$: $P(A \mid B \cap C) = P(A \cap B \mid C) / P(B \mid C)$, where $P(B \mid C) > 0$ because $P(B \cap C) > 0$.
4. So $P(A \mid B \cap C) = P(A \mid C)$ holds exactly when $P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\,P(B \mid C)$: multiply or divide both sides by $P(B \mid C)$.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="The two-group identity" rigor="full">

1. Write $p = P(C)$ with $0 < p < 1$, $a_1 = P(A \mid C)$, $a_0 = P(A \mid C^\complement)$, $b_1 = P(B \mid C)$ and $b_0 = P(B \mid C^\complement)$. Assume $A$ and $B$ are independent given $C$ and given $C^\complement$.
2. By the [law of total probability](/probability/law-of-total-probability/) and the assumption, $P(A \cap B) = p\,a_1 b_1 + (1 - p)\,a_0 b_0$.
3. By total probability again, $P(A) = p\,a_1 + (1 - p)\,a_0$ and $P(B) = p\,b_1 + (1 - p)\,b_0$.
4. Expand $P(A)\,P(B)$ and subtract. The terms collect into $P(A \cap B) - P(A)\,P(B) = p(1 - p)(a_1 - a_0)(b_1 - b_0)$, because $p - p^2 = p(1-p)$ and $(1-p) - (1-p)^2 = p(1-p)$.
5. Consequence: since $p(1 - p) > 0$, $A$ and $B$ are independent overall exactly when $a_1 = a_0$ or $b_1 = b_0$, that is, when $C$ doesn't change the rate of one of them. If $C$ moves both in the same direction, the difference is positive and they happen together more often than independence predicts.

</StepThrough>

| Rule | Statement | Summer numbers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | $P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\,P(B \mid C)$ | Hot: $3/8 = 3/4 \times 1/2$ |
| Equivalent form | $P(A \mid B \cap C) = P(A \mid C)$, for $P(B \cap C) > 0$ | Hot: $P(S \mid I \cap H) = 15/30 = 1/2$ |
| Two-group identity | $P(A \cap B) - P(A)P(B) = p(1-p)(a_1 - a_0)(b_1 - b_0)$ | $0.17 - 0.13 = 0.04$ |
| Given a variable | Every value $z$ with $P(Z = z) > 0$ | Hot and cool both pass |
| Collider | Independent overall, dependent given the effect | Coins: $1/2$, then $2/3$, then $1$ |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="The hot-day check in full">

On the 40 hot days, $P(I \mid H) = 3/4$, $P(S \mid H) = 1/2$ and $P(I \cap S \mid H) = 15/40 = 3/8 = 3/4 \times 1/2$. In the second form, $P(S \mid I \cap H) = 15/30 = 1/2 = P(S \mid H)$: among hot days, high sales leave the sunburn rate where it was.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="Find the error: one hot-day check">

A student checks only the hot days, finds $15/40 = 3/4 \times 1/2$, and writes "ice cream and sunburn are independent given the weather, so they are independent." Find the two errors.

First, the claim is about the weather, a variable, and only one of its values was checked. The cool days happen to pass too ($1/30 = 1/3 \times 1/10$), but that had to be checked. Second, conditional independence doesn't carry over to the whole summer: $P(I \cap S) = 0.17$, while $P(I)\,P(S) = 0.5 \times 0.26 = 0.13$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Two HIV tests, independent given the condition">

Here $M$ means a person has the condition (Zhang et al. call it $H$), $D_1$ means test 1 is positive and $D_2$ means test 2 is positive. Zhang et al. (§2.6.5) take prevalence $P(M) = 0.0015$. Test 1 has $P(D_1 \mid M) = 1$ and $P(D_1 \mid M^\complement) = 0.01$; test 2 has $P(D_2 \mid M) = 0.98$ and $P(D_2 \mid M^\complement) = 0.03$. Assume the tests are independent given $M$ and given $M^\complement$ <Cite id="zhang2023" />.

Both positive: $P(D_1 \cap D_2 \mid M) = 1 \times 0.98 = 0.98$ and $P(D_1 \cap D_2 \mid M^\complement) = 0.01 \times 0.03 = 0.0003$. Total probability gives $P(D_1 \cap D_2) = 0.98 \times 0.0015 + 0.0003 \times 0.9985 = 0.00176955$, so

$$
P(M \mid D_1 \cap D_2) = \frac{0.00147}{0.00176955} \approx 0.8307.
$$

After the first test alone, $P(D_1) = 1 \times 0.0015 + 0.01 \times 0.9985 = 0.011485$, so $P(M \mid D_1) = 0.0015 / 0.011485 \approx 0.1306$. The book calls the assumption crucial to this answer <Cite id="zhang2023" />.

The tests are not independent overall. $P(D_2) = 0.031425$, but $P(D_2 \mid D_1) = 0.00176955 / 0.011485 \approx 0.1541$: a first positive makes a second about 4.9 times as likely, because it raises the chance of $M$. Equivalently, the joint is about 4.9 times the product $P(D_1)\,P(D_2) \approx 0.000361$, and the gap matches the identity: $0.0015 \times 0.9985 \times (1 - 0.01) \times (0.98 - 0.03) \approx 0.0014086$. The Bayes page does the same update with simpler numbers in [updating twice](/probability/bayes-theorem/#updating-twice).

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={4} title="The coins and the light">

The four outcomes HH, HT, TH and TT each have probability $1/4$. The light is on for HH, HT and TH, so $P(\text{light}) = 3/4$ and $P(\text{first heads} \mid \text{light}) = (2/4)/(3/4) = 2/3$. Add "the second is tails": only HT is left, so $P(\text{first heads} \mid \text{light} \cap \text{second tails}) = 1$. Without the light, "the second is tails" leaves the first at $1/2$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={5} title="Find the error: independent stays independent">

"Two fair coins are independent, so they stay independent given anything." Which step fails?

Independence is a statement about the whole sample space. Given the light, the sample space shrinks to HH, HT and TH, and inside it $P(\text{first heads} \cap \text{second heads} \mid \text{light}) = 1/3$, while $P(\text{first heads} \mid \text{light})\,P(\text{second heads} \mid \text{light}) = 2/3 \times 2/3 = 4/9$. The product rule fails, so the coins are dependent given the light.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={6} title="Two spam words, counted twice" faded>

Made-up counts for 300 emails: 100 spam and 200 not. In spam, "free" appears in 60, "winner" in 40 and both in 24. In the rest, "free" appears in 10, "winner" in 4 and both in 2, from a newsletter that uses the pair. Check whether the two words are independent given each class. Then compute P(spam | both words) from the counts, and again with the naive product $P(\text{free} \mid y)\,P(\text{winner} \mid y)$.

<div slot="solution">

Spam: $24/100 = 0.24 = 0.6 \times 0.4$, so the words are independent given spam. Not spam: $2/200 = 0.01$, but $0.05 \times 0.02 = 0.001$, so they are not independent given not spam; the pair shows up ten times as often as the product says.

From the counts, 24 spam and 2 other emails have both words, so $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{both}) = 24/26 = 12/13 \approx 0.923$. The naive product gives $\tfrac{1}{3} \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.08$ for spam and $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 0.05 \times 0.02 = 1/1500$ for the rest, so $0.08 / (0.08 + 1/1500) = 120/121 \approx 0.992$. The naive answer is too confident: it treats the newsletter's two words as two separate clues that the email is spam, when for the newsletter they are one clue. A [naive Bayes classifier](/probability/naive-bayes-classifier/) makes this assumption on purpose.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Where conditional independence shows up in ML

A naive Bayes classifier assumes the features are independent given the class, so that $P(x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid y) = \prod_k P(x_k \mid y)$. Each factor is a count, which makes it cheap to train; the [naive Bayes section of the Bayes page](/probability/bayes-theorem/#naive-bayes) sets out the rule, and the [naive Bayes classifier](/probability/naive-bayes-classifier/) page builds one from counts. Worked example 6 shows the price: correlated features are counted twice, and the probabilities drift toward 0 and 1.

A Bayesian network writes a joint distribution as a product of each variable given its parents. The missing arrows are conditional independencies, and a graph rule called d-separation (see [forks, chains and colliders](#forks-chains-colliders)) reads them off the graph: if the distribution factorizes over the graph, every independence the graph shows holds in the distribution <Cite id="kuleshov-ermon-cs228" />. That is what lets a model with many variables store only small local tables.

## Check every count in code

This runs in your browser with exact fractions and stops with an error if any number on this page is off. It checks the summer tables, the identity on a grid of values, the coins, the dice, the two tests and the spam counts, then simulates 100,000 summer days.

<PyRunner
  title="conditional_independence.py"
  code={`
import random
from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product

# The summer: (ice cream and sunburn, ice cream only, sunburn only, neither) per kind of day.
hot = (15, 15, 5, 5)
cool = (2, 18, 4, 36)
assert sum(hot) == 40 and sum(cool) == 60


def rates(counts):
    both, ice_only, sun_only, neither = counts
    days = sum(counts)
    return F(both + ice_only, days), F(both + sun_only, days), F(both, days)


ice_h, sun_h, both_h = rates(hot)
ice_c, sun_c, both_c = rates(cool)
assert (ice_h, sun_h, both_h) == (F(3, 4), F(1, 2), F(3, 8)) and ice_h * sun_h == both_h
assert (ice_c, sun_c, both_c) == (F(1, 3), F(1, 10), F(1, 30)) and ice_c * sun_c == both_c
assert F(15, 30) == sun_h and F(2, 20) == sun_c == F(1, 10)

summer = tuple(h + c for h, c in zip(hot, cool))
assert summer == (17, 33, 9, 41)
ice, sun, both = rates(summer)
assert (ice, sun, both) == (F(1, 2), F(13, 50), F(17, 100))
assert ice * sun == F(13, 100) and both - ice * sun == F(1, 25)
both_s, ice_only, sun_only, neither = summer
assert F(both_s, both_s + ice_only) == F(17, 50) == F(34, 100)
assert F(sun_only, sun_only + neither) == F(9, 50) == F(18, 100)
p = F(sum(hot), sum(summer))
assert p == F(2, 5)
assert p * (1 - p) * (ice_h - ice_c) * (sun_h - sun_c) == F(1, 25)
assert p * (1 - p) == F(24, 100) and ice_h - ice_c == F(5, 12) and sun_h - sun_c == F(2, 5)
# What each clue says about the weather: P(H | I), P(H | S) and P(H | I and S).
assert F(hot[0] + hot[1], both_s + ice_only) == F(3, 5)
assert F(hot[0] + hot[2], both_s + sun_only) == F(10, 13)
assert F(hot[0], both_s) == F(15, 17)
print("summer: P(I and S) =", both, " P(I) P(S) =", ice * sun)

# The two-group identity, on a grid of exact values.
grid = [F(0), F(1, 4), F(1, 3), F(1, 2), F(9, 10), F(1)]
for p, a1, a0, b1, b0 in product([F(1, 5), F(1, 2), F(2, 3)], grid, grid, grid, grid):
    joint = p * a1 * b1 + (1 - p) * a0 * b0
    product_ab = (p * a1 + (1 - p) * a0) * (p * b1 + (1 - p) * b0)
    assert joint - product_ab == p * (1 - p) * (a1 - a0) * (b1 - b0)
    if a1 == a0:
        assert joint == product_ab

# Two coins and a light that is on when at least one lands heads.
coins = list(product("HT", repeat=2))


def chance(event, given=lambda o: True):
    kept = [o for o in coins if given(o)]
    return F(sum(1 for o in kept if event(o)), len(kept))


first_h = lambda o: o[0] == "H"
second_h = lambda o: o[1] == "H"
light = lambda o: "H" in o
assert chance(first_h) == F(1, 2) and chance(light) == F(3, 4)
assert chance(first_h, lambda o: not second_h(o)) == F(1, 2)
assert chance(first_h, light) == F(2, 3)

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Two thermometers in the same room rise and fall together. Are their errors independent given the true temperature?">
Usually yes. Each thermometer's error comes from its own parts, so once you know the true temperature, one reading tells you nothing about the other's error. The readings themselves are dependent, because both follow the temperature: a common cause, as with the weather in the summer. The errors stop being independent if something besides the temperature affects both, such as a shared sunbeam: like the beach holidays in [the analogy](#in-everyday-terms), a second common cause brings the link back.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="From the summer tables, what is P(S | I, cool), and does it equal P(S | cool)?">
$2/20 = 1/10$, and $P(S \mid H^\complement) = 6/60 = 1/10$, so yes. On cool days, high sales leave the sunburn rate unchanged.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Roll two fair dice, and let C be 'the sum is 7'. Are 'the first die shows 1' and 'the second die shows 6' independent given C?">
No. Given C, the six outcomes are (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2) and (6,1). $P(\text{first is 1} \mid C) = 1/6$ and $P(\text{second is 6} \mid C) = 1/6$, so the product is $1/36$, but both happen only in (1,6), so the joint is $1/6$. Given the sum, the first die fixes the second.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Suppose A and B are independent given C and given not C, with 0 < P(C) < 1. Use the two-group identity to show that if P(A | C) = P(A | not C), then P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).">
In the identity, $a_1 = P(A \mid C)$, $a_0 = P(A \mid C^\complement)$, $b_1 = P(B \mid C)$, $b_0 = P(B \mid C^\complement)$ and $p = P(C)$ (see [the formal definition](#formal-definition)). If $a_1 = a_0$, then $a_1 - a_0 = 0$, so $P(A \cap B) - P(A)\,P(B) = p(1 - p) \times 0 \times (b_1 - b_0) = 0$. A cause that doesn't move one of the events can't link them, whatever it does to the other.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Independent given C means independent.">
    Over the summer $P(I \cap S) = 0.17$, while $P(I)\,P(S) = 0.13$.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Independent means independent given anything.">
    Given the light, learning that the second coin is tails makes the first certain to be heads.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Checking one value of C is enough.">
    Given a variable means given every value with positive probability, so the cool days need checking too.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="If two things move together, one causes the other.">
    Ice cream doesn't cause sunburn. Both follow the weather, and given the weather the link disappears.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Running the same test twice counts as two independent tests.">
    A rerun repeats the first test's mistakes, so its errors are not independent given the condition. Zhang et al. note that the same test run again adds no new insight <Cite id="zhang2023" />.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
