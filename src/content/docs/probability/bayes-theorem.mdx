## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="The notebook needs numbers you rarely have written down: how often it rains here, and how often the sprinkler runs on a dry night. Guess them wrong and the answer is wrong with them, however carefully you count. And the notebook only gives a share of mornings. It can say that most wet mornings followed rain without saying anything certain about this morning.">

You wake up, look outside, and the lawn is wet. Did it rain overnight? You know one thing well: when it rains, the lawn gets wet. But that answers the opposite question. It says how likely a wet lawn is given rain, and you want how likely rain is given a wet lawn. Answering the second question from the first is turning the question around, and the two answers can be far apart.

Thinking only about rain is the blind spot. Rain is not the only way to wet a lawn: a sprinkler on a timer wets it too, on nights when it didn't rain. So picture a notebook with a line for every morning of a long season. Mark the mornings after rain, and the mornings the lawn was wet. Now read only the wet mornings and ask what share of them followed rain. If it rains on few nights and the sprinkler runs on many dry ones, most wet mornings were the sprinkler's doing, even though rain almost always wets the lawn. The answer depends on three things at once: how often it rains, how often rain wets the lawn, and how often the lawn gets wet without rain.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
A trained classifier's output is an estimate of how likely each class is given the input, and that number depends on how common each class was in the training data, not only on what the input looks like. When a model trained on balanced data meets a world where one class is rare, or when a screening model flags rare fraud or a rare disease, Bayes' theorem says how much a positive is worth, and the answer turns on how common the class is as well as on the model's accuracy. The same rule underlies naive Bayes classifiers, spam filters, and Bayesian methods that keep a probability over a model's weights instead of a single best guess.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Test positive for a rare condition

Before you touch anything: 1 person in 100 has a certain condition. A test for it is right 9 times in 10, whether or not you have the condition. You take the test and it comes back positive. Is the chance that you have the condition closer to 90%, 50% or 10%? Make a guess, then test it.

Three numbers decide the answer, and the board has a slider for each. P(A) is how common A is before you learn anything: the prior, also called the base rate. For the medical test, A is "has the condition", B is "tests positive", and P(A) = 0.01. P(B | A) and P(B | not A) are how often a positive shows up with and without A: the likelihoods, 0.9 and 0.1 here.

The board is a unit square that stands for everyone. Read it in three steps. First, find A's column on the left: its width is P(A). Second, find the highlighted block in A's column, labeled "B and A": those are the true positives, and its height is P(B | A). Third, find the shaded strip across the rest of the square, labeled "B and not A": those are the false positives, and its height is P(B | not A). Then compare the two areas. Everything in B is the two pieces together, and P(A | B) is the highlighted block's share of them.

Under the square, the buttons after "Example:" load a scenario: Any two events, Medical test or Wet lawn. The line under them says what A and B stand for. The three sliders, P(A), P(B | A) and P(B | not A), each also move with the arrow keys. The readout gives P(A | B) and P(B) to three decimals. The board opens on Any two events, where it reads "P(A | B) = 0.774   P(B) = 0.310", and Reset returns there. Your task: press Medical test and read P(A | B).

Closer to 10%. The line under the presets says "A: has the condition (1 in 100). B: tests positive. The test is right 9 times in 10 either way." and the readout says "P(A | B) = 0.083   P(B) = 0.108". A positive test leaves about an 8% chance of having the condition: 1 in 12.

Counting people shows why. Picture 1,000 people. 10 of them have the condition, and the test catches 9 of those 10. The other 990 don't have it, but the test is wrong for 1 in 10 of them, so 99 of them test positive too. That makes 9 + 99 = 108 positives, and only 9 of the 108 have the condition: 9/108 = 1/12 ≈ 0.083. The 108 positives out of 1,000 are the readout's P(B) = 0.108. As a table:

The picture shows the same thing as areas. A's column is only 0.01 wide, so thin that its labels sit beside it, and the highlighted "B and A" block is a sliver of area 0.9 × 0.01 = 0.009. The "B and not A" strip is only 0.1 tall, but it runs across the other 0.99 of the square, so its area is 0.1 × 0.99 = 0.099, eleven times the sliver. Of everything in B, the true positives are the small part.

Now change only how common the condition is. Before you drag, predict: if 1 person in 10 had the condition instead of 1 in 100, with the same test, what would a positive mean? Then, still on Medical test, drag the P(A) slider from 0.01 to 0.10 and watch P(A | B). It climbs from 0.083 to 0.500. Now press Wet lawn. The line under the presets says "A: it rained overnight. B: the lawn is wet. A sprinkler can also wet it when it did not rain." Rain happens on 2 nights in 10, so dry nights are 8 in 10. Rain wets the lawn 9 times in 10; the sprinkler wets it on 3 dry nights in 10. The readout says "P(A | B) = 0.429   P(B) = 0.420". Turning the question around: given rain, a wet lawn has probability 0.9, but given a wet lawn, rain has probability only 3/7 ≈ 0.429. Of the 0.42, rain gives 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.18 and the sprinkler gives 0.3 × 0.8 = 0.24. A wet lawn makes rain more than twice as likely as the prior of 0.2, but rain is still less likely than not (below 0.5).

## Add up every way the evidence can happen

The probability of B is the sum of the probabilities of the separate ways B can happen. With an event A that splits the world in two, that is

P(B) = P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | not A) P(not A).

This is the [law of total probability](/probability/law-of-total-probability/), and it is how the board finds its P(B) readout.

### Why the two pieces add

Every outcome either is in A or is not, so A and not A split the [sample space](/probability/sample-spaces-and-events/) into two parts that share no outcome. B splits the same way, into "B and A" and "B and not A". Those two pieces can't happen together, so by the third of [the probability axioms](/probability/probability-axioms/) their probabilities add: P(B) = P(B and A) + P(B and not A). Then the [multiplication rule](/probability/conditional-probability/) rewrites each piece, P(B and A) = P(B | A) P(A) and P(B and not A) = P(B | not A) P(not A).

For the medical test: P(B) = 0.9 × 0.01 + 0.1 × 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.099 = 0.108. On the board, those two terms are the areas of the highlighted block and the shaded strip, and P(B) is their total.

### More than two ways

The same rule works for any finite number of pieces, as long as they share no outcome and together cover the whole sample space: add P(B | piece) P(piece) over all of them. Grinstead and Snell give it in this form in §4.1, as their equation (4.3) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />, and Kolmogorov states it in Chapter I, §4 as the theorem on total probability <Cite id="kolmogorov1950" />. Probabilities also add over an endless list of pieces ([countable additivity](/probability/probability-axioms/#countable-additivity)), so the same holds there, and pieces of probability 0 can be dropped, since they add nothing to P(B). The accuracy example on the [conditional probability](/probability/conditional-probability/) page used it with two groups: 0.95 × 0.9 + 0.60 × 0.1 = 0.915. The overall accuracy is a weighted average of the accuracy within each group, weighted by each group's share.

## Turn the question around

Bayes' theorem finds P(A | B) from P(B | A):

P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B).

It follows from the multiplication rule on the [conditional probability](/probability/conditional-probability/) page, written both ways around. P(A and B) = P(A | B) P(B), and also P(A and B) = P(B | A) P(A). The two right-hand sides are equal, and dividing by P(B), which must be greater than 0, gives the theorem. It also needs P(A) > 0, so that P(B | A) is defined. For the medical test, P(A | B) = 0.009 / 0.108 = 1/12.

### Prior, likelihood, evidence, posterior

Each part of the formula has a name.

- **Prior**, P(A): how likely A is before you see the evidence. For the medical test, 0.01.
- **Likelihood**, P(B | A): how likely the evidence is if A is true. 0.9.
- **Evidence**, P(B): how likely the evidence is overall, whether or not A is true, found by the law of total probability above. For the medical test, it is how likely a positive result is overall: 0.108.
- **Posterior**, P(A | B): how likely A is after you see the evidence. 1/12.

Grinstead and Snell call the possible explanations hypotheses, the observed event the evidence, and the probabilities before and after it the prior and posterior probabilities; their §4.1 gives Bayes' formula for any number of hypotheses (pp. 145–146) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. Kolmogorov proves the same theorem in Chapter I, §4, where P(A) is called the a priori probability <Cite id="kolmogorov1950" />. Putting the law of total probability into the bottom gives the version the board computes:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / (P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | not A) P(not A)).

### The base rate decides a lot

The posterior is the prior times the likelihood, divided by the total over every explanation. So the prior can move the answer as far as the test can. When A is rare, P(not A) is close to 1, and even a small P(B | not A) multiplied by it can outweigh P(B | A) P(A). That is why the P(A) slider alone took the medical test's posterior from 0.083 to 0.500 above. A test's accuracy alone does not say what a positive means. The [base rate fallacy in medical tests](/probability/base-rate-fallacy-medical-test/) page counts a whole population to show why.

### A second test: the posterior becomes the prior

Evidence can come in one piece at a time, and after each piece the posterior becomes the prior for the next. Suppose you test positive, then take a second, different test that is also right 9 times in 10 either way, and it is positive too. Assume the two tests' errors are independent given whether you have the condition: among people who have it, the second test is positive 9 times in 10 whatever the first said, and among people who don't, 1 time in 10 whatever the first said. Then Bayes' theorem uses the new prior 1/12 for having the condition, and 1 − 1/12 = 11/12 for not having it:

P(has it | both positive) = (0.9 × 1/12) ÷ (0.9 × 1/12 + 0.1 × 11/12) = 0.9 ÷ (0.9 + 1.1) = 9/20 = 0.45.

The middle step multiplies the top and the bottom by 12, which turns 0.1 × 11/12 into 0.1 × 11 = 1.1. Two positives move the chance from 1/100 to 1/12 to 9/20, which is still below an even chance. The second test has to make its mistakes independently of the first: running the same test again on the same sample doesn't count, because its mistakes repeat, and even a fresh sample isn't enough if the mistakes come from the person rather than the sample. The strength of a piece of evidence can be measured by its likelihood ratio, P(B | A) / P(B | not A): how many times more likely the evidence is if A is true than if it isn't. Each of these tests has a likelihood ratio of 0.9 / 0.1 = 9.

<Simpler label="the same 1,000 people as a tree, then flipped">

The table's four cells, 9, 1, 99 and 891, can be reached in two orders, and each order is a tree. The first tree splits the 1,000 people by the condition first: 10 have it, 990 don't. Then it splits each branch by the test: the 10 into 9 positive and 1 negative, the 990 into 99 positive and 891 negative. Its branches carry the numbers you are given: 1 in 100, then 9 in 10 and 1 in 10.

The flipped tree splits by the test first: 108 positive, 892 negative. Then it splits each by the condition: of the 108 positives, 9 have it and 99 don't; of the 892 negatives, 1 has it and 891 don't. Its branches carry the numbers you want, such as 9 in 108. The people at the ends are the same; only the order of the questions changed. Bayes' theorem is the arithmetic of flipping the tree, and a tree keeps working when a third or fourth split makes a table awkward. Grinstead and Snell draw the same picture and call it a reverse tree (§4.1, p. 136) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

</Simpler>

<GoDeeper label="the odds form: prior odds times the likelihood ratio" id="odds-form">

The odds of A are P(A) / P(not A), written like a bet: a probability of 1/100 is odds of 1 to 99, or 1:99. In odds, Bayes' theorem becomes one multiplication:

posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio,

with the likelihood ratio from above, P(B | A) / P(B | not A). To see why, write Bayes' theorem for A and for not A and divide one by the other. Both have P(B) at the bottom, so it cancels: P(A | B) / P(not A | B) = (P(A) / P(not A)) × (P(B | A) / P(B | not A)). This needs 0 < P(A) < 1, so that both odds and both likelihoods are defined, and P(B | not A) > 0, so that the ratio is. If P(B | not A) = 0, the evidence never happens without A, and P(A | B) = 1.

For the medical test, the prior odds are 1:99 and the likelihood ratio is 0.9 / 0.1 = 9. The posterior odds are 9:99 = 1:11, and odds of 1:11 mean a probability of 1/(1 + 11) = 1/12. For the wet lawn, prior odds 1:4 times a likelihood ratio of 0.9 / 0.3 = 3 give 3:4, a probability of 3/7. The odds form never needs P(B), and it shows at once how strong a piece of evidence is: a likelihood ratio of 9 multiplies the odds by 9, whatever they were.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper label="updating twice: counts, one step or two, and when the assumption fails" id="updating-twice">

In odds, the second positive takes 1:11 to 1:11 × 9 = 9:11, a probability of 9/20. Counting agrees. Of 10,000 people, 100 have the condition: 90 test positive the first time and 81 of those test positive again. Of the 9,900 who don't, 990 test positive the first time and 99 of those test positive again. So 81 of the 180 double positives have the condition, 81/180 = 9/20. Doing both updates at once gives the same answer: with prior 0.01 and likelihoods 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81 and 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01, the posterior is 0.0081 / (0.0081 + 0.0099) = 9/20.

Every route here leans on the independence assumption above, and it fails if both tests react to the same harmless cause, or if the second test is a rerun on the same sample. Then a false positive tends to repeat, the second positive carries less information, and 9/20 is usually too high. In general the second positive multiplies the odds by P(second positive | first positive, A) / P(second positive | first positive, not A). That ratio falls below 9 when false positives tend to repeat.

On the board, press Medical test and set the P(A) slider to 0.08, the nearest step to 1/12. The readout shows 0.439, close to 0.45.

</GoDeeper>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["sample-space", "event-collection", "event", "complement", "intersection", "probability", "conditional", "density"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space, as on the [probability axioms](/probability/probability-axioms/) page, and let $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ be events with $P(B) > 0$. If also $P(A) > 0$, so that $P(B \mid A)$ is defined, then

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}.
$$

If $0 < P(A) < 1$, the law of total probability gives $P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^\complement)\,P(A^\complement)$, so

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^\complement)\,P(A^\complement)}.
$$

More generally, let $H_1, H_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}$ be finitely or countably many events that are pairwise disjoint and cover $\Omega$. Then $P(B) = \sum_k P(B \cap H_k)$. Each term with $P(H_k) > 0$ equals $P(B \mid H_k)\,P(H_k)$, and each term with $P(H_k) = 0$ is 0 and can be dropped. So for every $i$ with $P(H_i) > 0$,

$$
P(H_i \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)}{\sum_{k \,:\, P(H_k) > 0} P(B \mid H_k)\,P(H_k)}.
$$

Boundary cases. If $P(B) = 0$, then $P(A \mid B)$ is undefined. A prior of 0 or 1 never moves, and both cases follow from the definition $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$, which needs only $P(B) > 0$: if $P(A) = 0$, then $P(A \mid B) = 0$, because $A \cap B \subseteq A$; if $P(A) = 1$, then $P(A \mid B) = 1$, because $P(B \cap A^\complement) \le P(A^\complement) = 0$. The last two cases need $0 < P(A) < 1$, so that both likelihoods are defined. If $P(B \mid A) = P(B \mid A^\complement)$ (both positive), the evidence is equally likely either way, and $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. If $P(B \mid A^\complement) = 0$, so the evidence never happens without $A$, then $P(A \mid B) = 1$.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="The law of total probability: P(B) = P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | Aᶜ) P(Aᶜ)" rigor="full">

1. Assume $0 < P(A) < 1$, so that $P(A^\complement) = 1 - P(A) > 0$ and both $P(B \mid A)$ and $P(B \mid A^\complement)$ are defined.
2. Every outcome is in exactly one of $A$ and $A^\complement$, so $B = (B \cap A) \cup (B \cap A^\complement)$.
3. The two pieces are disjoint: $(B \cap A) \cap (B \cap A^\complement) \subseteq A \cap A^\complement = \emptyset$.
4. Additivity (axiom 3) gives $P(B) = P(B \cap A) + P(B \cap A^\complement)$.
5. The multiplication rule rewrites each term: $P(B \cap A) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$ and $P(B \cap A^\complement) = P(B \mid A^\complement)\,P(A^\complement)$. Substituting gives the law. With $m$ pieces $H_1, \ldots, H_m$ instead of two, the pieces $B \cap H_k$ are pairwise disjoint, since $(B \cap H_j) \cap (B \cap H_k) \subseteq H_j \cap H_k = \emptyset$ for $j \ne k$, and finite additivity (proved on the [probability axioms](/probability/probability-axioms/) page) gives the sum of $m$ terms.

</StepThrough>

<StepThrough title="Bayes' theorem from the multiplication rule" rigor="full">

1. Assume $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$, so that $P(B \mid A)$ and $P(A \mid B)$ are both defined.
2. The multiplication rule, conditioning on $B$: $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)$.
3. The multiplication rule, conditioning on $A$: $P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$.
4. Both right-hand sides equal $P(A \cap B)$, so $P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$.
5. Divide both sides by $P(B) > 0$: $P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) / P(B)$.
6. If also $P(A) < 1$, replace $P(B)$ by the law of total probability above to get the expanded form. With hypotheses $H_1, \ldots, H_m$, the same steps with $H_i$ in place of $A$ and the $m$-term sum in place of $P(B)$ give the general form.

</StepThrough>

| Rule | Statement | Medical test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total probability | $P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^\complement)\,P(A^\complement)$ | $0.9 \times 0.01 + 0.1 \times 0.99 = 0.108$ |
| Bayes' theorem | $P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) / P(B)$ | $0.009 / 0.108 = 1/12$ |
| Complement of the posterior | $P(A^\complement \mid B) = 1 - P(A \mid B)$ | $11/12$ of positives don't have it |
| Odds form, for $0 < P(A) < 1$ and $P(B \mid A^\complement) > 0$ | $\dfrac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A^\complement \mid B)} = \dfrac{P(A)}{P(A^\complement)} \cdot \dfrac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B \mid A^\complement)}$ | $1/99 \times 9 = 1/11$ |
| Uninformative evidence | $P(B \mid A) = P(B \mid A^\complement)$ gives $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ | A test positive 1 time in 2 either way leaves $0.01$ |
| Prior of 0 | $P(A) = 0$ gives $P(A \mid B) = 0$ | No evidence can raise a probability of 0 |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="Which bag did the red marble come from?">

A fair coin picks one of two bags. Bag 1 holds 6 red marbles out of 10, bag 2 holds 2 red out of 10. You draw a red marble without seeing the bag. By the law of total probability, $P(\text{red}) = 0.6 \times 0.5 + 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.4$. By Bayes' theorem, $P(\text{bag 1} \mid \text{red}) = 0.6 \times 0.5 / 0.4 = 3/4$. Bag 1 holds three times as many red marbles, and both bags were equally likely to start with, so it gets three times the posterior: $3/4$ against $1/4$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="A better test, then a rarer condition">

A much better test is right 99 times in 100 either way. If 1 person in 100 has the condition, count 10,000 people: 100 have it and 99 of them test positive; 9,900 don't and 99 of them test positive. So $P(\text{has it} \mid \text{positive}) = 99/198 = 1/2$. Even a test that is right 99 times in 100 leaves a positive at an even chance.

Now make the condition rarer, 1 in 1,000, with the same test. Count 100,000 people: 100 have it and 99 test positive; 99,900 don't and 999 test positive. The posterior is $99/(99 + 999) = 99/1098 = 11/122 \approx 0.090$. With formulas: $P(B) = 0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.01 \times 0.999 = 0.01098$, and $0.00099 / 0.01098 = 11/122$. The test did not change, only the base rate, and the posterior fell from $1/2$ to about $0.09$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Three machines and a faulty part" faded>

A factory has three machines. Machine 1 makes half of all parts, machine 2 makes 30% and machine 3 makes 20%. Their parts are faulty 1%, 2% and 5% of the time. A part picked at random is faulty. Which machine most likely made it, and with what probability? Use the general form with three hypotheses.

<div slot="solution">

The three machines split the parts into three pieces that share nothing, so the law of total probability gives $P(\text{faulty}) = 0.01 \times 0.5 + 0.02 \times 0.3 + 0.05 \times 0.2 = 0.005 + 0.006 + 0.010 = 0.021$. Bayes' theorem for each machine divides its own term by that total: $P(\text{machine 1} \mid \text{faulty}) = 0.005 / 0.021 = 5/21$, $P(\text{machine 2} \mid \text{faulty}) = 0.006 / 0.021 = 2/7$, and $P(\text{machine 3} \mid \text{faulty}) = 0.010 / 0.021 = 10/21 \approx 0.476$. Machine 3 makes the fewest parts but is the most likely source, and two numbers pull against each other. Its fault rate is five times machine 1's, but machine 1 makes 2.5 times as many parts, so the net ratio is $5 / 2.5 = 2$ to 1: $10/21$ against $5/21$. The three posteriors add up to $21/21 = 1$, as they must.

## Where Bayes' theorem shows up in ML

### A classifier's output is a posterior estimate

A classifier with a softmax output layer produces a vector $\hat{y}$ with $\hat{y}_i = P(y = i \mid x)$: an estimate of the probability of class $i$ given the input $x$ <Cite id="goodfellow2016" />. In Bayes' terms that is a posterior. The theorem connects it to two other quantities:

$$
P(y = i \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid y = i)\,P(y = i)}{\sum_{j} p(x \mid y = j)\,P(y = j)},
$$

where $p(x \mid y = i)$ is how likely an input like $x$ is within class $i$ (for continuous inputs, such as pixel values, it is a [density](/probability/probability-density-functions/)), and $P(y = i)$ is how common class $i$ is. A model trained to output $P(y \mid x)$ directly folds both into one number, including how common each class was in its training data.

### When the base rate shifts

The same evidence gives a different posterior when the prior changes. The medical test showed it: with the same test, a positive means $1/12$ when 1 person in 100 has the condition and $1/2$ when 1 in 10 does. A classifier carries the class frequencies of its training data in the same way. A common correction multiplies each output by a correction factor, the ratio of the new class frequency to the old one, and rescales the results to add up to 1. Saerens and coauthors derive it from Bayes' theorem (§2.2) <Cite id="saerens2002" />. It rests on three assumptions:

- $p(x \mid y)$ is the same in training and in use;
- the outputs are calibrated, meaning the scores are honest frequencies: among training inputs that get a score of 0.9 for a class, 9 in 10 belong to it. This must hold for every class's score, not only for the most confident one, so that each score equals the training-set probability of that class given all the scores;
- the new class frequencies are known. In practice they have to be estimated; the same paper does it with the EM algorithm (§2.3).

For example, a model trained on a set that is half positive outputs 0.9 for some input, but in use only 1 case in 100 is positive. The correction factors are $0.01/0.5 = 0.02$ for positive and $0.99/0.5 = 1.98$ for negative. The scores become $0.9 \times 0.02 = 0.018$ and $0.1 \times 1.98 = 0.198$, and rescaling gives $0.018 / 0.216 = 1/12$, the medical test's posterior again.

### Naive Bayes

A naive Bayes classifier applies Bayes' theorem with many features $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, such as the words in an email, and adds one assumption: the features are [independent given the class](/probability/conditional-independence/). Then $P(x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid y) = \prod_k P(x_k \mid y)$, where $\prod$ means "the product of", and

$$
P(y \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n) \propto P(y) \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(x_k \mid y),
$$

where $\propto$ means proportional to: equal up to a factor that doesn't depend on $y$. Each $P(x_k \mid y)$ is easy to estimate by counting (for discrete features; continuous ones get a fitted density), which makes naive Bayes cheap to train. The independence assumption is usually false for real features, which is where the name comes from. It is the same assumption as the second test above, whose errors were independent of the first's given the condition: each feature then multiplies in its own likelihood. When features are correlated, the same evidence is counted twice, which pushes the classifier's probabilities toward 0 and 1 even when the predicted class is right. That is the same mechanism as the correlated second test. [Naive Bayes](/probability/naive-bayes-classifier/) has a page of its own.

## Research frontier

<ResearchFrontier reviewed="2026-11-24">

**A posterior over a network's weights.** Bayes' theorem applies to a whole model. Take the weights $w$ as the hypothesis and the training data $D$, pairs $(x_i, y_i)$, as the evidence. The weights are continuous, so the theorem is written with densities:

$$
\begin{gathered}
p(w \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid w)\,p(w)}{p(D)}, \qquad p(D) = \int p(D \mid w)\,p(w)\,dw, \\
p(D \mid w) = \prod_i P(y_i \mid x_i, w).
\end{gathered}
$$

The product assumes the training examples are independent given the weights, with the inputs taken as given. Instead of one best set of weights, this gives a probability distribution (a density) over weight settings, which says how uncertain the model is. It is conditioning on a random variable, the case the [conditional probability frontier](/probability/conditional-probability/#research-frontier) describes. Blundell and coauthors open by noting that plain networks often make overly confident decisions, and that exact Bayesian inference on a network's weights is intractable, because there are very many weights and the network's form does not allow the integrals to be done exactly. They therefore fit a variational approximation $q(w \mid \theta)$, a simpler distribution over the weights chosen to be close to the true posterior, and train it with backpropagation; they call the method Bayes by Backprop (Section 1) <Cite id="blundell2015" />.

The cost they minimize, their equation (1), is the variational free energy. The paper writes it in script; here it is a plain $F$, because $\mathcal{F}$ on this site is the collection of events:

$$
F(D, \theta) = \mathrm{KL}\big(q(w \mid \theta) \,\|\, p(w)\big) - \mathbb{E}_{q(w \mid \theta)}\big[\log p(D \mid w)\big].
$$

Writing out the KL divergence from $q$ to the true posterior, and using Bayes' theorem above, turns their equation (1) into the identity

$$
\log p(D) = -F(D, \theta) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q(w \mid \theta) \,\|\, p(w \mid D)\big).
$$

The left side does not depend on $\theta$, so making $F$ smaller brings $q$ closer to the posterior. Since a KL divergence is never negative, $-F$ is a lower bound on $\log p(D)$, which the abstract calls the expected lower bound on the marginal likelihood <Cite id="blundell2015" />. The hard part is exactly the denominator $p(D)$, the evidence, an integral over every weight setting; the identity lets the method work without ever computing it. Even with the posterior in hand, a prediction averages over it, which their Section 3 calls equivalent to an ensemble of infinitely many networks and intractable for networks of any practical size <Cite id="blundell2015" />.

**Where the prior comes from.** The same paper notes that choosing the prior's settings from the same data the prior is meant to regularize, called empirical Bayes, is a subject of much debate, and reports that doing so worked poorly for their method (Section 3.3) <Cite id="blundell2015" />. How to pick a sensible prior over millions of weights, and how close a given approximation comes to the exact posterior, are questions a single paper does not settle.

</ResearchFrontier>

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="Your medical test comes back negative. Is the chance that you have the condition now exactly 0?">
No, but it is very small. A negative is the evidence now, and the test misses 1 in 10 people who have the condition. Bayes' theorem with $P(\text{negative} \mid \text{has it}) = 0.1$ and $P(\text{negative} \mid \text{doesn't}) = 0.9$ gives $0.1 \times 0.01 / (0.1 \times 0.01 + 0.9 \times 0.99) = 0.001/0.892 = 1/892$. Counting 1,000 people: 1 of the 892 negatives has it.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="For the medical test, which raises P(A | B) more: catching every case, P(B | A) = 1, or halving the false positives, P(B | not A) = 0.05?">
Halving the false positives. With $P(B \mid A) = 1$: $0.01 / (0.01 + 0.099) = 10/109 \approx 0.092$, barely up from $0.083$. With $P(B \mid A^\complement) = 0.05$: $0.009 / (0.009 + 0.0495) = 2/13 \approx 0.154$. When the condition is rare, the false positives from the large "not A" group make up most of $P(B)$, so cutting them helps most. Try both on the board: P(B | A) at its top, 0.99, gives 0.091; P(B | not A) at 0.05 gives 0.154.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question='2 emails in 10 are spam. The word "prize" appears in 30% of spam emails and in 5% of the others. An email contains "prize". What is P(spam | prize)?'>
$3/5 = 0.6$. The law of total probability gives $P(\text{prize}) = 0.3 \times 0.2 + 0.05 \times 0.8 = 0.06 + 0.04 = 0.10$, and Bayes' theorem gives $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{prize}) = 0.06 / 0.10 = 3/5$. In odds: the prior odds of spam are 1:4, the word is $0.3 / 0.05 = 6$ times as likely in spam, and $1:4 \times 6 = 6:4 = 3:2$, a probability of $3/5$. Spam is the rarer kind of email, but the word is strong enough evidence to make it the likelier one.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that if 0 < P(A) < 1 and P(B | A) = P(B | not A) > 0, then P(A | B) = P(A).">
Call the common value $q$. The law of total probability gives $P(B) = q\,P(A) + q\,(1 - P(A)) = q > 0$, so Bayes' theorem gives $P(A \mid B) = q\,P(A)/q = P(A)$. Evidence that is equally likely whether or not $A$ holds can't change how likely $A$ is. The [Monty Hall problem](/probability/monty-hall-problem/) is this case: the host's door is equally likely whether or not the car is behind yours. On the board, set P(B | A) and P(B | not A) to the same value and the readout's P(A | B) equals the P(A) slider.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="The test is right 90% of the time, so a positive means a 90% chance of having the condition.">
    That swaps the two directions. 90% is $P(\text{positive} \mid \text{has it})$; the question asks for $P(\text{has it} \mid \text{positive})$, which here is $1/12$. The same swap happens in court when "the evidence would be very unlikely if the person were innocent" is read as "the person is very unlikely to be innocent". The first is P(evidence | innocent), the second P(innocent | evidence), and they can differ, as the [conditional probability](/probability/conditional-probability/) page showed with hats and scarves. This page's medical test shows how far: 0.9 against 1/12.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Only the test's accuracy matters; how common the condition is doesn't change what a positive means.">
    The base rate changes it a lot. The same test, right 9 times in 10, gives a positive a $1/12$ chance when 1 person in 100 has the condition and exactly $1/2$ when 1 in 10 does. Ignoring the prior is the most common way to misread a positive result.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="P(B) is just the true positives, P(B | A) P(A): the people who have the condition and test positive.">
    P(B) counts every positive, including the false ones. Leave out the false-positive term $P(B \mid A^\complement)\,P(A^\complement)$ and the medical test's P(B) becomes $0.009$ instead of $0.108$, so the "posterior" comes out as $0.009/0.009 = 1$. The law of total probability adds every way B can happen.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="After Bayes' theorem, I know whether A is true.">
    A posterior is still a probability. After one positive, $1/12$ means 11 of every 12 people in your position don't have the condition. New evidence moves it again, and it is only as good as the prior and the likelihoods that went in. Only a prior of exactly 0 or 1, or evidence that is impossible one way, gives certainty.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
