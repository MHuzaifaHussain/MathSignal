## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="The cups only work because your friend peeked and always lifts an empty cup. If they knocked a cup over by accident and it happened to be empty, swapping would win only half the time. And if they always lifted the right-hand cup when they could, the answer would change again.">

Three cups sit upside down, and one hides a sweet. You point at the left cup. Your friend peeked earlier, so they know where the sweet is. They always lift an empty cup from the other two, and when both are empty they flip a coin to choose. Keep your cup or swap?

Now picture 100 cups. You point at one, and your friend lifts 98 empty cups, leaving one other cup down. Your first point was right only 1 time in 100, so swapping wins the other 99 times.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
A model only sees the data that some process chose to show it, and whether that process knew the answer decides what the data proves. The host's door is the smallest example: an open door means one thing when he chose it knowing where the car is, and another when he fell against it. Reading it takes [Bayes' theorem](/probability/bayes-theorem/) and an account of how the evidence arose.
</WhyThisMatters>

## Six equally likely games, shrunk to the door the host opened

A car is behind one of three doors, each equally likely, with goats behind the others. You pick door 1. The host knows where the car is, always opens another door hiding a goat, and offers the switch. He tosses a coin in every game, but it decides only when both other doors hide goats: heads he opens door 2, tails door 3.

Before you count: you picked door 1 and the host opened door 3. Switch, stick, or does it not matter? And as a number: of all the games where he opens door 3, what share have the car behind door 1?

Your goal: shrink the world to "host opens door 3", then read P(A | B) as on the [conditional probability](/probability/conditional-probability/) page, with A set to "car behind door 1", then "car behind door 2". Each cell is one game, with probability 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6, and names the door the host opens.

| Car behind | Coin heads | Coin tails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **door 1** | opens 2 | opens 3 |
| **door 2** | opens 3 | opens 3 |
| **door 3** | opens 2 | opens 2 |

Switch. B holds 3 of the 6 cells, and 1 has the car behind door 1, so P(car 1 | opens 3) = 1/3. For door 2 it is 2 of 3, so P(car 2 | opens 3) = 2/3; the car-3 row has no cell in B. If he opens door 2, the count gives 1/3 for door 1 and 2/3 for door 3.

Now read the rows. The host opens door 3 in 1 of 2 cells of row 1, both cells of row 2, and neither of row 3. Those shares, 1/2, 1 and 0, are how likely his action is for each place the car could be, and they are the whole puzzle. The coin column is his tie-break.

## Decide before the host moves: stick wins 1 game in 3

Decided in advance, sticking wins 1 game in 3 and switching wins the other 2. In 300 games the car is behind door 1 in 100 on average, and sticking wins those. In the other 200, the host has opened the only other goat door, so switching lands on the car.

Grinstead and Snell call this count correct but note that it does not quite answer the question asked, which is conditional: switching given that you chose door 1 and the host opened door 3 (Example 4.6, p. 137) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. The table answered that, and agrees.

## Turn it around with Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem gives the same 2/3 with three hypotheses. Write C₁, C₂ and C₃ for "the car is behind door 1, 2 or 3", and O₃ for "the host opens door 3".

<StepThrough title="P(C₂ | O₃) by Bayes' theorem" rigor="full">

1. Priors: P(C₁) = P(C₂) = P(C₃) = 1/3.
2. Likelihoods: P(O₃ | C₁) = 1/2 (the coin), P(O₃ | C₂) = 1 (his only goat door), P(O₃ | C₃) = 0 (he never shows the car).
3. Total probability: P(O₃) = 1/6 + 1/3 + 0 = 1/2.
4. Posteriors: P(C₁ | O₃) = (1/6)/(1/2) = 1/3 and P(C₂ | O₃) = (1/3)/(1/2) = 2/3.

</StepThrough>

Grinstead and Snell's tree also includes your pick of door 1, so its two surviving paths have probabilities 1/18 and 1/9, and the car-2 path is twice as likely (Figures 4.3 and 4.4, pp. 138–139) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

Your door stays at 1/3 because the host opens door 3 half the time either way: if the car isn't behind door 1, it is behind door 2 or 3 with probability 1/2 each, so P(O₃ | not C₁) = 1 × 1/2 + 0 × 1/2 = 1/2. That is the uninformative case on the [Bayes' theorem](/probability/bayes-theorem/) page.

<Simpler label="the same answer as a ratio">

With equal priors, the posteriors are proportional to the likelihoods, 1/2 : 1 : 0. Divide by their total, 3/2, and they become 1/3 : 2/3 : 0. Rosenthal calls this the proportionality principle (§3), and his appendix shows that it is Bayes' theorem restated <Cite id="rosenthal2008" />. In the [odds form](/probability/bayes-theorem/#odds-form), door 2 against door 1 starts at 1 : 1, the likelihood ratio is 1 ÷ 1/2 = 2, and 2 : 1 is a probability of 2/3.

</Simpler>

## Why the host's rule decides the answer

The answer depends on how the open door was chosen. Grinstead and Snell list the assumptions behind 2/3: each door equally likely for the car, a host who knows where it is and always opens a goat door, and, when he has a choice, each door with probability 1/2 (p. 137) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. Each fold below changes one rule; worked examples 3 and 4 repeat them.

<GoDeeper label="a host who opens a door by accident" id="falling-host">

In Rosenthal's Monty Fall, the host slips and pushes open a door that happens to hide a goat (§2) <Cite id="rosenthal2008" />. If he falls against door 3, the likelihoods that it shows a goat are 1, 1 and 0, so the posteriors are 1/2, 1/2 and 0 (§4) <Cite id="rosenthal2008" />. The argument that your door keeps 1/3 because the host's action changes nothing works for the knowing host but fails here, which is why Rosenthal calls it shaky.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper label="a host who prefers door 3" id="tie-break">

If the host, given a choice, opens door 3 with probability q, the likelihoods of O₃ become q, 1, 0, and switching after door 3 wins 1/(1 + q). Rosenthal works this as Monty Small, with p for the tie-break (§4) <Cite id="rosenthal2008" />; this page writes q, since p belongs to densities. With q = 3/4, switching after door 3 wins 4/7, while the switch strategy decided in advance still wins 2/3 (Grinstead and Snell, p. 139) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

</GoDeeper>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["sample-space", "outcome", "event", "intersection", "complement", "probability", "conditional", "cardinality", "car-behind", "host-opens", "host-tie-break", "class-label"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

You pick door 1. An outcome $\omega = (c, o) \in \Omega$ records the car's door $c \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ and the door $o \in \{2, 3\}$ the host opens; $C_i = \{c = i\}$ and $O_j = \{o = j\}$. Assume (i) $P(C_i) = 1/3$; (ii) the host never opens door 1 or the car's door, and always offers the switch; (iii) when $c = 1$, he opens door 3 with probability $q \in [0, 1]$. So $P(1, 2) = (1 - q)/3$, $P(1, 3) = q/3$, $P(2, 3) = P(3, 2) = 1/3$, and

$$
P(C_2 \mid O_3) = \frac{1}{1 + q}, \quad P(C_1 \mid O_3) = \frac{q}{1 + q}, \quad P(C_3 \mid O_2) = \frac{1}{2 - q}, \quad P(C_1 \mid O_2) = \frac{1 - q}{2 - q}.
$$

Boundary cases. $P(O_3) = (1 + q)/3$ and $P(O_2) = (2 - q)/3$ are at least $1/3$, so both conditionals exist. With $q = 1/2$, switching wins $2/3$, and on the six equally likely (car, coin) cells of the table, $|O_3| = 3$ and $|C_1 \cap O_3| = 1$; with $q = 1$, $O_3$ leaves $1/2$ each; with $q = 0$, $O_3$ makes $C_2$ certain. If the host doesn't know where the car is, (ii) fails: given that an accidental door 3 shows a goat, $C_1$ and $C_1^\complement$ have $1/2$ each.

</PreciseStatement>

<FormalDefinition>

<StepThrough title="Switching wins 1/(1 + q) after door 3, and 2/3 overall, for every q" rigor="full">

1. $C_1, C_2, C_3$ split $\Omega$, each with probability $1/3 > 0$.
2. By (ii) and (iii): $P(O_3 \mid C_1) = q$, $P(O_3 \mid C_2) = 1$, $P(O_3 \mid C_3) = 0$.
3. Total probability: $P(O_3) = (1 + q)/3 > 0$.
4. Bayes' theorem: $P(C_2 \mid O_3) = (1/3) \big/ \big((1 + q)/3\big) = 1/(1 + q)$. With $O_2$, likelihoods $1 - q$, $0$, $1$, the same steps give $P(C_3 \mid O_2) = 1/(2 - q)$.
5. Overall: $P(O_3) \cdot \frac{1}{1 + q} + P(O_2) \cdot \frac{1}{2 - q} = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$.
6. Since $q \le 1$, $\frac{1}{1 + q} \ge \frac{1}{2} \ge \frac{q}{1 + q}$: after door 3 switching never loses to sticking, and ties only at $q = 1$.

</StepThrough>

| Host's rule | Switching wins |
| --- | --- |
| Classic, $q = 1/2$ | $2/3$ |
| Falls against a door that hides a goat | $1/2$ |
| $q = 3/4$ | $4/7$ after door 3, $4/5$ after door 2 |
| $q = 0$ | $1$ after door 3, $1/2$ after door 2 |
| $n$ doors, $n - 2$ goat doors opened | $(n - 1)/n$ |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="600 games, counted">

In 600 classic games the car is behind each door in 200. Car behind door 1: the coin sends the host to door 2 in 100 and door 3 in 100. Car behind door 2: door 3 in all 200. Car behind door 3: door 2 in all 200. Given door 3, 300 games remain, 100 and 200: $1/3$ and $2/3$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="Find the wrong step">

A student writes: (a) "Two doors are left, so each has 1/2." (b) "The host picks one of three doors, so $P(O_3) = 1/3$." With (b), the posteriors come out as $(1/6)/(1/3) = 1/2$, $(1/3)/(1/3) = 1$ and $0$, which add up to $3/2$, so (b) must be wrong: he never opens door 1, and $P(O_3) = 1/2$. Step (a) ignores the unequal likelihoods, $1/2$ and $1$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="The falling host gives 1/2">

The host falls against door 2 or door 3 with probability 1/2 each, whatever is behind them. In 600 games he falls against door 3 in 300, 100 per car position. In 100 he reveals the car, so they are not your game; the other 200 split 100 and 100: $1/2$ each. An accidental door reveals the car $100/300 = 1/3$ of the time.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={4} title="A host who prefers door 3, q = 3/4">

After door 3: $P(O_3) = (1 + 3/4)/3 = 7/12$, switching wins $1/(1 + 3/4) = 4/7$ and sticking $3/7$. After door 2: $P(O_2) = (2 - 3/4)/3 = 5/12$, and switching wins $1/(2 - 3/4) = 4/5$. Overall, $7/12 \times 4/7 + 5/12 \times 4/5 = 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3$, as for the classic host.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={5} title="Unequal doors: find the wrong step">

In Grinstead and Snell's Exercise 63(b) (pp. 161–162), the car is behind door A 45% of the time, B 40% and C 15%, and you pick A <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. A student says "always switch". If the host opens C, the products are $0.45 \times 1/2 = 0.225$ and $0.40$, total $0.625$: sticking wins $0.36$, switching $0.64$. If he opens B, they are $0.225$ and $0.15$, total $0.375$: sticking wins $0.6$, switching $0.4$, so the step fails. Better still, pick C and then switch: that wins $1 - 0.15 = 0.85$, against $0.55$ for picking A and switching.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={6} title="Four doors" faded>

You pick door 1 of four, and the host opens a goat door at random from the other three: door 4. The likelihoods are $1/3$, $1/2$, $1/2$, $0$ and the priors $1/4$, so the products are $1/12$, $1/8$, $1/8$, $0$, with total $1/3$. Finish the posteriors: does switching help?

<div slot="solution">

Divide by $1/3$: $1/4$, $3/8$, $3/8$, $0$. Sticking wins $1/4$, and switching to door 2 (or door 3) wins $3/8$, so switch.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Who chose the data: Monty Hall and selection bias

The host's door is data chosen by a process that depends on the unknown answer, and training data can be chosen that way too. Zadrozny gives each example $(x, y)$ a selection variable $s$, where only examples with $s = 1$ are seen. When $s$ depends only on the label $y$ (her case 3), the selected sample has different class priors (§2) <Cite id="zadrozny2004" />.

Take 1,000 examples with 200 positives, a share of 0.2. Keep positives with probability 0.9 and negatives with 0.3: on average 180 + 240 = 420 are kept, and the positive share becomes 180/420 = 3/7 ≈ 0.429. Weighting each kept example by 1 over its label's keep probability gives 180/0.9 = 200 and 240/0.3 = 800, recovering 0.2. The correction for a shifted class share is on the [Bayes' theorem](/probability/bayes-theorem/#when-the-base-rate-shifts) page.

When $s$ depends only on $x$ instead (case 2), a model of $P(y \mid x)$ such as logistic regression is unaffected (§3.2), while naive Bayes is affected (§3.1.1) <Cite id="zadrozny2004" />.

import random
from fractions import Fraction as F


def posterior(priors, likelihoods):
    """Exact posteriors by Bayes' theorem, and the total P(evidence)."""
    products = [prior * likelihood for prior, likelihood in zip(priors, likelihoods)]
    total = sum(products)
    if total == 0:
        raise ValueError("the evidence can't happen (probability 0)")
    return [product / total for product in products], total


def knowing_host(q, opened):
    """P(host opens this door | car behind door 1, 2, 3), you picked door 1."""
    if opened == 3:
        return [q, F(1), F(0)]
    if opened == 2:
        return [1 - q, F(0), F(1)]
    raise ValueError("the host opens door 2 or door 3")


def decimal(value, places):
    return f"{float(value):.{places}f}"


uniform = [F(1, 3)] * 3
half = F(1, 2)

# Way 1: the six equally likely games (car, coin) and the door the host opens.
opens = {(1, "H"): 2, (1, "T"): 3, (2, "H"): 3, (2, "T"): 3, (3, "H"): 2, (3, "T"): 2}
assert len(opens) == 6 and F(1, 3) * F(1, 2) == F(1, 6)
opened_3 = {cell for cell, door in opens.items() if door == 3}
assert len(opened_3) == 3
assert [len({cell for cell in opened_3 if cell[0] == car}) for car in (1, 2, 3)] == [1, 2, 0]
assert [F(len({cell for cell in opened_3 if cell[0] == car}), len(opened_3)) for car in (1, 2)] == [F(1, 3), F(2, 3)]
opened_2 = {cell for cell, door in opens.items() if door == 2}
assert [F(len({cell for cell in opened_2 if cell[0] == car}), len(opened_2)) for car in (1, 3)] == [F(1, 3), F(2, 3)]
# Reading the rows: the host opens door 3 in 1 of 2, 2 of 2 and 0 of 2 cells.
assert [F(sum(1 for coin in "HT" if opens[(car, coin)] == 3), 2) for car in (1, 2, 3)] == [half, 1, 0]

# Way 2: decide in advance, 300 games.
assert 300 * F(1, 3) == 100 and 300 - 100 == 200
assert F(100, 300) == F(1, 3) and F(200, 300) == F(2, 3)

# Way 3: Bayes' theorem.
classic_3, p_open_3 = posterior(uniform, knowing_host(half, 3))
assert p_open_3 == F(1, 6) + F(1, 3) + 0 == half
assert classic_3 == [F(1, 3), F(2, 3), F(0)]
assert F(1, 6) / half == F(1, 3) and F(1, 3) / half == F(2, 3)
classic_2, p_open_2 = posterior(uniform, knowing_host(half, 2))
assert classic_2 == [F(1, 3), F(0), F(2, 3)] and p_open_2 == half
# The tree paths include your pick of door 1 (probability 1/3).
path_1 = F(1, 3) * F(1, 3) * half
path_2 = F(1, 3) * F(1, 3) * 1
assert (path_1, path_2) == (F(1, 18), F(1, 9)) and path_2 / path_1 == 2
# Your door: P(O3 | not C1) = 1 x 1/2 + 0 x 1/2 = 1/2, the same as P(O3 | C1).
assert 1 * half + 0 * half == half == knowing_host(half, 3)[0]
# The ratio: likelihoods 1/2 : 1 : 0, total 3/2; odds 1 : 1 times 2 gives 2 : 1.
assert sum(knowing_host(half, 3)) == F(3, 2)
assert [x / F(3, 2) for x in knowing_host(half, 3)] == classic_3
assert F(1) / half == 2 and F(2, 2 + 1) == F(2, 3)

# The falling host: likelihoods 1, 1, 0 that door 3 shows a goat.
fall, _ = posterior(uniform, [F(1), F(1), F(0)])
assert fall == [half, half, F(0)]

# The tie-break q, for several values, and the overall 2/3.
for q in (F(0), F(1, 4), half, F(3, 4), F(1)):
    after_3, p3 = posterior(uniform, knowing_host(q, 3))
    after_2, p2 = posterior(uniform, knowing_host(q, 2))
    assert p3 == (1 + q) / 3 and p2 == (2 - q) / 3 and min(p3, p2) >= F(1, 3)
    assert after_3[1] == 1 / (1 + q) and after_3[0] == q / (1 + q)
    assert after_2[2] == 1 / (2 - q) and after_2[0] == (1 - q) / (2 - q)
    assert p3 * after_3[1] + p2 * after_2[2] == F(1, 3) + F(1, 3) == F(2, 3)
    assert after_3[1] >= half >= after_3[0]
    assert (after_3[1] == after_3[0]) == (q == 1) and (after_2[2] == after_2[0]) == (q == 0)
# The four path probabilities in the precise statement.
for q in (F(0), F(1, 4), F(3, 4)):
    paths = {(1, 2): (1 - q) / 3, (1, 3): q / 3, (2, 3): F(1, 3), (3, 2): F(1, 3)}
    assert sum(paths.values()) == 1
# q = 0 (Monty Crawl) and q = 1.
assert posterior(uniform, knowing_host(F(0), 3))[0][1] == 1
assert posterior(uniform, knowing_host(F(0), 2))[0] == [half, F(0), half]
assert posterior(uniform, knowing_host(F(1), 3))[0] == [half, half, F(0)]

# Worked example 1: 600 games.
games = 600
car = [games * p for p in uniform]
assert car == [200, 200, 200]
assert (car[0] * half, car[0] * half) == (100, 100)
door_3 = car[0] * half + car[1]
assert door_3 == 300 and F(100, 300) == F(1, 3) and F(200, 300) == F(2, 3)

# Worked example 2: the wrong P(O3) = 1/3 gives posteriors that add up to 3/2.
wrong = [F(1, 6) / F(1, 3), F(1, 3) / F(1, 3), F(0)]
assert wrong == [half, 1, 0] and sum(wrong) == F(3, 2)
assert F(1, 6) + F(1, 3) == half

# Worked example 3: the falling host, 600 games.
falls_on_3 = games * half
assert falls_on_3 == 300 and [falls_on_3 * p for p in uniform] == [100, 100, 100]
kept = 100 + 100
assert kept == 200 and F(100, kept) == half and F(100, 300) == F(1, 3)

# Worked example 4: q = 3/4 (Grinstead and Snell, p. 139).
q = F(3, 4)
after_3, p3 = posterior(uniform, knowing_host(q, 3))
after_2, p2 = posterior(uniform, knowing_host(q, 2))
assert p3 == F(7, 12) and after_3[1] == F(4, 7) and after_3[0] == F(3, 7)
assert p2 == F(5, 12) and after_2[2] == F(4, 5)
assert F(7, 12) * F(4, 7) + F(5, 12) * F(4, 5) == F(1, 3) + F(1, 3) == F(2, 3)

# Worked example 5: unequal doors A, B, C (Exercise 63(b)); you pick A.
doors = [F(45, 100), F(40, 100), F(15, 100)]
open_c, p_open_c = posterior(doors, [half, F(1), F(0)])
assert [d * l for d, l in zip(doors, [half, F(1)])] == [F(225, 1000), F(40, 100)]
assert p_open_c == F(625, 1000) == F(5, 8)
assert (open_c[0], open_c[1]) == (F(9, 25), F(16, 25))
assert (decimal(open_c[0], 2), decimal(open_c[1], 2)) == ("0.36", "0.64")
open_b, p_open_b = posterior(doors, [half, F(0), F(1)])
assert doors[2] * 1 == F(15, 100) and p_open_b == F(375, 1000) == F(3, 8)
assert (open_b[0], open_b[2]) == (F(3, 5), F(2, 5))
assert (decimal(open_b[0], 1), decimal(open_b[2], 1)) == ("0.6", "0.4")
assert 1 - doors[2] == F(85, 100) and 1 - doors[0] == F(55, 100)
assert (decimal(1 - doors[2], 2), decimal(1 - doors[0], 2)) == ("0.85", "0.55")

# Worked example 6: four doors, door 4 opened.
four = [F(1, 4)] * 4
likelihoods = [F(1, 3), half, half, F(0)]
assert [p * l for p, l in zip(four, likelihoods)] == [F(1, 12), F(1, 8), F(1, 8), F(0)]
four_after, total = posterior(four, likelihoods)
assert total == F(1, 3) and four_after == [F(1, 4), F(3, 8), F(3, 8), F(0)]


def one_door_opened(n):
    """Switching to one particular closed door, n doors, the host opens one goat door."""
    priors = [F(1, n)] * n
    likes = [F(1, n - 1)] + [F(1, n - 2)] * (n - 2) + [F(0)]
    return posterior(priors, likes)[0][1]

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="There are 100 doors. You pick one, and a host who knows where the car is opens 98 goat doors. Switch?">
Yes: switching wins 99 times in 100, because your pick was right only 1 time in 100.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A host always opens the lowest-numbered goat door he can (q = 0), and he opens door 2. Should you switch? What if he opens door 3?">
After door 2, it doesn't matter: likelihoods 1, 0, 1 leave $1/2$ each. After door 3, switching wins for certain, since he opens door 3 only when door 2 holds the car.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A host who doesn't know where the car is opens door 2 by accident, and it shows a goat. How often does switching win?">
$1/2$. In 600 games he falls against door 2 in 300, 100 per car position. In 100 he shows the car; the other 200 split evenly between doors 1 and 3.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Show that always switching wins 2/3 for every q in [0, 1], and that P(C₂ | O₃) is at least 1/2.">
Switching wins $P(C_2 \cap O_3) + P(C_3 \cap O_2) = 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3$, since $C_2 \subseteq O_3$ and $C_3 \subseteq O_2$; $q$ never enters. And $P(C_2 \mid O_3) = 1/(1 + q) \ge 1/2$ because $1 + q \le 2$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Two doors are left, so it's 50/50.">
    The host's action has likelihood $1/2$ under your door and $1$ under the other, so the other door ends at $2/3$.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The host's reveal never changes the chance of your own door.">
    It stays $1/3$ for the classic host, but is $1/2$ for a falling host and $3/7$ after door 3 when $q = 3/4$.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Switching wins 2/3, whatever the host does.">
    Only the strategy decided in advance wins $2/3$ for every tie-break. After door 3 with $q = 3/4$ switching wins $4/7$, and with unequal doors sticking wins $0.6$ after the host opens B.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The host picks one of three doors, so P(host opens door 3) = 1/3.">
    He never opens your door, so $P(O_3) = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2$. Using $1/3$ makes the posteriors add up to $3/2$.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
