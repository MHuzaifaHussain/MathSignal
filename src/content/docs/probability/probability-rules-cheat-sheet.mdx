## The marble bag every example uses

The examples in the tables draw from one bag of 10 marbles: 4 red and 6 blue, and some of each are striped. You draw one marble, and every marble is equally likely. Some examples draw more than once. With replacement, the marble goes back before the next draw, so the bag is the same each time; without replacement, it stays out. Each example says which.

| Marbles | Striped | Plain | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Red** | 3 | 1 | 4 |
| **Blue** | 2 | 4 | 6 |
| **Total** | 5 | 5 | 10 |

So $P(\text{red}) = 4/10 = 2/5$, $P(\text{blue}) = 3/5$, $P(\text{striped}) = 5/10 = 1/2$ and $P(\text{red} \cap \text{striped}) = 3/10$.

A second setup appears only where a rule needs cases: two bags. A fair coin picks bag 1, which holds 8 red marbles out of 10, or bag 2, which holds 4 red out of 10, and you draw from the bag it picked. When you draw twice from the bag the coin picked, the first marble goes back into that bag before the second draw.

## Which rule do I need?

The wording of the question names the rule. Match the phrase, then check the warning before you calculate.

| The question asks for | Rule | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "not A" | [Complement](#rules-for-one-event) | Nothing extra: any event works |
| "A or B" | [Addition](#or-the-addition-rules) | Take the overlap off unless the events are disjoint (can't both happen) |
| "A given B" | [Conditional probability](#given-and-and-conditional-product-and-chain-rules) | Divide by $P(B)$, not by the whole space; needs $P(B) > 0$ |
| "A and B" | [Product (multiplication) rule](#given-and-and-conditional-product-and-chain-rules) | Multiply by $P(A \mid B)$; plain $P(A)\,P(B)$ only for independent events |
| "A, then B, then C" | [Chain rule](#given-and-and-conditional-product-and-chain-rules) | Each factor is given everything before it |
| "B, when it depends on which case you are in" | [Total probability](#split-into-cases-or-turn-the-question-around) | The cases must not overlap and must cover everything |
| "the cause, given the evidence" | [Bayes' theorem](#split-into-cases-or-turn-the-question-around) | Don't swap $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$; include the base rate, $P(A)$ before any evidence |
| "does knowing B change A?" | [Independence test](#independence-and-its-look-alikes) | Disjoint is not independent |

## Rules for one event

The three axioms say every probability is at least 0, the whole space gets 1, and disjoint events add. These rules hold for any event, and they are the first consequences of the axioms.

| Rule | Formula | Needs | In the bag | Explained on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Range | $0 \le P(A) \le 1$ | Any event | $P(\text{red}) = 2/5$ | [the three probability axioms](/probability/probability-axioms/#three-rules-for-any-probability) |
| Certain and impossible | $P(\Omega) = 1$, $P(\emptyset) = 0$ | Always true | $P(\text{red or blue}) = 1$ | [the whole sample space gets 1](/probability/probability-axioms/#the-whole-sample-space-gets-1) |
| Complement | $P(A^\complement) = 1 - P(A)$ | Any event | $P(\text{not red}) = 1 - 2/5 = 3/5$ | [not A is one minus A](/probability/probability-axioms/#not-a-is-one-minus-a) |
| Monotonicity | If $A \subseteq B$, then $P(A) \le P(B)$ | $A$ inside $B$ | $P(\text{red} \cap \text{striped}) = 3/10 \le P(\text{red}) = 2/5$ | [a bigger event is never less likely](/probability/probability-axioms/#a-bigger-event-is-never-less-likely) |

For a discrete sample space, Grinstead and Snell's Theorem 1.1 lists nonnegativity, $P(\Omega) = 1$, monotonicity, additivity for two disjoint events and the complement rule (p. 22) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

## "Or": the addition rules

"A or B" adds the two probabilities, then takes off once what they share.

| Rule | Formula | Needs | In the bag | Explained on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Disjoint events | $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ | $A \cap B = \emptyset$ | $P(\text{red striped or blue striped}) = 3/10 + 2/10 = 1/2$ | [events that can't happen together add up](/probability/probability-axioms/#events-that-cant-happen-together-add-up) |
| Any two events (inclusion–exclusion) | $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$ | Any events | $P(\text{red or striped}) = 2/5 + 1/2 - 3/10 = 3/5$; count: 4 red and 2 blue striped make 6 of 10 | [add, then take the overlap off once](/probability/probability-axioms/#a-or-b-add-then-take-the-overlap-off-once) |

Countably many disjoint events add too, which is the third axiom in full: see [why the third axiom is countable](/probability/probability-axioms/#countable-additivity). Inclusion–exclusion is Grinstead and Snell's Theorem 1.4, equation (1.1) (p. 24) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.

## "Given" and "and": conditional, product and chain rules

"Given B" keeps only the outcomes in B, and "and" multiplies along a chain of such conditions.

| Rule | Formula | Needs | In the bag | Explained on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conditional probability | $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$ | $P(B) > 0$ | $P(\text{striped} \mid \text{red}) = (3/10)/(2/5) = 3/4$ | [divide by B, not by everything](/probability/conditional-probability/#divide-by-b-not-by-everything) |
| The other direction | $P(B \mid A) = P(A \cap B)/P(A)$ | $P(A) > 0$ | $P(\text{red} \mid \text{striped}) = (3/10)/(1/2) = 3/5$, not $3/4$ | [hat given scarf is not scarf given hat](/probability/conditional-probability/#hat-given-scarf-is-not-scarf-given-hat) |
| Complement given $B$ | $P(A^\complement \mid B) = 1 - P(A \mid B)$ | $P(B) > 0$ | $P(\text{plain} \mid \text{red}) = 1 - 3/4 = 1/4$ | [not A, given B](/probability/conditional-probability/#not-a-given-b) |
| Product (multiplication) rule | $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)$ | $P(B) > 0$ | $P(\text{red} \cap \text{striped}) = 3/4 \times 2/5 = 3/10$; two reds without replacement: $4/10 \times 3/9 = 2/15$ | [the multiplication rule](/probability/conditional-probability/#the-multiplication-rule) |
| Chain rule | $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\,P(C \mid A \cap B)$ | $P(A \cap B) > 0$ | Three reds without replacement: $4/10 \times 3/9 \times 2/8 = 1/30$ | [the chain rule derivation](/probability/conditional-probability/#formal-definition) |

A conditional probability obeys every rule on this page, with B as the new whole space: see [why conditioning gives a probability](/probability/conditional-probability/#conditioning-is-a-probability).

## Split into cases, or turn the question around

Total probability adds up an event over cases, and Bayes' theorem divides one case's share by that total. In the table, $H_1, \ldots, H_m$ are the cases.

| Rule | Formula | Needs | In the bag | Explained on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total probability | $P(B) = \sum_{k} P(B \mid H_k)\,P(H_k)$ | The $H_k$ share no outcome and cover $\Omega$; cases with $P(H_k) = 0$ are dropped | Cases red and blue: $P(\text{striped}) = P(\text{striped} \mid \text{red})\,P(\text{red}) + P(\text{striped} \mid \text{blue})\,P(\text{blue}) = 3/4 \times 2/5 + 2/6 \times 3/5 = 3/10 + 1/5 = 1/2$ (2 of the 6 blue marbles are striped). Two bags: $P(\text{red}) = 1/2 \times 4/5 + 1/2 \times 2/5 = 3/5$ | [the law of total probability, worked through](/probability/law-of-total-probability/#weight-chance-multiply-add) |
| Bayes' theorem | $P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)/P(B)$ | $P(A) > 0$, $P(B) > 0$ | $P(\text{red} \mid \text{striped}) = (3/4 \times 2/5)/(1/2) = 3/5$. Two bags: $P(\text{bag 1} \mid \text{red}) = (1/2 \times 4/5)/(3/5) = 2/3$ | [turn the question around](/probability/bayes-theorem/#turn-the-question-around) |
| Odds form | Odds of $A$ are $P(A)/P(A^\complement)$: $\dfrac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A^\complement \mid B)} = \dfrac{P(A)}{P(A^\complement)} \cdot \dfrac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B \mid A^\complement)}$ | $0 < P(A) < 1$, $P(B \mid A^\complement) > 0$ | Prior odds of red against blue: $(2/5)/(3/5) = 2/3$. Likelihood ratio: $P(\text{striped} \mid \text{red})/P(\text{striped} \mid \text{blue}) = (3/4)/(1/3) = 9/4$. Odds given striped: $2/3 \times 9/4 = 3/2$, so $P(\text{red} \mid \text{striped}) = 3/(3 + 2) = 3/5$ | [the odds form](/probability/bayes-theorem/#odds-form) |
| Updating twice | The updated probability after one piece of evidence (the posterior) becomes the starting probability (the prior) for the next | The pieces of evidence are independent given each case (conditional independence, below) | Two bags, two reds with replacement: the first red gives $P(\text{bag 1} \mid \text{red}) = 2/3$, the new prior, then $(2/3 \times 4/5)/(2/3 \times 4/5 + 1/3 \times 2/5) = 4/5$. In one step, with $P(\text{red, red})$ for both draws red: $P(\text{red, red} \mid \text{bag 1}) = (4/5)^2 = 16/25$ and $P(\text{red, red}) = 2/5$, so $(1/2 \times 16/25)/(2/5) = 4/5$ | [updating twice](/probability/bayes-theorem/#updating-twice) |

For cases that share no outcome and cover $\Omega$, Grinstead and Snell's Theorem 1.3 gives $P(B) = \sum_k P(B \cap H_k)$ (p. 23) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. The multiplication rule turns each term into $P(B \mid H_k)\,P(H_k)$. For more on where the sum comes from, see [add up every way the evidence can happen](/probability/bayes-theorem/#add-up-every-way-the-evidence-can-happen). Two pages work these rules through on a single famous problem each: [the base rate fallacy in medical tests](/probability/base-rate-fallacy-medical-test/#count-four-groups-then-read-one-column) and [the Monty Hall problem](/probability/monty-hall-problem/#turn-it-around-with-bayes-theorem).

## Independence and its look-alikes

A and B are independent when knowing one leaves the other's probability unchanged, which the product test checks.

| Rule | Formula | Needs | In the bag | Explained on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Independent events | $A \indep B$ exactly when $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$; the same as $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ when $P(B) > 0$ | Nothing extra; an event of probability 0 is independent of everything | Red and striped are not independent: $3/10 \ne 2/5 \times 1/2 = 1/5$. Two draws with replacement are: $P(\text{red, red}) = 2/5 \times 2/5 = 4/25$ | [two ways to say it](/probability/independence/#two-ways-to-say-it) |
| Mutual independence | For every group of two or more of the events, the chance that all of them happen is the product of their probabilities: for three, $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)\,P(B)\,P(C)$ and every pair multiplies | Checking only the pairs is not enough | Two fair coins: "first heads", "second heads" and "both the same" multiply in every pair ($1/4 = 1/2 \times 1/2$), but all three happen together with probability $1/4$, not $1/8$ | [three or more events](/probability/independence/#three-or-more-events) |
| Mutually exclusive is not independent | $A \cap B = \emptyset$ with $P(A), P(B) > 0$ makes them dependent | Both events possible | Red and blue on one draw: $P(\text{both}) = 0$, but $2/5 \times 3/5 = 6/25$ | [exclusive events with positive probability are dependent](/probability/independence-vs-mutual-exclusivity/#exclusive-events-with-positive-probability-are-always-dependent) |
| Conditional independence | $A \indep B \mid C$ exactly when $P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\,P(B \mid C)$ | $P(C) > 0$ | Given bag 1, $P(\text{red, red}) = 16/25 = (4/5)^2$. Overall $P(\text{red, red}) = 1/2 \times 16/25 + 1/2 \times 4/25 = 2/5$, not $(3/5)^2 = 9/25$ | [check it inside each group](/probability/conditional-independence/#check-it-inside-each-group) |

Grinstead and Snell's Definition 4.1 counts two events as independent when at least one has probability 0, their Theorem 4.1 proves that the product test decides independence, and their Definition 4.2 asks every subset of the events to multiply (pp. 139–141) <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. [Naive Bayes](/probability/naive-bayes-classifier/#from-bayes-theorem-to-one-multiplication-per-feature) rests on the last row: it assumes the features are independent given the class.

## The same rules under machine learning names

A random variable is a quantity whose value is a random outcome, like the color of the drawn marble, and $P(x)$ is the chance that it equals $x$. $P(x, y)$ means $x$ and $y$ together, and marginalizing means adding up over every value of the other variable. Machine learning texts give some of these rules other names, and two of the names clash with this page. The middle column gives the name and equation in Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville's Chapter 3.

| Rule on this page | Name in the textbook | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total probability, for random variables: $P(x) = \sum_{y} P(x, y)$ | "Sum rule", marginalizing (§3.4, eq. 3.3) | [Mixture models](/probability/law-of-total-probability/#mixture-models-a-hidden-case-for-every-data-point) add up over a hidden group |
| Conditional probability | Conditional probability, defined only when the condition has positive probability (§3.5, eq. 3.5) | A [classifier's output](/probability/conditional-probability/#a-classifiers-output) estimates $P(y \mid x)$ |
| Chain rule | "Chain rule, or product rule" (§3.6, eq. 3.6) | A language model scores a text one [next token](/probability/conditional-probability/#the-next-token) at a time |
| Independence and conditional independence | Independence and conditional independence (§3.7, eqs. 3.7–3.8) | [Naive Bayes](/probability/naive-bayes-classifier/) assumes the features are independent given the class |
| Bayes' theorem | "Bayes' rule" (§3.11, eq. 3.42) | A [classifier's output as a posterior](/probability/bayes-theorem/#a-classifiers-output-is-a-posterior-estimate) |

The book names marginalizing the sum rule, $P(x) = \sum_y P(x, y)$, in §3.4 <Cite id="goodfellow2016prob" />. It defines conditional probability only when the condition has positive probability, and calls splitting the chance of several values together into a product of one-at-a-time conditional chances "the chain rule, or product rule" (§§3.5–3.6) <Cite id="goodfellow2016prob" />. It writes independence with a single up tack, $\perp$, and it computes the bottom of Bayes' rule as $P(y) = \sum_x P(y \mid x)\,P(x)$ (§§3.7 and 3.11) <Cite id="goodfellow2016prob" />. This site writes independence with a double up tack, $\indep$, and keeps the single up tack for perpendicular vectors.

Two warnings follow. The "sum rule" means marginalizing, not the addition rule for "or". And "product rule" can mean the two-event multiplication rule or the whole chain rule, depending on the book.

## Check yourself

The questions mix the rules on purpose, because choosing the rule is the hard part. Name the rule first, then calculate.

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="One draw from the bag. What is P(blue or plain)?">
$7/10$, by inclusion–exclusion: $6/10 + 5/10 - 4/10 = 7/10$. Adding $6/10 + 5/10 = 11/10$ counts the 4 blue plain marbles twice, and a probability can't be more than 1. Counting agrees: 6 blue marbles and 1 red plain one make 7 of 10. See [add, then take the overlap off once](/probability/probability-axioms/#a-or-b-add-then-take-the-overlap-off-once).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="On one draw, are the events 'striped' and 'plain' independent?">
No. They are mutually exclusive: $P(\text{striped} \cap \text{plain}) = 0$, but $P(\text{striped})\,P(\text{plain}) = 1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4$. Knowing the marble is striped makes plain impossible, the opposite of "tells you nothing". See [independent vs mutually exclusive events](/probability/independence-vs-mutual-exclusivity/).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A class of 12 children: 5 wear hats, 4 wear scarves, and 2 wear both. Pick one child at random. What is P(hat | no scarf)?">
$3/8$. Eight children have no scarf, and 3 of them wear hats. Divide by the 8 without a scarf, not by the 12 in the class and not by the 5 with hats. See [the class on the conditional probability page](/probability/conditional-probability/#divide-by-b-not-by-everything).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Two marbles drawn from the bag without replacement. What is P(both blue)?">
$1/3$, by the product rule with a conditional: $P(\text{first blue}) = 6/10$, and given that, 5 of the 9 marbles left are blue, so $6/10 \times 5/9 = 1/3$. The plain product $(3/5)^2 = 9/25$ is right only with replacement, where the draws are independent. See [the multiplication rule](/probability/conditional-probability/#the-multiplication-rule).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="A fair coin picks bag 1 (8 red of 10) or bag 2 (4 red of 10), and you draw one marble. What is P(blue)?">
$2/5$, by total probability: bag 1 has 2 blue of 10 and bag 2 has 6, so $1/2 \times 1/5 + 1/2 \times 3/5 = 1/10 + 3/10 = 2/5$. Averaging the two bags' chances works here only because the coin is fair; with an unfair coin the weights change. See [the law of total probability](/probability/law-of-total-probability/).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="1 person in 100 has a condition, and a test catches 9 in 10 people who have it and clears 9 in 10 who don't. What does a positive mean?">
$P(\text{has it} \mid \text{positive}) = 1/12 \approx 0.083$, by Bayes' theorem with total probability in the bottom. $P(\text{positive and has it}) = 0.01 \times 0.9 = 0.009$ and $P(\text{positive}) = 0.009 + 0.99 \times 0.1 = 0.108$, so the answer is $0.009/0.108 = 1/12$. The $9/10$ is $P(\text{positive} \mid \text{has it})$, the other direction. See [the base rate decides a lot](/probability/bayes-theorem/#the-base-rate-decides-a-lot).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="If A and B are independent, show that A and the complement of B are independent too.">
$A$ splits into the disjoint pieces $A \cap B$ and $A \cap B^\complement$, so additivity gives $P(A \cap B^\complement) = P(A) - P(A \cap B)$. Independence turns this into $P(A) - P(A)\,P(B) = P(A)\,(1 - P(B))$, and the complement rule makes it $P(A)\,P(B^\complement)$. The rules used are additivity, the product test and the complement rule.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Same two bags, and the marble you drew is red. What is P(bag 2 | red)?">
$1/3$, by Bayes' theorem: $(1/2 \times 2/5)/(3/5) = (1/5)/(3/5) = 1/3$. It is not $P(\text{red} \mid \text{bag 2}) = 2/5$, which turns the question the other way. See [turn the question around](/probability/bayes-theorem/#turn-the-question-around).
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="Draw twice, with replacement, from the bag the coin picked. Show, using two blues, that the two draws are independent given the bag but not overall.">
Given bag 1, $P(\text{blue, blue}) = (1/5)^2 = 1/25$, and given bag 2, $(3/5)^2 = 9/25$, so the draws multiply inside each bag. Overall, total probability gives $P(\text{blue, blue}) = 1/2 \times 1/25 + 1/2 \times 9/25 = 1/5$, but $P(\text{blue})^2 = (2/5)^2 = 4/25$. Equivalently, $P(\text{second blue} \mid \text{first blue}) = (1/5)/(2/5) = 1/2$, more than $2/5$: a first blue is evidence for bag 2. Independence given the bag does not give independence. See [conditional independence](/probability/conditional-independence/#why-a-common-cause-links-them-overall).
</CheckYourself>
