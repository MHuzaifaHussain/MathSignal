## In everyday terms

<Analogy breaks="Cards in a pile look equally likely, but outcomes don't have to be: a loaded die still has six outcomes, they just aren't equally likely, and then counting cards gives the wrong answer. And for a measurement such as a height there are far too many outcomes to write on cards, which is where the precise statement further down takes over.">

Before you toss a coin, take a pile of blank cards and write one possible result on each: one card says "heads", one says "tails". The pile is the sample space: everything that could happen. When you toss, exactly one card comes true. Each card is an outcome.

Now ask a question about the toss, such as "did it land heads?". The answer picks out some of the cards, here just one. That group of cards is an event. A question like "did it land heads or tails?" picks out the whole pile, and "did it land on its edge?", if you left that card out, picks out none.

Toss two coins, a penny and a dime, and the pile needs more care. "Penny heads, dime tails" and "penny tails, dime heads" are different cards, because you can tell the coins apart. Write them as one card and the pile no longer matches the world: "one of each" can happen in two ways, while "both heads" can happen in only one. A good pile has one card for each distinct way the experiment can turn out.

</Analogy>

<WhyThisMatters>
Every probability a model reports is a probability of some event in some sample space. A classifier spreads its probability over a fixed set of labels, and a language model spreads it over its whole vocabulary of tokens. Test accuracy is how often the event "the prediction is right" happens. So when a model reports 0.9, ask which sample space and which event the 0.9 belongs to. Get those wrong, for example by merging outcomes that should stay separate, and the resulting numbers will look precise while being wrong.
</WhyThisMatters>

## What is a sample space?

A sample space is the list of every possible outcome of an experiment, written so that exactly one outcome happens each time it runs. For one coin it is heads and tails. For one die it is the six faces. For two coins it is the four ordered results HH, HT, TH and TT. For a measurement such as a height it is a whole range of numbers, which is where densities like the [normal distribution](/statistics/normal-distribution/) come in.

Two rules make a list a sample space. The outcomes must not overlap, so no single toss can count as two of them, and they must cover everything, so every toss counts as one of them. "HH" and "at least one head" can't both be outcomes of the same list, because one includes the other.

There is no single correct sample space for an experiment <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. For two coins you could also record only the number of heads, 0, 1 or 2. That is a legitimate sample space, but its three outcomes are not equally likely, so you can't find probabilities in it by counting.

<SideNote>
This mistake has a long history. Grinstead and Snell note that d'Alembert, in the eighteenth century, gave 0, 1 and 2 heads in two tosses equal probabilities, while Cardano, two centuries earlier, had already counted two dice as 36 ordered pairs <Cite id="grinstead2003" />.
</SideNote>

### Outcomes and events

An outcome is one result; an event is a group of outcomes, chosen because it answers a question. "At least one head" is the event made of HH, HT and TH. An event happens when the outcome that actually turns up is one of its members.

The smallest event is the empty event, which holds no outcomes and never happens. The smallest nonempty events are the single outcomes on their own. The largest is the whole sample space, which always happens. With four outcomes there are 16 possible events, from the empty one to all four.

<Simpler label="the same idea with one die">

Roll one die. The sample space is the six faces, 1 to 6. "The die shows 4" is an event with one outcome in it. "The die shows an even number" is an event with three: 2, 4 and 6. "The die shows 7" is an event too, the empty one, because no face shows 7. Each event is a question you could ask about the roll, written as the faces that answer yes.

</Simpler>

### Combining events: not, and, or

Events combine the way questions do, and each combination is again an event. "Not A" holds the outcomes outside A. "A and B" holds the outcomes in both. "A or B" holds the outcomes in either one, or in both. For two coins, the opposite of "at least one head" is "no heads", the single outcome TT. The rules that probabilities of these combined events must follow are the subject of the next page, [probability axioms](/probability/probability-axioms/).

### Counting works only when outcomes are equally likely

When every outcome in the sample space is equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of its outcomes divided by the number of outcomes in the sample space. That is how 3 of 4 became 3/4 above. The condition matters. Drawing outcomes as squares or cards of the same size doesn't make them equally likely; whether they are depends on the experiment, such as a fair coin or a fair die.

Is a sum of 7 as likely as a sum of 2, when you roll two dice? Guess first. The board below lists all 36 ordered results, first die down the side and second die across the top. Tap squares to build event A, or press the presets "Sum is 7" and "Sum is 2", and read the count under the table.

</div>

No. A sum of 7 happens in 6 of the 36 outcomes, (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2) and (6,1), so its probability is 6/36 = 1/6. A sum of 2 happens only as (1,1): 1/36. Each outcome is equally likely with fair dice, but the sums are not, because they hold different numbers of outcomes.

<GoDeeper label="when the outcomes are not equally likely">

A loaded die still has the sample space 1 to 6, but now each face gets its own weight, a number from 0 to 1, and the six weights add up to 1. The probability of an event is the sum of the weights of its outcomes. Counting is the special case where every weight is the same, 1/6 here. The general rules for weights are the probability axioms on the next page.

</GoDeeper>

## Notation

<NotationKey symbols={["sample-space", "outcome", "event", "complement", "intersection", "union", "empty-set", "probability", "cardinality", "event-collection"]} />

<PreciseStatement>

Let $\Omega$ be a finite, nonempty set, the sample space; each $\omega \in \Omega$ is an outcome, and exactly one outcome occurs. On a finite sample space we can, and usually do, take every subset $A \subseteq \Omega$ as an event, so the collection of events is $\mathcal{F} = 2^\Omega$, all subsets of $\Omega$. This is a convention: a smaller collection also works if it contains $\Omega$ and is closed under the operations below. Events combine by $A^\complement = \Omega \setminus A$, $A \cap B$ and $A \cup B$, and each result is again an event.

When every outcome is equally likely, and only then, the probability of every event $A$ is

$$
P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}.
$$

Boundary cases: the empty set $\emptyset$ and the whole space $\Omega$ are both events, with $P(\emptyset) = 0$ and $P(\Omega) = 1$. An empty sample space is excluded, since some outcome must occur and $|\Omega|$ would be 0. On finite and countable sample spaces every subset can be an event <Cite id="grinstead2003" />. On an uncountable sample space, such as $[0, 1)$, $\mathcal{F}$ cannot always be all subsets; see the fold below.

</PreciseStatement>

<GoDeeper label="why uncountable sample spaces need a collection of events" id="non-measurable-sets">

Pick a point uniformly at random from $[0, 1)$, and shift sets by wrapping around: shifting $x$ by $t$ gives $x + t$, minus 1 if that reaches 1. We would like a probability on every subset of $[0, 1)$ that gives each interval its length, adds up over countably many disjoint pieces, and doesn't change when a set is shifted. No such probability exists. Using the axiom of choice, one can build a set $V$ whose shifts by the rational numbers in $[0, 1)$ are disjoint and together cover $[0, 1)$, and there are only countably many of those shifts. Every shift would get the same probability as $V$, so countable additivity would make the total 0 if that probability is 0, or infinite if it is positive, and never 1. Tao gives the same argument with ordinary shifts on the line <Cite id="tao2011" />.

So on spaces like $[0, 1)$, probabilities are assigned only to the sets in a chosen collection $\mathcal{F}$, which contains $\Omega$ and is closed under complements and countable unions. This is the shape of Kolmogorov's axiomatic framework: outcomes, a collection of events, and a probability on those events <Cite id="kolmogorov1950" />. On a finite or countable space none of this bites, and we can take $\mathcal{F}$ to be every subset.

</GoDeeper>

<FormalDefinition>

**Product spaces.** Two experiments run one after the other have the sample space $\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2$, the set of ordered pairs $(\omega_1, \omega_2)$. Two coins give $\{H, T\} \times \{H, T\} = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$.

<StepThrough title="Why two coins have four outcomes: |Ω₁ × Ω₂| = |Ω₁| |Ω₂|" rigor="full">

1. Split $\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2$ by the first coordinate: for each $\omega_1 \in \Omega_1$, the pairs that start with it are $\{\omega_1\} \times \Omega_2$.
2. Each of these pieces has exactly $|\Omega_2|$ members, one for each choice of second coordinate.
3. The pieces don't overlap, because a pair has only one first coordinate, and together they cover every pair.
4. So $|\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2| = \sum_{\omega_1 \in \Omega_1} |\Omega_2| = |\Omega_1|\,|\Omega_2|$. For two coins, $2 \times 2 = 4$; for two dice, $6 \times 6 = 36$.

</StepThrough>

The count takes ordered pairs as given. Choosing them is a modeling decision, and it fits two coins because $(H, T)$ and $(T, H)$ describe different tosses: which coin showed heads differs. Unordered, the two would merge into one item that can happen in two ways, and the three items would no longer be equally likely.

<StepThrough title="A sample space with n outcomes has 2ⁿ subsets, so 2ⁿ possible events" rigor="full">

1. A subset is fixed by answering, for each outcome, one yes-or-no question: is it in the subset?
2. With $|\Omega| = n$ outcomes there are $n$ separate yes-or-no choices, so by the product rule above, applied $n - 1$ times (induction on $n$), there are $2 \times 2 \times \dots \times 2 = 2^n$ ways to answer them.
3. Different answers give different subsets, and every subset arises this way, so there are exactly $2^n$ subsets. The all-no answer is $\emptyset$ and the all-yes answer is $\Omega$, so both are counted. Taking every subset as an event, as we do on a finite space, gives $2^n$ possible events.

</StepThrough>

**The complement rule for counts.** Every outcome is in $A$ or in $A^\complement$, never both, so $|A^\complement| = |\Omega| - |A|$. With equally likely outcomes this gives $P(A^\complement) = 1 - P(A)$.

| Experiment | Outcomes $\lvert\Omega\rvert$ | Subsets $2^{\lvert\Omega\rvert}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One coin | 2 | 4 |
| Two coins | 4 | 16 |
| One die | 6 | 64 |
| Two dice | 36 | $2^{36} = 68{,}719{,}476{,}736$ |
| $n$ coins | $2^n$ | $2^{2^n}$ |

</FormalDefinition>

## Worked examples

<WorkedExample number={1} title="An even number on one die">

$\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ and the event is $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$. A fair die makes the six faces equally likely, so $P(A) = |A|/|\Omega| = 3/6 = 1/2$.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={2} title="The first die beats the second">

Roll two dice and let $A$ be the event that the first die shows more than the second. The 36 ordered pairs split into 6 doubles and 30 pairs with different faces. By symmetry, half of those 30 have the first die larger, so $|A| = 15$ and $P(A) = 15/36 = 5/12$. Note that this needs ordered pairs: $(5, 2)$ is in $A$ and $(2, 5)$ is not.

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample number={3} title="Three coins, at least one head" faded>

Toss three fair coins. How many outcomes are there, and what is the probability of at least one head? Hint: count the complement.

<div slot="solution">

$|\Omega| = 2^3 = 8$ ordered results, from HHH to TTT. The complement of "at least one head" is "no heads", which is only $\{TTT\}$, so $|A| = 8 - 1 = 7$ and $P(A) = 7/8$. Listing the seven outcomes directly gives the same answer, but the complement needs one outcome instead of seven.

</div>

</WorkedExample>

## Outcomes and events in machine learning

### A classifier's labels

A classifier that sorts images into cat, dog and bird works on the sample space $\Omega = \{\text{cat}, \text{dog}, \text{bird}\}$. Its softmax output gives a probability to each outcome <Cite id="goodfellow2016" />, for example $0.7$, $0.2$ and $0.1$. These are not equally likely, so counting doesn't apply: the probability of an event is the sum of its outcomes' probabilities. The event "a four-legged animal" is $\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}\}$, with probability $0.7 + 0.2 = 0.9$. Choosing the label set is choosing the sample space; an image of a horse has no outcome to land on, and the model must still spread all of its probability over the three labels.

### Next-token prediction

A language model predicts the next token from a vocabulary $V$, which is its sample space: one outcome per token, often tens of thousands of them. Given the text so far, its output $P$ is the model's distribution for the next token, a probability for every $\omega \in V$. Sampling methods work with events. Top-$k$ sampling keeps the event $V_k$ made of the $k$ most likely tokens and samples from it. If several tokens tie at rank $k$, a rule has to decide which of them to keep, and implementations break the tie one way or another. Each kept token $\omega \in V_k$ gets the new probability

$$
\frac{P(\{\omega\})}{P(V_k)}.
$$

In words: each kept token's probability is divided by the total probability of the kept tokens, so they add up to 1 again, and every token outside $V_k$ gets probability 0. This renormalizing is conditioning on the event $V_k$, which conditional probability, a later topic, makes precise.

### Test-set accuracy

Accuracy is the share of test examples on which the event "prediction equals label" happened. If a model gets 912 of 1,000 test examples right, its accuracy is $912/1000 = 0.912$. Like the Toss 100 tallies on the board, this is a frequency from a sample, an estimate of the probability of that event on new data. It is a good estimate only when the test set is drawn the same way as the data the model will meet.

## Count outcomes in code

This runs in your browser, builds the sample spaces from this page with `itertools.product`, and stops with an error if a result is off:

<PyRunner
  title="sample_space.py"
  code={`
import random
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product


def probability(event, space):
    """P(A) = |A| / |Omega|, valid only when every outcome is equally likely."""
    return Fraction(len(event), len(space))


# Two coins: ordered pairs, so HT and TH are different outcomes.
coins = ["".join(pair) for pair in product("HT", repeat=2)]
at_least_one_head = [outcome for outcome in coins if "H" in outcome]
print("two coins:", coins)
assert coins == ["HH", "HT", "TH", "TT"]
assert probability(at_least_one_head, coins) == Fraction(3, 4)

# The wrong model merges HT and TH into one tile and treats the 3 tiles as equally likely.
tiles = ["HH", "one of each", "TT"]
wrong_answer = probability([tile for tile in tiles if tile != "TT"], tiles)
print("wrong model says", wrong_answer, "but the truth is", probability(at_least_one_head, coins))
assert wrong_answer == Fraction(2, 3)
assert wrong_answer != Fraction(3, 4)

# Two dice: 36 ordered pairs.
dice = list(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
sum_is_7 = [roll for roll in dice if sum(roll) == 7]
sum_is_2 = [roll for roll in dice if sum(roll) == 2]
first_beats_second = [roll for roll in dice if roll[0] > roll[1]]
assert len(dice) == 36
assert probability(sum_is_7, dice) == Fraction(6, 36) == Fraction(1, 6)
assert probability(sum_is_2, dice) == Fraction(1, 36)
assert probability(first_beats_second, dice) == Fraction(15, 36) == Fraction(5, 12)
print("sum is 7:", probability(sum_is_7, dice), " sum is 2:", probability(sum_is_2, dice))

# One die and three coins (worked examples 1 and 3).
die = list(range(1, 7))
assert probability([face for face in die if face % 2 == 0], die) == Fraction(1, 2)
three_coins = list(product("HT", repeat=3))
no_heads = [outcome for outcome in three_coins if "H" not in outcome]
assert len(three_coins) == 8 and len(no_heads) == 1
assert 1 - probability(no_heads, three_coins) == Fraction(7, 8)

# A space with n outcomes has 2**n subsets (the properties table).
assert sum(1 for _ in product([False, True], repeat=len(coins))) == 16
assert [2**n for n in (2, 4, 6, 36)] == [4, 16, 64, 68_719_476_736]

# Simulate: the share of tosses with at least one head settles near 3/4, not 2/3.
rng = random.Random(0)
tosses = 100_000
results = [rng.choice("HT") + rng.choice("HT") for _ in range(tosses)]
share_head = sum("H" in result for result in results) / tosses
share_mixed = sum(result in ("HT", "TH") for result in results) / tosses
print(f"at least one head in {share_head:.3f} of {tosses} tosses; one of each in {share_mixed:.3f}")
assert abs(share_head - 0.75) < 0.01
assert abs(share_mixed - 0.5) < 0.01
assert abs(share_head - 2 / 3) > 0.05
`}
/>

The simulation's share is within 0.01 of 0.75 and the share of "one of each" is within 0.01 of 0.5, so the tosses side with the model of four equally likely outcomes.

## Research frontier

<ResearchFrontier reviewed="2026-11-24">

**Why probability is defined on a collection of events.** Kolmogorov built probability on a set of outcomes, a collection of events and a probability on those events <Cite id="kolmogorov1950" />, and this framework is the standard one. The middle piece is there because of [non-measurable sets](#non-measurable-sets), a settled result.

**Nothing open here.** For finite sample spaces we can take every subset as an event and nothing goes wrong; the subtleties begin only with uncountable spaces, such as a point picked from $[0, 1]$, and Kolmogorov's framework above is the standard way to handle them.

</ResearchFrontier>

## Check yourself

<CheckYourself level="intuition" question="You toss a coin and roll a die together. How many outcomes does the sample space have?">
12. Each of the 2 coin faces pairs with each of the 6 die faces, and every pair is a different way the two can land, so there are $2 \times 6 = 12$ ordered outcomes.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Someone lists the results of rolling two dice as 21 unordered pairs, such as {1, 2} and {1, 1}, and counts. Why do they get wrong probabilities?">
The 21 unordered pairs are not equally likely. The pair $\{1, 2\}$ covers two ordered outcomes, $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$, while $\{1, 1\}$ covers only one. Counting treats them all as the same size, so it gives doubles too much probability: $1/21$ each instead of $1/36$.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="standard" question="Is sum is 7 an outcome or an event when you roll two dice?">
An event. It is the set of 6 outcomes $\{(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)\}$. An outcome is one ordered pair, such as $(3, 4)$, and it is also the one-outcome event $\{(3, 4)\}$ when you treat it as a question.
</CheckYourself>

<CheckYourself level="proof" question="On a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes, show that P(A ∪ B) ≤ P(A) + P(B) for any events A and B.">
Every outcome in $A \cup B$ is in $A$ or in $B$, so it is counted at least once in $|A| + |B|$; outcomes in both are counted twice. So $|A \cup B| \le |A| + |B|$. Dividing by $|\Omega| > 0$ gives $P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B)$, with equality exactly when $A \cap B = \emptyset$.
</CheckYourself>

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistakes>
  <Mistake wrong="Two coins can land in three ways: two heads, two tails, or one of each, so at least one head has probability 2/3.">
    There are four equally likely outcomes, HH, HT, TH and TT, because the first coin showing heads and the second showing heads are different results. At least one head is $3/4$. The three-way list is a valid sample space, but its outcomes are not equally likely: one of each has probability $1/2$. Try the switch and Toss 100 on the board above.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="An outcome and an event are the same thing.">
    An outcome is one result, such as HT. An event is a set of outcomes, such as $\{HH, HT, TH\}$, and can hold none, one or many. A single outcome becomes the event $\{HT\}$ only when you ask about it.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="Equally sized squares mean equally likely outcomes.">
    The board draws every outcome the same size, but that is a drawing choice and says nothing about the experiment. $P(A) = |A|/|\Omega|$ holds only when the outcomes really are equally likely, as with fair coins and dice. For a loaded die or a classifier's labels, add up the outcomes' probabilities instead.
  </Mistake>
  <Mistake wrong="The empty set is not a real event, because nothing is in it.">
    $\emptyset$ is a well-defined event: the event that can't happen, such as a die showing 7. It always has probability 0. It is needed so that events stay closed under not, and, or: two events with no outcome in common have $A \cap B = \emptyset$.
  </Mistake>
</CommonMistakes>
